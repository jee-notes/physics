About These Notes

Students preparing for JEE often ask a practical question: "Is NCERT sufficient for JEE Advanced?". But a more fundamental question is rarely asked: What is the difference between knowledge and understanding?

Knowledge is primarily acquired through the senses. You read a textbook, listen to a lecture, or watch a solution being explained. Information enters the mind through sight and sound. In this sense, knowledge acquisition is largely an input process.

Understanding, however, is something different. It is not simply received; it must be constructed internally. It requires deliberate mental work: examining ideas, relating them to each other, testing them against examples, and organizing them into a coherent structure.

Because reading and thinking often occur together, people frequently assume that knowledge automatically produces understanding. But anyone who reflects carefully begins to notice that the two processes are distinct. One can possess many facts without truly seeing how they fit together.

To understand something means to study it structurally. Consider a simple object placed in front of you — a table. You begin by observing and describing it. It has a horizontal surface, it stands on legs, it is made of a particular material, and it has a specific color and shape. These are its properties.

Next, you examine your relation to the object. You use the table to place books, write notes, or hold a computer. Other entities may interact with it differently. These interactions describe the relations the object has with other systems.

Now consider change. Suppose the table changes color from white to grey to black and then back again. Each configuration of properties represents a particular state. When these properties evolve with time, we study the dynamics of the system. A transition between states is what we call an event.

To study an object properly therefore means organizing four things: its properties, its relations, its possible states, and the events that cause transitions between those states. This act of internal organization is what we call understanding.

Physics attempts to perform this organization for the natural world. Physical quantities describe properties. Interactions describe relations between systems. The configuration of a system defines its state. Laws of physics describe how states evolve and how events occur.

Textbooks such as NCERT provide valuable knowledge. They present definitions, laws, and examples. But no book can supply understanding by itself. Understanding must ultimately be built inside the mind of the learner.

These notes are therefore not presented as the final or authoritative way to understand physics. They simply represent one attempt to organize the subject — focusing on meaning, structure, and the reasoning that connects physical ideas with mathematics.

Some explanations here may be useful, others may be incomplete, and a few may even be incorrect. That is acceptable. The goal is not authority, but clarity. The purpose of these notes is to assist the reader in constructing their own understanding of physics.
