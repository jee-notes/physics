Melting Point

Melting Point as a thermal property.

Melting Point as a Competition

Particles in a solid are held together by attractive forces. These may be hydrogen bonds in ice, metallic bonds in iron, ionic bonds in sodium chloride, etc.

Temperature represents the level of thermal agitation within the substance. As temperature increases, particles vibrate more vigorously about their mean positions.

Key Idea: Melting is a competition between the forces holding the solid together and the thermal agitation trying to disrupt the ordered structure.

At low temperatures, the attractive forces dominate and the solid remains stable.

As temperature rises, thermal agitation becomes stronger. Eventually, a temperature is reached where the ordered solid structure is no longer strongly preferred over the liquid state.

This temperature is called the melting point.

Why Do Different Substances Have Different Melting Points?

At the same temperature, all substances experience a similar scale of thermal agitation.

However, the forces holding their particles together can be very different.

  • Ice is held together primarily by hydrogen bonds.
  • Iron is held together by strong metallic bonds.
  • Sodium chloride is held together by ionic bonds.

Since these interactions have different strengths, different amounts of thermal agitation are required to disrupt the solid structure.

Melting Point: Different substances have different melting points because the stability of their solid structures is different.

For example, ice melts at \(0^\circ\text{C}\), while iron melts at about \(1538^\circ\text{C}\). The thermal agitation needed to destabilize the iron lattice is much greater than that needed to disrupt the hydrogen-bond network of ice.

Why Does the Entire Solid Melt?

At atmospheric pressure, ice melts at \(273\,\text{K}\). Whether we have \(1\,\text{g}\) of ice or \(1\,\text{kg}\) of ice, the melting temperature remains the same.

The only difference is the amount of heat required. A larger mass requires proportionally more heat to completely melt.

Temperature is related to the thermal agitation of molecules. At \(273\,\text{K}\), each water molecule possesses a characteristic amount of thermal energy.

At this temperature, the thermal agitation becomes sufficient to compete with the hydrogen-bond network that maintains the ice lattice. As a result, the solid structure is no longer strongly preferred over the liquid state.

Key Idea: Melting point is independent of mass because it is determined by the balance between thermal agitation and intermolecular forces at the molecular level.

Since every part of the ice lattice is made of the same water molecules interacting through the same hydrogen bonds, this balance occurs throughout the solid at the same temperature.

Therefore, both \(1\,\text{g}\) and \(1\,\text{kg}\) of ice melt at \(273\,\text{K}\). The larger sample simply requires more heat to convert all of its molecules from the solid phase to the liquid phase.

Why Doesn't 1 kg of Ice Need a Higher Melting Point?

A larger block of ice contains more molecules and therefore requires more heat to melt completely.

However, melting point is not determined by the total number of molecules. It is determined by the behaviour of each individual molecule within the crystal lattice.

At \(273\,\text{K}\), every water molecule possesses a characteristic level of thermal agitation. This agitation becomes sufficient to compete with the hydrogen bonds holding the ice lattice together.

Since the bonding environment of a water molecule is essentially the same throughout the crystal, this condition is reached everywhere at the same temperature.

Key Idea: Mass determines how much heat is required for melting. It does not determine the temperature at which melting begins.

Therefore, both \(1\,\text{g}\) and \(1\,\text{kg}\) of ice melt at \(273\,\text{K}\). The larger sample simply requires more energy because it contains more molecules that must undergo the phase change.
