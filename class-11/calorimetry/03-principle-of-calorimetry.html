Principle of Calorimetry

Applying the law of conservation of energy to thermal interactions.

Conservation of Energy

The principle of calorimetry is fundamentally a restatement of the First Law of Thermodynamics, specifically tailored for macroscopic heat exchange between multiple bodies.

When two or more distinct systems at different temperatures are placed in thermal contact, they will exchange heat. Energy from the hotter bodies transfers to the cooler bodies until all the systems arrive at a single, common equilibrium temperature.

If the system is perfectly isolated — meaning no heat flows into the system from the surrounding environment, and no heat escapes the system — the total internal energy of the system remains constant.

Heat Lost vs Heat Gained

The Principle of Calorimetry: In an isolated thermal system, the heat lost by the hotter bodies is exactly equal to the heat gained by the colder bodies.

Mathematically, this balancing act can be expressed as:

\[ \sum Q_{\text{lost}} = \sum Q_{\text{gained}} \]

Here, \( Q_{\text{lost}} \) represents the energy relinquished by the hot components as they cool down, and \( Q_{\text{gained}} \) represents the energy absorbed by the cold components as they warm up.

This equality is strictly valid only when thermal isolation is guaranteed. In real-world calorimetry experiments, significant systematic errors arise when heat inevitably leaks out into the room or into the walls of the container holding the mixture.

Calculations in Isolated Systems

Because we use the specific heat formula \(Q = mc\Delta T\) to compute the heat, we can set up an algebraic equation to solve for unknown variables like the final mixing temperature \(T_f\).

If a hot mass \(m_1\) with specific heat \(c_1\) at initial temperature \(T_1\) is mixed with a cold mass \(m_2\) with specific heat \(c_2\) at initial temperature \(T_2\), they will reach an equilibrium temperature \(T_f\).

The fundamental equality states:

\[ m_1 c_1 (T_1 - T_f) = m_2 c_2 (T_f - T_2) \]
  • Notice the structure of the temperature differences. In the "heat lost" expression on the left, we use \((T_1 - T_f)\) to assure the value is intrinsically positive.
  • In the "heat gained" expression on the right, we use \((T_f - T_2)\) to similarly guarantee a positive value.

Solving this linear equation for \( T_f \) gives the weighted average of the temperatures based on their individual thermal capacities:

\[ T_f = \frac{m_1 c_1 T_1 + m_2 c_2 T_2}{m_1 c_1 + m_2 c_2} \]
