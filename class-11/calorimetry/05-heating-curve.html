Heating Curve

A visual representation of thermal transitions as energy is supplied uniformly.

Understanding the Heating Curve

When a pure substance (like perfectly solid ice at \(-20^\circ\text{C}\)) is placed over an external, constant heat source, it will continuously absorb energy over time. By diligently tracking the temperature of the substance as a function of the total heat supplied (or simply against chronological time if power is perfectly constant), we produce a Heating Curve.

The graphical structure of a heating curve is fundamentally composed of two distinct geometric features: linearly sloping ramps and perfectly horizontal plateaus. Each tells a specific physical story.

Analyzing Sloping Regions (Temperature Change)

When an object absorbs heat without undergoing a phase change, its temperature rises linearly according to \( Q = mc\Delta T \).

If we model the \(y\)-axis as Temperature (\(T\)) and the \(x\)-axis as the total Heat added (\(Q\)), we can rewrite the formula in a standard slope-intercept arrangement \( y = mx + b \):

\[ \Delta T = \frac{1}{mc} \cdot Q \]

The slope (\(m\)) of the \(T\text{ vs }Q\) graph is therefore strictly inversely proportional to the mass product with the specific heat capacity (\(mc\)).

A steeper slope implies a smaller specific heat. The substance heats up extremely quickly for a tiny addition of heat. A shallower slope implies a larger specific heat, representing higher thermal inertia.

For example, liquid water handles immense thermal loading gracefully — it would exhibit a significantly shallower slope compared to a heating curve mapped out for an equivalent chunk of solid ice.

Analyzing Flat Regions (Phase Change)

Eventually, the steeply climbing solid temperature strikes a rigid physical boundary — its melting point \(T_m\). Here, \( \Delta T = 0 \) while \(Q\) continues to stubbornly increase.

The slope flattens to absolute zero because kinetic energy is stationary. All incoming thermal energy is instantly diverted to tearing apart intermolecular potential bonds.

The horizontal length of this plateau is the total heat added to finish the exact phase transition, governed exclusively by the Latent Heat formulation \( Q = mL \).

Therefore, a wider plateau represents a substantially larger Latent Heat constant for that specific phase change. This is directly observable in the heating curve for water — the vaporization plateau (boiling) extends along the x-axis almost seven times wider than the fusion plateau (melting) because \(L_v \gg L_f\).
