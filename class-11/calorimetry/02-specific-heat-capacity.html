Heat Capacity

Quantifying a material's thermal inertia.

Concept

We already know what temperature is. It is a mathematical expression of the degree of hotness or coolness. In other words, it indicates the thermal state of a system or a body.

We now need to quantify how the thermal state changes when energy is transferred to or from a system. This is important in engineering and technology of thermal machines.

A common thermal machine is an air conditioner.
Suppose we have a \(15\,\text{m}\times15\,\text{m}\times5\,\text{m}\) room, so its volume is \(1125\,\text{m}^3\).
If the outside temperature is \(35^\circ\text{C}\) and we want to maintain the room at \(25^\circ\text{C}\), how much energy must be removed from the room, and at what rate?
Such questions require us to understand the quantitative relationship between energy transfer and temperature.

NCERT mentions a simple experiment to investigate this relationship. A given quantity of water is heated using the same source of heat, and a stopwatch is used to measure the heating time.

Since the same heating arrangement is used, the heating time provides a way to compare the relative amount of heat supplied. The experiment shows that:

  • For the same mass of the same substance, doubling the temperature rise requires approximately twice the heat.
  • For the same substance and the same temperature rise, doubling the mass requires approximately twice the heat.
  • For the same mass and the same temperature rise, different substances require different amounts of heat.

Thus, the quantity of heat required depends upon:

  • The mass of the substance, \(m\)
  • The change in temperature, \(\Delta T\)
  • The nature of the substance

These observations motivate the need for a physical quantity that quantitatively connects heat transfer with the resulting change in temperature.

Heat Capacity

We now have two measurable aspects of a thermal process: heat transfer and change in temperature.

The important question is whether these two can be quantitatively related for a given system (or body).

Suppose we know that a certain quantity of heat has been transferred to or from a particular body. We can then observe the resulting change in its temperature, \(\Delta T\).

Conversely, if we know how the temperature of the body has changed, we should be able to determine the corresponding heat transfer.

Thus, for a given body, we want a physical quantity that establishes a quantitative relation between the heat transacted with the body and its change in temperature.

\[ \boxed{\text{Heat transacted} \quad \longleftrightarrow \quad \text{Change in temperature}} \]

This physical quantity is called the heat capacity of the body.

If a small quantity of heat \(dQ\) is transferred to or from the body and produces a small change in its temperature \(dT\), then:

\[ \boxed{S=\frac{dQ}{dT}} \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{dQ=S\,dT} \]

The significance of heat capacity is that once the heat capacity of a body is known, its temperature change provides a quantitative measure of the heat transferred to or from that body.

Heat capacity establishes the quantitative connection between heat transfer and temperature change of a particular body.
If we know which body has transacted heat and we know its heat capacity, its \(\Delta T\) tells us the corresponding heat transfer.

Heat capacity therefore characterizes the entire body. Two bodies made of the same material may have different heat capacities because their masses may be different.

This leads naturally to the definition of specific heat capacity, which normalizes heat capacity with respect to mass.

Specific Heat Capacity

Heat capacity characterizes the entire body under consideration.

Therefore, two bodies made of the same material may have different heat capacities simply because they contain different amounts of that material. A bucket of water and a spoonful of water are different bodies, and hence they can have different heat capacities.

We therefore introduce the idea of making the quantity specific to the material. Instead of characterizing the thermal behaviour of one particular body, we describe the thermal behaviour associated with a standard amount of the material.

The resulting quantity is called specific heat capacity. Specific heat capacity (\(s\)) is the heat capacity per unit mass:

\[ \boxed{s=\frac{1}{m}\frac{dQ}{dT}} \]

Thus, specific heat capacity represents the quantity of heat required to raise the temperature of unit mass of a substance by \(1\text{ K}\) (or \(1^\circ\text{C}\)).

The important consequence of this specification is that the quantity is no longer tied to the particular size of the body. A bucket of water and a spoonful of water are different bodies, but they are made of the same material, or species. Once heat capacity is specified per unit mass, we obtain a quantity that characterizes that material rather than the particular sample.

Heat capacity belongs to the body; specific heat capacity characterizes the material.
The word specific indicates that we have removed the dependence on the amount of material, so that the quantity can be associated with the material or species itself.

This gives specific heat capacity the same practical role as other material properties such as density. A standardised laboratory can measure the specific heat capacity of a material once, and that value can subsequently be used to describe the thermal behaviour of other samples of the same material.

Different materials therefore possess different specific heat capacities. For example, water and copper have different values, which means that equal masses of the two materials require different amounts of heat to produce the same change in temperature.

Formula and Variables

The fundamental relation connecting heat transfer, mass, temperature change, and specific heat capacity is:

\[ Q = m \cdot s \cdot \Delta T \]
  • Variables:
    • \( Q \) : Amount of heat transferred (Joules, \( \text{J} \)). Positive if heat is absorbed, negative if released.
    • \( m \) : Mass of the substance (Kilograms, \( \text{kg} \)).
    • \( s \) : Specific heat capacity of the material.
    • \( \Delta T \) : Change in temperature (\( T_{\text{final}} - T_{\text{initial}} \)). Can be evaluated in Kelvin (\( \text{K} \)) or degrees Celsius (\( ^\circ\text{C} \)) since \(\Delta T\) is identical in both scales.
  • SI Unit of \(s\) : \( \text{J} \cdot \text{kg}^{-1} \cdot \text{K}^{-1} \) (or \( \text{J} \cdot \text{kg}^{-1} \cdot ^\circ \text{C}^{-1}\))
  • Dimensional Formula of \(s\) : \( [s] = \text{[M}^0 \text{L}^2 \text{T}^{-2} \text{K}^{-1}\text{]} \)

A closely related quantity is the Molar Specific Heat Capacity (\(C\)).

Instead of considering one kilogram of a substance, molar heat capacity measures the heat required to raise the temperature of one mole of the substance by \(1\text{ K}\).

\[ Q = n \cdot C \cdot\Delta T \]
  • Variables:
    • \( n \) : Amount of substance in moles (\( \text{mol} \)).
    • \( C \) : Molar specific heat capacity of the material.
  • SI Unit of \(C\) : \( \text{J} \cdot \text{mol}^{-1} \cdot \text{K}^{-1} \) (or \( \text{J} \cdot \text{mol}^{-1} \cdot ^\circ \text{C}^{-1}\))
  • Dimensional Formula of \(C\) : \( [C] = \text{[M}^1 \text{L}^2 \text{T}^{-2} \text{K}^{-1} \text{mol}^{-1} \text{]} \)

For solids and liquids, specific heat capacities depend only on the material.

For gases, however, the situation is more subtle. In later chapters we will learn that the molar heat capacity of a gas also depends on the thermodynamic process through which heat is exchanged.

Specific Heat Capacity normalizes Heat Capacity by mass.
Molar Specific Heat Capacity normalizes Heat Capacity by number of moles.
Both quantities describe the thermal response of a material, but use different measures of the amount of substance.
This normalization is often the main source of confusion in units, dimensions and numerical values of heat capacities.
Bridging the Units (The Molar Pitfall):
The conversion between specific heat capacity (\(s\)) and molar specific heat capacity (\(C\)) is anchored by the molar mass (\(M\)) of the substance: \[ C = M \cdot s \] When calculating numerical values during question solving, ensure that the unit of mass is uniform.
For instance, while solving for \(\text{CO}_2\), its Molecular Wt. is (\(M = 44\text{ g/mol}\)).
Molecular Wt. \(M\) must be scaled to SI units as \(44 \times 10^{-3}\text{ kg/mol}\) before evaluating other parameters.

Depending Factors (Material and State)

Specific heat capacity is fundamentally an intensive property — it characterizes the substance itself (i.e. the material / species), not how much of it we have. However, it is not an absolute constant for a material.

  • Nature of the Material: It heavily depends on the molecular structure and degrees of freedom. Liquid water has \( s_{water} = 4200 \text{ J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)} \), while iron is around \( 450 \text{ J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)} \).
  • Physical State (Phase): The same chemical compound (material) will have entirely different specific heats in solid, liquid, or gas phases. For example, the specific heat of ice (\( s_{ice} = 2.1 \text{ kJ/(kg}\cdot\text{K)} \)) is roughly half that of liquid water.
    • The same chemical substance may have different specific heat capacities in different phases.
    For example, ice and water are both \(H_2O\), yet their specific heat capacities differ by nearly a factor of two.
    Therefore, specific heat capacity depends not only on the material but also on its thermodynamic state.
  • Temperature: Over large temperature ranges, \(s\) actually varies with temperature. However, in exam syllabus, it is treated as a constant over temperature ranges.
For exam, the specific heat capacities of water and ice should be memorized from NCERT: \[ s_{water} = 4.2 \text{ kJ/(kg}\cdot\text{K)} \] \[ s_{ice} = 2.1 \text{ kJ/(kg}\cdot\text{K)} \] Equivalent values in calorie units are:
\[ s_{water} = 1 \text{ cal/(g}\cdot\text{K)} \] \[ s_{ice} = 0.5 \text{ cal/(g}\cdot\text{K)} \]

Thermal Inertia

Specific Heat Capacity can be interpreted as a material's thermal inertia — its resistance to changing temperature when heat is supplied or removed.

To understand this interpretation, we must first identify what acts as the cause and what acts as the effect in a physical law.

In \(F = ma\), Force \(F\) is cause and acceleration \(a\) is the effect. Therefore, \(m\) is the capacity or inertia. This is because, general form of the equation is:

\[ \text{Cause} = \text{Capacity} \times \text{Effect Effect} = \text{Sensitivity} \times \text{Cause} \]

A large capacity indicates that system can absorb a lot of cause and show less effect. A large sensitivity indicates that system can show a lot of effect for less cause.

In \(Q = ms\Delta T\), Heat \(Q\) is cause and temperature change \(\Delta T\) is the effect.

Therefore, the product \(ms\) acts as the thermal capacity or thermal inertia of the system.

For a fixed mass, the Specific Heat Capacity (\(s\)) determines how strongly the material resists changes in temperature.

A large Specific Heat Capacity means that a large amount of heat is required to produce a small temperature change.

Water has an exceptionally large specific heat capacity. As a result, it can absorb or release large quantities of heat while undergoing only small temperature changes.

This is one of the primary reasons coastal climates remain comparatively moderate. Oceans act as massive thermal reservoirs, absorbing heat during the day and releasing it at night without experiencing extreme temperature fluctuations.
