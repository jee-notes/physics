Change of State & Latent Heat

Analyzing the energy required to break macroscopic molecular bonds.

Change of State

Matter universally exists in three primary phases: solid, liquid, and gas. A change of state occurs when a substance functionally shifts from one of these phases to another.

  • Melting (Fusion): Solid to liquid.
  • Freezing: Liquid to solid.
  • Boiling/Evaporation (Vaporization): Liquid to gas.
  • Condensation: Gas to liquid.
  • Sublimation: Solid directly to gas.

Isothermal Transitions

When we supply heat (\(Q = mc\Delta T\)) to an ice cube originally at \(-10^\circ\text{C}\), the ice warms up until it hits \(0^\circ\text{C}\). However, an interesting physics phenomenon occurs right at the melting point.

During a phase transition, the temperature of an isolated pure substance remains completely constant, even as immense amounts of heat are continually absorbed or rejected from the system.

Why does the temperature freeze at \(0^\circ\text{C}\)? Temperature is a macroscopic measure of the average kinetic energy of the molecules. However, during a phase change, the heat energy being supplied is used exclusively to alter the potential energy between the molecules — breaking the rigid intermolecular bonds holding the solid ice structure together to form the looser, flowing structure of liquid water.

Latent Heat of Fusion

Because the formula \( Q = mc\Delta T \) inherently forces \( Q=0 \) if \( \Delta T=0 \), we need a brand new physical mechanism to quantify the tremendous heat absorbed during melting.

Latent Heat of Fusion (\(L_f\)): The precise quantity of heat required to legally change a unit mass of a substance entirely from solid state to liquid state at its specific melting point.

The mathematical relation is independent of temperature change:

\[ Q = m \cdot L_f \]
  • Variables:
    • \( Q \): Heat supplied or removed during the phase change (J)
    • \( m \): Mass of the substance changing phase (kg)
    • \( L_f \): Latent heat of fusion of the material.
  • SI Unit: Joule per kilogram (\( \text{J} \cdot \text{kg}^{-1} \))
  • Dimensional Formula: \( [L] = \text{M}^0 \text{L}^2 \text{T}^{-2} \)

Standard Physical Constant

For water, the latent heat of fusion of ice is exceptionally high:
\[ L_f \approx 3.34 \times 10^5 \text{ J/kg} = 80 \text{ cal/g} \] This requires roughly 80 times more heat to melt $1 \text{ gram}$ of ice than to simply warm that water by $1^\circ\text{C}$. This massive heat requirement is why snow in the mountains does not melt instantaneously on the first warm day of spring.

Latent Heat of Vaporization

A perfectly analogous constant exists for the boiling transition when liquid converts entirely into a gaseous vapor at the boiling point.

Latent Heat of Vaporization (\(L_v\)): The quantity of heat required to change a unit mass of a substance entirely from liquid state to gaseous state at its specific boiling point.

The overarching formula is functionally identical, just scaling out the fusion parameter:

\[ Q = m \cdot L_v \]
For water, \( L_v \approx 2.26 \times 10^6 \text{ J/kg} \) (roughly \( 540 \text{ cal/g} \)). Notice that \(L_v\) is almost seven times larger than \(L_f\). Breaking the intermolecular bonds entirely to create a freely expanding gas requires substantially more energy than merely loosening them into a flowing liquid structure. This massive energy threshold is the primary reason steam burns are significantly more severe than burns from boiling water.
