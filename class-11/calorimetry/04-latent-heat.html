Change of State & Latent Heat

Analyzing the energy required to break macroscopic molecular bonds.

Change of State

For the present discussion, matter is considered to exist in three primary states:

  • Solid
  • Liquid
  • Gas
Other States
In chemistry we learn that other states exist like Bose-Einstein Condensate and Plasma.
These states are not part of JEE syllabus.

A change of state occurs when a substance undergoes a thermodynamic transition from one state to another. Six fundamental state changes are physically possible:

  • Melting (Fusion): Solid to liquid.
  • Freezing (Solidification): Liquid to solid.
  • Vaporization (Boiling/Evaporation): Liquid to gas.
  • Condensation: Gas to liquid.
  • Sublimation: Solid directly to gas.
  • Deposition: Gas directly to solid.

State vs. Phase (Thermodynamic Distinction)

While often used interchangeably, "State" and "Phase" have distinct thermodynamic definitions.

A State of matter refers to its broad macroscopic form (Solid, Liquid, Gas). A Phase is a highly specific, homogeneous region of matter that possesses identical physical and chemical properties throughout its entire bulk.

For example, solid carbon exists in one solid state, but it can exist in multiple distinct phases (e.g., diamond and graphite) depending on its internal crystal lattice structure.

In JEE syllabus, unless specifically mentioned otherwise, change of state is also called as phase transition.

Isothermal Phase Transitions

So far, we have modeled macroscopic heat exchange using the relation:

\[ Q = ms\Delta T \]

This equation inherently implies that any continuous transfer of thermal energy into a substance must manifest as a continuous increase in its temperature. However, a striking physical contradiction is observed when a substance undergoes a change of state.

Consider a block of ice initially at \(-10^\circ\text{C}\). As heat is supplied, the temperature of the ice rises steadily until it reaches exactly \(0^\circ\text{C}\). One might logically expect the temperature to continue rising as the burner continues to supply heat. Surprisingly, it does not.

During a change of state, a pure substance can absorb or release large amounts of heat while its temperature remains constant.

At \(0^\circ\text{C}\), the ice continues to absorb heat and melt into liquid water, yet its temperature remains fixed at \(0^\circ\text{C}\) until the very last crystal of ice has melted. A perfectly analogous phenomenon occurs during boiling: once water reaches \(100^\circ\text{C}\), further heat input does not raise the temperature, but instead gradually converts the liquid bulk into steam at \(100^\circ\text{C}\).

The Mathematical Contradiction:
The relation \(Q = ms\Delta T\) dictates that if \(\Delta T = 0\), then the transacted heat \(Q\) must also be zero. However, during a phase transition, a massive amount of heat is actively absorbed even though \(\Delta T = 0\). Therefore, the thermal mechanics of a state change cannot be described using Specific Heat Capacity alone.

The Microscopic Explanation

Why does the thermometer stop rising even though thermal energy is still actively entering the system?

Temperature is a macroscopic gauge of the average translational kinetic energy of the molecules. During an ordinary heating phase, the supplied thermal energy accelerates molecular motion, causing the temperature to rise.

During a phase transition, however, the system reroutes all incoming thermal energy away from kinetic excitation. Instead, the energy is consumed entirely as work done against intermolecular forces—altering the structural potential energy to dismantle a rigid solid lattice into a fluid, or to separate liquid molecules into a freely expanding gas.

During a phase transition, transacted heat modifies the intermolecular potential energy rather than the molecular kinetic energy. Because kinetic energy remains static, the temperature remains rigorously constant.

Coexistence and Equilibrium

The specific constant temperature at which a solid and liquid can indefinitely coexist in thermal equilibrium is defined as the melting point (or freezing point). Similarly, the temperature at which a liquid and gas coexist is the boiling point.

For pure water at standard atmospheric pressure (\(1\text{ atm}\)):

  • Melting Point: \(0^\circ\text{C}\)
  • Boiling Point: \(100^\circ\text{C}\)
At \(0^\circ\text{C}\), solid ice and liquid water can coexist indefinitely in thermal equilibrium. Similarly, at \(100^\circ\text{C}\), boiling water and steam exist in thermal equilibrium.

This proves that thermal equilibrium does not require interacting subsystems to exist in the identical state of matter. It strictly requires a zero temperature gradient (\(\Delta T = 0\)).

The total inability of the \(Q = ms\Delta T\) framework to account for isothermal energy absorption directly necessitates the introduction of a new physical parameter: Latent Heat.

Latent Heat

It is established that change of state is a thermodynamic phenomenon but at constant temperature. As a result, for change of state we cannot use the formula \( Q = ms\Delta T \).

This is because the formula inherently forces \( Q=0 \) if \( \Delta T=0 \)

Therefore, we need a new physical quantity to quantify heat exchange during melting and boiling.

The heat required during a change of state depends upon the particular transformation of state (wether melting or boiling) and the mass of the substance whose state is being changed.

The mathematical model to represent this thermodynamic process is:

\[ Q = m \cdot L \]
  • Variables:
    • \( Q \): Heat supplied (or removed) during the phase change (J)
    • \( m \): Mass of the substance changing phase (kg)
    • \( L \): Latent (hidden) heat as there is heat transfer without change in temperature
  • SI Unit: Joule per kilogram (\( \text{J} \cdot \text{kg}^{-1} \))
  • Dimensional Formula: \( [L] = \text{M}^0 \text{L}^2 \text{T}^{-2} \)

The value of L also depends on the pressure. Its value is usually quoted at standard atmospheric pressure.

Latent Heat of Fusion

Latent Heat of Fusion (\(L_f\)): The quantity of heat required to change a unit mass of a substance entirely from solid state to liquid state at its melting point.

\[ Q = m \cdot L_f \]

It is represented as \(L_f\) and it is a property of material.

For water, the latent heat of fusion of ice is exceptionally high:
\[ L_f = 3.33 \times 10^5 \text{ J/kg} = 80 \text{ cal/g} \] Whereas, the specific heat of water is \(1 \text{ cal/g}\).
Thus 80 times more heat is required to melt \(1 \text{ gram}\) of ice to water, than to simply warm that water by \(1^\circ\text{C}\).
This massive heat requirement is why snow in the mountains does not melt instantaneously on the first warm day of spring.

Latent Heat of Vaporization

Latent Heat of Vaporization (\(L_v\)): The quantity of heat required to change a unit mass of a substance entirely from liquid state to gaseous state at its boiling point.

\[ Q = m \cdot L_v \]

It is represented as \(L_v\) and it is a property of material.

For water, the latent heat of vaporisation is exceptionally high:
\[ L_v = 22.6 \times 10^5 \text{ J/kg} = 540 \text{ cal/g} \] Notice that \(L_v\) is almost seven times larger than \(L_f\).
Breaking the intermolecular bonds entirely to create a freely expanding gas requires substantially more energy than merely loosening them into a flowing liquid structure.
This massive energy threshold is the primary reason steam burns are significantly more severe than burns from boiling water.
