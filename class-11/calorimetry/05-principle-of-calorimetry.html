Principle of Calorimetry

Applying the law of conservation of energy to thermal interactions.

The Central Question

Suppose a hot iron block is placed inside a container of cold water.

The iron gradually cools while the water gradually warms.

After sufficient time, both attain the same temperature and thermal equilibrium is established.

A natural question arises: Can we predict the final equilibrium temperature of interacting bodies?

The answer lies in the law of Conservation of Energy.

Calorimetry is essentially the application of energy conservation to thermal interactions between systems.

Conservation of Energy in Thermal Systems

When two or more systems at different temperatures are placed in thermal contact, heat is exchanged between them.

The hotter bodies lose thermal energy while the colder bodies gain thermal energy.

If the interacting systems are perfectly isolated from the surroundings, no energy enters or leaves the combined system.

If the system is perfectly isolated — meaning no heat flows into the system from the surrounding environment, and no heat escapes the system — the total internal energy of the system remains constant.

Therefore, any heat lost by one part of the system must be gained by another part of the system.

This simple consequence of Conservation of Energy forms the foundation of Calorimetry.

Heat Lost vs Heat Gained

The Principle of Calorimetry: In an isolated thermal system, the heat lost by the hotter bodies is exactly equal to the heat gained by the colder bodies.

Mathematically, this balancing act can be expressed as:

\[ \sum Q_{\text{lost}} = \sum Q_{\text{gained}} \text{| same as |} \sum Q_{\text{lost}} - \sum Q_{\text{gained}} = 0 \] or \[ \sum Q_{changes} = 0 \]

The formula depends on what does \( Q_{\text{lost}} \) or \( Q_{\text{gained}} \) or \( Q_{\text{changes}} \) represent mathematically.

Such different forms are possible in any conservation equation - including momentum conservation or charge conservation in KCL.

Standard representation is: \( Q_{\text{lost}} \) represents the energy relinquished by the hot components as they cool down, and \( Q_{\text{gained}} \) represents the energy absorbed by the cold components as they warm up. But this is good to use if you know the initial temperatures of all interacting bodies.

In problems, when we don't know the initial temperature of interacting bodies, other forms of the formula are used.

Calculations in Isolated Systems

Because we use the specific heat formula \(Q = ms\Delta T\) to compute the heat, we can set up an algebraic equation to solve for unknown variables like the final mixing temperature \(T_f\).

If a hot mass \(m_1\) with specific heat \(s_1\) at initial temperature \(T_1\) is mixed with a cold mass \(m_2\) with specific heat \(s_2\) at initial temperature \(T_2\), they will reach an equilibrium temperature \(T_f\).

Irrespective of the values of \(m_1\) , \(s_1\) , \(m_2\) , \(s_2\) , as long as \(T_1 \gt T_2\) , heat will flow from \(\text{body}_1\) to \(\text{body}_2\) .

The equilibrium temperature \(T_f\) will always be \(T_1 \gt T_f \gt T_2\) .

The fundamental equality states:

\[ m_1 s_1 (T_1 - T_f) = m_2 s_2 (T_f - T_2) \]
  • Notice the structure of the temperature differences. In the "heat lost" expression on the left, we use \((T_1 - T_f)\) to assure the value is intrinsically positive.
  • In the "heat gained" expression on the right, we use \((T_f - T_2)\) to similarly guarantee a positive value.

Solving this linear equation for \( T_f \) gives the weighted average of the temperatures based on their individual thermal capacities:

\[ T_f = \frac{m_1 s_1 T_1 + m_2 s_2 T_2}{m_1 s_1 + m_2 s_2} \]

Calorimeter

In real experiments, heat exchange with the surroundings introduces errors.

To minimize these losses, thermal interactions are performed inside a device called a calorimeter.

Thus, a calorimeter is a device in which heat measurement can be done.

A calorimeter typically consists of:

  • A metallic vessel and stirrer made of the same material.
  • An insulating outer jacket.
  • A thermometer for temperature measurement.

The insulation reduces unwanted heat exchange with the surroundings, allowing the Principle of Calorimetry to be applied more accurately.
