Thermal Properties of Matter

Temperature, Heat and Heat Capacity - the properties that describe thermal behavior of matter.

Conceptual Overview

This chapter is the first chapter of the entire thermodynamics unit in physics.

The sequencing within the thermodynamics unit is often not explained clearly. There is, however, a definite progression, similar to the way the mechanics section is organised.

In mechanics, we first define the basic quantities used to describe motion, such as position, velocity, acceleration and then move to Newton's Laws of Motion. A similar sequencing is followed in thermal physics.

We first define the basic quantities used to describe thermal behaviour (of bulk matter):

  • Temperature
  • Heat
  • Heat Capacity

We then study how matter behaves thermally, through phenomena such as

  • Thermal Expansion
  • Change of Phase
  • Ideal-gas Behaviour

Finally, we bring these ideas together through the Laws of Thermodynamics. Thus, the sequence is:

  1. Define the quantities and concepts used to describe the thermal behaviour of bulk matter.
  2. Describe the thermal behaviour itself through standard processes and phenomena.
  3. Formulate the general laws of thermodynamics.

But before studying this sequence, we should first understand: what exactly is thermodynamics?

Just as mechanics is the study of the natural phenomenon of energy interaction between the body and its surrounding that affects its mechanical state, similarly, thermodynamics is the study of the natural phenomenon of energy interaction between the body and its surrounding that affects its thermodynamic state.

We study the dynamics of mechanical state in mechanics and the dynamics of thermodynamic state in thermodynamics.

This study became historically important with the invention of the steam engine, which was a crucial driver of the Industrial Revolution.

Before the widespread use of the steam engine, many machines relied on naturally available sources of power, such as wind in wind-mill or flowing water in water-wheel.
What an engine does is basically provide a source of mechanical drive.
The steam engine enabled mechanical power to be generated when and where it was needed, rather than depending directly on the availability of wind or flowing water.
It also enabled rail transport and other forms of powered transportation that did not depend on animal traction like horse carriages.

Understanding how thermal energy could be transferred and used to produce useful mechanical work was one of the central motivations behind the development of thermodynamics.

Thermodynamics Studies Energy Interaction of Bulk Matter

This is usually the main structural doubt in thermodynamics of a student. In mechanics too we study energy interaction then why have a separate unit for studying energy interactions?

The difference between mechanics and thermodynamics is the type of the energy whose interactions we are studying.
Firstly, thermodynamics is only related to bulk matter and bulk matter can posses different types of energies that may be independent of each other.
On the other hand, a point mass may only possess mechanical energy in the form of kinetic and potential energy.
Energy exchange with bulk matter might lead to deformation, change in temperature, change in state (solid,liquid,gas) etc.
A point mass has no shape or size, no temperature and no state.

In thermodynamics, we consider a system as an entity that possesses energy. This energy can be transferred between systems or between a system and its surroundings.

Energy is also transferred or transformed in mechanical processes. For example, when a pebble is dropped from a height, its gravitational potential energy is converted into kinetic energy:

\[ \boxed{U_g \rightarrow K} \]

Similarly, when a force acts on a body for some time, it can impart an impulse and change its momentum. Mechanical energy can also be transferred to a body through work done by a force.

In mechanics, the relevant energy is associated with the macroscopic mechanical degrees of freedom such as position, velocity and momentum, which we can describe directly using mechanics.

When we interact with a human, we treat the person as a whole rather than being concerned of their internal organs.
We assume that whatever is visible outside is also existing inside (because we don't care).
Similarly, in the point-mass model assumption, we describe the body's mechanical motion without considering its internal structure.
However, when studying bulk matter thermally, the internal structure becomes important.
Copper conducts heat much better than wood because their internal structures and mechanisms of energy transport differ.

Now consider what happens when the pebble strikes the ground inelastically. Just before the collision, the pebble has kinetic energy. After the collision, its macroscopic kinetic energy has greatly reduced.

So we naturally ask: Where did the kinetic energy go?

It is not as though another pebble has started moving or the ground as a whole has acquired an equal amount of visible kinetic energy. There is no corresponding change in the macroscopic mechanical degrees of freedom that we can track in the usual way.

The mechanical energy appears to have disappeared. But it has not disappeared from the physical world.

The energy has been distributed among the microscopic degrees of freedom of the matter constituting the pebble and the ground. The atoms and molecules may undergo microscopic motion and interaction, and energy may also be associated with microscopic deformation, vibration and sound.

Where did the KE go?
The kinetic energy did not disappear.
It entered the enormous collection of microscopic degrees of freedom of matter.
These microscopic degrees of freedom form a kind of energy reservoir.
We do not track the motion and interaction of every individual particle.
Instead, thermodynamics gives us a macroscopic framework for describing this otherwise hidden energy and its flow into and out of the microscopic reservoir.

This is the important conceptual transition from mechanics to thermodynamics. The question is no longer about a force transferring energy through work done. We now account for energy that has entered the microscopic realm, having a vast number of microscopic degrees of freedom that are not individually trackable.

Thus, when we study energy flow in thermodynamics, we are studying the transfer of energy into or out of a physical system whose energy can be stored in an enormous number of microscopic degrees of freedom.

Imagine a beekeeper having a swarm containing thousands of bees.
It is practically impossible to track the detailed motion of every bee individually, yet the beekeeper can describe and control the swarm using macroscopic quantities and actions such as feeding the swarm as a whole.
Similarly, matter contains an enormous number of microscopic constituents.
We do not track each particle individually.
Instead, thermodynamics describes the collective behaviour of this microscopic reservoir using macroscopic physical quantities.

We will later see how this leads naturally to quantities such as internal energy, temperature, pressure and others used to describe the macroscopic state of the system.

Describing Thermal Behaviour

In mechanics, we learned that our everyday descriptions of motion — terms such as slow, fast, position, distance etc. — are given precise physical meanings when we want to study motion scientifically.

Similarly, our everyday descriptions of thermal behaviour are based on familiar experiences.

We say that an object is hot or cold, or that one body becomes hotter or colder when something happens to it. These observations are useful, but they do not by themselves provide a quantitative description of the physical phenomenon.

We therefore introduce physical quantities that allow us to describe thermal behaviour in a precise and measurable way. The important quantities introduced at this stage are:

  • Temperature
  • Heat
  • Heat Capacity

These quantities allow us to move from the qualitative observations of hot and cold to a quantitative description of what is happening to matter.

Once these quantities are defined, we can use them to describe different thermal phenomena and understand how matter responds to changes in its thermal state.

As thermodynamics deals with the interaction of energy with the microscopic state of matter, a large variety of phenomena come under the study of thermal behaviour.
For example, when we heat a utensil on a gas flame, its temperature increases.
If water is placed in the utensil, the water also gets heated and its temperature increases.
Heating can also produce a change of state. Solids such as ice can melt, and liquids can change into gases.
Gases also show characteristic changes in their behaviour when they are heated or cooled.
All such observations form part of the thermal behaviour of matter that we will study across the thermal-physics section.

Scope of this Chapter

This chapter specifically deals with the Thermal Properties of Matter. A central problem here is to understand how temperature can be measured through a measurable physical property of matter. Such a property is called a thermometric property.

Physical quantities such as mass and charge are also properties of matter, but their definitions do not require us to construct a separate material-dependent measuring property in the same way that temperature does.

For example, if a sample of hydrogen gas has a mass of \(13\,\text{g}\), and a sample of ice has a mass of \(13\,\text{g}\), both simply have the same mass. The quantity being measured is the same regardless of the material.

Temperature can also be common to physically different bodies. For example, \(1\,\text{L}\) of water can be at \(27^\circ\text{C}\), while a \(50\,\text{g}\) copper ball can also be at \(27^\circ\text{C}\). The two bodies are completely different in amount and material, yet they can have the same temperature.

Temperature therefore describes a thermal state rather than the amount of matter present. A thermometer does not measure temperature by directly "looking at" temperature. Instead, it uses a physical property of matter that changes in a reproducible way with temperature. This is the basic idea of a thermometric property.

We will therefore study temperature scales and the thermometric properties of matter used to construct thermometers.

In this chapter, we specifically study the thermal expansion of solids and liquids and its use in thermometry. Other thermometric properties, such as electrical resistance and the thermal behaviour of gases, are studied separately.
