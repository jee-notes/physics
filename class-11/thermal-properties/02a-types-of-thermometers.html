Types of Thermometers

Investigating the physical instruments used to manifest temperature scales.

Liquid-in-Glass Thermometers

The most common and historically significant thermometers rely on the thermal expansion of a liquid—usually mercury or colored alcohol—contained within a glass bulb and a fine capillary tube.

The thermometric property is the length of the liquid column (\(L\)) in the capillary. Assuming the expansion of the liquid is relatively uniform, the temperature \(T\) at a given length \(L_T\) is calculated as:

\[ T = \left( \frac{L_T - L_0}{L_{100} - L_0} \right) \times 100^\circ\text{C} \]

Where:

  • \(L_0\) is the length of the column at the ice point (\(0^\circ\text{C}\)).
  • \(L_{100}\) is the length at the steam point (\(100^\circ\text{C}\)).

Mercury was favored for decades because it stays liquid over a wide temperature range (\(-39^\circ\text{C}\) to \(357^\circ\text{C}\)), expands uniformly, does not wet glass, and is easily visible. However, due to its high toxicity, alcohol (often dyed red) is increasingly preferred for everyday use.

Constant-Volume Gas Thermometer

Unlike liquid thermometers that are limited by freezing and boiling points, a gas thermometer operates on the principle that the pressure of a fixed mass of dilute gas at constant volume is directly proportional to its absolute temperature (Gay-Lussac's Law).

The thermometric property here is the pressure of the gas (\(P\)). The temperature \(T\) is given by:

\[ T = \left( \frac{P_T}{P_{\text{tr}}} \right) \times 273.16\text{ K} \]

Where \(P_{\text{tr}}\) is the pressure of the gas at the triple point of water (\(273.16\text{ K}\)).

Ideal Gas Standard: All real gases behave remarkably similarly at very low pressures. As pressure approaches zero, all gases perfectly align with this scale, earning the constant-volume gas thermometer the status of the standard against which all other thermometers are calibrated.

Platinum Resistance Thermometer

For high-precision industrial and scientific measurements across a very broad range, electrical resistance thermometers are used. The electrical resistance of a clean metal wire increases with temperature.

The thermometric property is the electrical resistance (\(R\)). Platinum is typically chosen because it is highly stable, resists oxidation, and provides reproducible results from roughly \(-200^\circ\text{C}\) to \(1200^\circ\text{C}\).

For a narrow temperature range, the relation is approximately linear:

\[ R_T = R_0(1 + \alpha T) \]

Where:

  • \(R_0\) is the resistance at \(0^\circ\text{C}\).
  • \(\alpha\) is the temperature coefficient of resistance for platinum.

Therefore, the temperature can be evaluated as:

\[ T = \left( \frac{R_T - R_0}{R_{100} - R_0} \right) \times 100^\circ\text{C} \]
