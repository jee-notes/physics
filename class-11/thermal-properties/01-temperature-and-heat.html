Temperature and Heat

Understanding the macroscopic fundamental quantities that govern thermal physics.

Concept of thermal state

Even though this chapter is about the thermal properties of matter, we cannot begin the thermodynamics section without first understanding what we mean by temperature and heat.

These two concepts are the minimum requirements for discussing any thermodynamic phenomena within the framework of science.

For example, we need to understand exactly what it means when we say that the temperature of a bucket of water is \(80^\circ\text{C}\). Similarly, when we say, "heat the mixture to \(70^\circ\text{C}\)," what exactly does this statement mean?

Scientific study requires such everyday statements to be expressed through unambiguous physical definitions.

Before defining temperature, however, we first introduce a more basic idea. We consider a physical system and say that it exists in a particular thermal state. This is similar to mechanics, where we first consider the mechanical state of a body and describe it, for example, as being at rest or in motion.

Thus, just as a body can have different mechanical states, a system can have different thermal states. The thermal state determines how the system behaves when it is brought into thermal contact with another system — whether it will spontaneously gain or lose heat.

We can then ask: how do we characterize the thermal state of a system? This leads us to the concept of temperature.

Concept of Temperature

Temperature is a physical property of matter that quantitatively expresses our common notions of hotness and coldness.

At the macroscopic level, temperature characterises the thermal state of a system. When two bodies capable of thermal interaction are placed in contact, the difference in their temperatures determines the direction of spontaneous heat transfer.

For ordinary thermal contact, heat flows spontaneously from the body at higher temperature to the body at lower temperature. The flow continues until thermal equilibrium is reached.

Definition of Temperature: Temperature is a scalar physical quantity that characterises the thermal state of a macroscopic system and determines the direction of spontaneous heat transfer when two systems are brought into thermal contact.

Microscopically, temperature is related to the energy associated with the random microscopic motion of the constituents of matter. For an ideal gas, the temperature is directly proportional to the average translational kinetic energy of its particles.

  • SI Unit: Kelvin (\(K\))
  • Dimensional Formula: \( [K] \)
  • Nature: Scalar quantity
  • Property type: Intensive

Temperature is an intensive property; it does not depend on the amount of substance present. This is different from mass (or other extensive properties like volume).

For example, a cup of hot water and a large pot of hot water and a spoon of hot alcohol - all can be at \(60^\circ\text{C}\), even though, all the three systems contain very different amounts (or types) of matter and therefore can possess very different amounts of internal energy.

Temperature is not a measure of the total thermal energy of a body.
A larger body can contain much more internal energy than a smaller body while both bodies have the same temperature.

Concept of Heat

Temperature describes the state of a system. Heat, on the other hand, does not describe a state of the system. It describes a process of energy transfer.

Definition of Heat: Heat (\(Q\)) is energy transferred across the boundary of a system solely because of a temperature difference between the system and its surroundings.

Therefore, heat is associated with the energy transfer process. We say that heat flowed into the system or heat flowed out of the system.

  • SI Unit: Joule (\(J\))
  • Common Unit: Calorie (\(\text{cal}\))
  • Conversion: \(1\,\text{cal} = 4.18\,\text{J}\)
  • Dimensional Formula: \( [ML^2T^{-2}] \)
A system does not "contain heat". A system contains internal energy.
Heat refers only to energy while it is being transferred because of a temperature difference.
Once the transferred energy becomes part of the system, we no longer call that energy "heat"; it contributes to the system's internal energy.

This distinction is fundamental: temperature is a property of the state of a system, whereas heat describes energy transfer between systems.

Heat Flow

Consider two bodies containing very different amounts of water: one contains \(1000\,\text{kg}\) of water at \(98^\circ\text{C}\), while the other contains only \(1\,\text{g}\) of water at \(99^\circ\text{C}\).

If the two bodies are brought into thermal contact, heat flows from the \(1\,\text{g}\) sample at \(99^\circ\text{C}\) to the \(1000\,\text{kg}\) sample at \(98^\circ\text{C}\).

The \(1000\,\text{kg}\) body contains enormously more internal energy than the \(1\,\text{g}\) body.
Yet heat flows from the \(1\,\text{g}\) body to the \(1000\,\text{kg}\) body.
Thus, the amount of matter or the total internal energy of a body does not determine the direction of heat flow.
The direction is determined by the temperature difference.

This is an experimentally observed behaviour of matter. Thermodynamics is built upon such empirical observations and expresses them in the form of general laws.

Temperature and Internal Energy
Temperature is related to the internal energy of a system, but temperature does not singlehandedly indicate the internal energy.
For the same mass of the same substance, a higher temperature generally corresponds to greater internal energy.
For example, \(1000\,\text{kg}\) of water at \(99^\circ\text{C}\) has more internal energy than \(1000\,\text{kg}\) of water at \(98^\circ\text{C}\).
However, the internal energy also depends on other properties of the system, such as the amount of matter and the nature and state of the substance.
Therefore, temperature alone is not a measure of the total internal energy of a body.

This is why \(1\,\text{g}\) of water at \(99^\circ\text{C}\) can transfer heat to \(1000\,\text{kg}\) of water at \(98^\circ\text{C}\), even though the \(1000\,\text{kg}\) body contains much more total internal energy.

Temperature is an intensive property, whereas internal energy is an extensive property. It is somewhat like density. A \(1\,\text{g}\) sample of mercury is denser than \(1000\,\text{kg}\) of water, but the \(1000\,\text{kg}\) sample of water has much greater total mass than the sample of mercury.

Temperature Increase Does Not Mean Heat

A change in temperature by itself does not tell us whether heat has been transferred.

The temperature increase is an effect of energy entering the system; it does not characterize the mode of energy transfer.

Consider a utensil initially at room temperature. When it is brought into contact with a hot flame, the flame is at a higher temperature than the utensil.

Energy is transferred to the utensil because of this temperature difference, and its temperature increases. This energy transfer is called heat.

Now take a similar utensil and mechanically rub your hands against it. The utensil again becomes slightly hotter than before.

However, the energy has entered the utensil through a mechanical interaction, not because of a temperature difference.

Therefore, this energy transfer is not heat.

Both processes can increase the temperature of the utensil.
But only the first involves energy transfer on account of a temperature difference.
Therefore, \[ \boxed{\text{temperature increase} \neq \text{heat transfer}} \]

Thus, to identify heat, we should not ask only "Did the temperature increase?" We should ask: "Why did the energy enter the system?"

Thermal Equilibrium and the Zeroth Law

Consider two macroscopic bodies at different temperatures. When they are placed in thermal contact, energy is transferred from the body at higher temperature to the body at lower temperature.

As energy is transferred, their thermal states change and their temperatures tend to approach each other. Eventually, the net heat transfer becomes zero. The bodies are then said to be in thermal equilibrium.

Thermal Equilibrium: Two systems are said to be in thermal equilibrium when, after being placed in thermal contact, there is no net transfer of heat between them.

The empirical observation that thermal equilibrium possesses a transitive character is expressed by the Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics.

Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics:
If body \(A\) is in thermal equilibrium with body \(B\), and body \(B\) is separately in thermal equilibrium with body \(C\), then body \(A\) and body \(C\) are also in thermal equilibrium with each other.

The importance of the Zeroth Law is much greater than its simple statement may suggest. It tells us that thermal equilibrium can be characterised by a common scalar property. That property is what we call temperature.

Thus, the Zeroth Law provides the logical foundation for assigning a temperature to a system. If two systems are each in thermal equilibrium with a third system, they must have the same temperature.

The Thermometer Idea
Let body \(B\) be a thermometer.
If body \(A\) and body \(C\) both establish thermal equilibrium with the thermometer and produce the same thermometer reading, then \(A\) and \(C\) have the same temperature
Therefore, when placed in thermal contact with each other, there is no net heat transfer between them.

A thermometer is therefore not merely a device for "measuring hotness". Its operation rests on the deeper physical principle expressed by the Zeroth Law.

Empirical Observations

The physical quantities used in thermal physics arise from observations of how matter behaves. Scientists observed regular and reproducible thermal phenomena in nature and developed physical quantities and measurement procedures to describe them, much as quantities such as position, velocity and acceleration were developed to describe motion.

In the case of temperature, the key observation is the existence of thermal equilibrium. The Zeroth Law then provides the principle that makes temperature measurement possible.

This led to the development of thermometry — the measurement of temperature using a measurable physical property that changes systematically with temperature.

To construct a temperature scale, scientists needed reproducible reference states. Two historically important reference points were obtained using water and its phase changes under standard atmospheric pressure.

  • Ice point: melting point of ice at \(1\,\text{atm}\), taken as \(0^\circ\text{C}\).
  • Steam point: boiling point of water at \(1\,\text{atm}\), taken as \(100^\circ\text{C}\).

The interval between these two fixed points was divided into 100 equal parts to construct the Celsius temperature scale.

The conceptual sequence is:
Thermal behaviour observed in nature \(\rightarrow\) Zeroth Law to describe it qualitatively \(\rightarrow\) temperature as a state variable \(\rightarrow\) thermometry and temperature scales

State Variables and Flow

To fully grasp the physical nature of temperature, it is helpful to look at mathematically identical structures in other branches of physics.

In macroscopic physics, spontaneous flow between two systems is always driven by a difference in a specific intensive state variable.

Physics Domain State Variable Driving Condition Process / Transfer Nature of State
Thermal Physics Temperature (\(T\)) Temperature Difference (\(\Delta T\)) Heat Flow (\(Q\)) Thermal State
Fluid Mechanics Pressure (\(P\)) Pressure Difference (\(\Delta P\)) Fluid Flow Mechanical State
Electrostatics Electric Potential (\(V\)) Potential Difference (\(\Delta V\)) Charge Flow (\(I\)) Electrical State
The Difference Drives the Process
Just as a pressure difference (\(\Delta P\)) drives fluid flow, and a potential difference (\(\Delta V\)) drives charge flow, a temperature difference (\(\Delta T\)) drives heat flow between two bodies.
The transfer proceeds in the direction that reduces the difference, terminating when the state variables equalize.

Thus, temperature does not behave like a mechanical force. It behaves like a potential.

There exists a deeper mathematical analogy between these physical quantities.
In mechanics, a scalar potential energy field \(U(x)\) produces force through its spatial derivative: \[ \boxed{F=-\frac{dU}{dx}} \] Similarly, temperature is a scalar field \(T(x)\), and its spatial derivative \[ \boxed{\frac{dT}{dx}} \] acts as a local driving quantity for heat transfer.
Thus, although temperature itself is not a force, the temperature gradient is mathematically analogous to a force in the sense that both are spatial derivatives of scalar fields and represent local driving tendencies.
For heat conduction, this appears explicitly in Fourier's law: \[ \boxed{\vec J_Q=-k\nabla T} \] Just as force points in the direction of decreasing potential energy, heat flux points in the direction of decreasing temperature.

Consider a charged conductor at a high electric potential. The conductor contains electrical potential energy, but it does not contain "current." Current only exists when a potential difference causes charge to move.

Similarly, a body at a high temperature contains internal energy. It does not contain "heat." Heat is strictly the energy in transit, existing only while the temperature difference drives the transfer.
