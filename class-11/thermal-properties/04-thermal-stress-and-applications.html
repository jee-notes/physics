Thermal Stress and Applications

Investigating the colossal mechanical forces generated by constrained thermal expansion.

Thermal Stress and Strain

When a solid is free to expand or contract as its temperature changes, no internal stresses build up. However, if the ends of a rigid rod are rigidly clamped or clamped securely between unyielding walls, it is artificially prevented from expanding or contracting. This geometric constraint forces the material to undergo elastic deformation.

Thermal Stress Derivation:

Suppose a cool rod of original length \(L\), cross-sectional area \(A\), and Young's modulus \(Y\) is clamped. The temperature is raised by \(\Delta T\).

If the rod were totally free, it would forcefully expand by a distance: \[ \Delta L = \alpha L \Delta T \] Because it is clamped, the walls must physically compress the rod by exactly this \(\Delta L\) back to its original length \(L\). This induces mechanical strain within the rod. The resulting thermal strain is:

\[ \text{Strain} = \frac{\Delta L}{L} = \frac{\alpha L \Delta T}{L} = \alpha \Delta T \]

According to Hooke's Law (\( \text{Stress} = Y \times \text{Strain} \)), the thermal stress acting on the rod is:

\[ \text{Thermal Stress} = Y \alpha \Delta T \]

The total force \(F\) the rod dangerously exerts outward against its rigid supports is:

\[ F = (\text{Stress}) \times A = Y A \alpha \Delta T \]
Remember, thermal stress forces are staggeringly huge. If a massive steel girder is prevented from marginally expanding on a hot day, it can easily buckle surrounding concrete or snap high-strength bolts. This is why railway tracks and bridge decks have engineered expansion gaps.

Bimetallic Strips

A bimetallic strip is constructed by firmly riveting or welding two strips of different metals (e.g., brass and steel) together along their length. Because the two metals possess different coefficients of linear expansion (e.g., \(\alpha_{\text{brass}} > \alpha_{\text{steel}}\)), the strip cannot simply lengthen equally when heated or cooled.

When heated, the brass naturally tries to expand more than the steel. To accommodate this unequal expansion while remaining physically bonded, the entire strip is forced to definitively violently curve. The metal with the higher expansivity (brass) is forced onto the outer radius of the curve.

Because the degree of curvature corresponds beautifully to temperature, bimetallic coils are commonly used as cheap, rugged thermal switches in mechanical thermostats, fire alarms, and domestic circuit breakers.

Effect of Temperature on Pendulum Clocks

The time period \(T_0\) of a simple pendulum is given structurally by \(T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}\).

If a pendulum clock has a metallic rod, warming it in summer will cause its physical length to expand to \(L' = L(1 + \alpha\Delta T)\). Consequently, its time period subtly increases to: \[ T' = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L(1 + \alpha\Delta T)}{g}} \approx T_0(1 + \frac{1}{2}\alpha\Delta T) \]

A longer period strictly means the clock takes more time to complete each swing; it runs slower. The fractional loss of time per second is \(\frac{\Delta T_0}{T_0} = \frac{1}{2}\alpha\Delta T\). Over an entire day (\(86,400\text{ s}\)), the total time lost is:

Daily Error Formula: \[ \text{Time lost/gained per day} = \frac{1}{2} \alpha \Delta T \times 86400 \text{ seconds} \]

Expansion of Cavities

A common conceptual trap is predicting how a hollow spherical cavity or a hole within a solid block will behave when uniformly heated. Will the material expand inward, shrinking the hole?

No. As previously established, thermal expansion scales everything proportionately, like an image enlargement. The circumference of a hole is lined by atoms. As those atoms desperately expand and push away from one another, the perimeter physically grows.

The Rule of Cavities: The volume of a cavity inside a solid block expands identically to the volume of a solid piece of the exact same material that perfectly fills the cavity. If a brass sheet with a \(2\text{ cm}\) hole is heated, the hole expands exactly as a solid \(2\text{ cm}\) brass coin would expand.
