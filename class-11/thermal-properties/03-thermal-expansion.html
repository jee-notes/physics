Thermal Expansion

The macroscopic geometric response of matter to changes in its internal energy state.

Introduction to Thermal Expansion

Most substances — solids, liquids and gases — generally expand when their temperature increases and contract when their temperature decreases.

This change in the dimensions of matter with temperature is called thermal expansion.

Thermally induced dimensional changes are important in engineering and architecture because even small changes in temperature can produce appreciable changes in the dimensions of large structures.

Train tracks, bridge joints and the pistons of engines are therefore designed to accommodate the expansion and contraction that occur with changes in temperature.

Although expansion on heating is the usual behaviour of matter, there are exceptions over particular temperature ranges. A familiar example is the anomalous expansion of water between \(0^\circ\text{C}\) and \(4^\circ\text{C}\).

For ordinary materials over a suitable temperature range, the change in dimension is generally proportional to both the original dimension and the change in temperature.

Microscopic Origin of Thermal Expansion

The microscopic origin of thermal expansion is related to the nature of the interaction between neighbouring particles.

The interaction between neighbouring particles is related to the inter-molecular forces which is studied in physical chemistry in detail - like water has inter-molecular hydrogen bonding.

The potential energy of this interaction (inter-molecular force of attraction) depends on their separation \(r\), and this potential is asymmetric about its minimum.

\[ \boxed{U(r)\text{ is minimum at }r=r_0} \]

For \(r^- \lt r_0\) , \(U(r^-) \gt U(r_0)\) and for \(r^+ \gt r_0\) , \(U(r^+) \gt U(r_0)\) .

As a simple model assumption, we can say that at absolute \(0 \text{ K}\) temperature, the molecules of the material are stationary and at a distance \(r_0\) from each other.

When the temperature of the material is increased to non-zero, energy is supplied to its molecules, enabling thermal motion at the microscopic level of degrees of freedom. The molecules now explore larger range of separations from each other.

Because the interparticle potential is asymmetric, this increased microscopic motion produces an increase in the average separation between neighbouring particles.

Solids

In a solid, the particles are relatively strongly bound and remain around well-defined equilibrium positions in the structure of the material. They vibrate about these positions.

Suppose the equilibrium separation of two neighbouring atoms is \(r_0=10\,\mathring{A}\) . At a finite temperature, the atoms vibrate and their instantaneous separation may vary, for example, from \(9\,\mathring{A}\) to \(12\,\mathring{A}\) .

This oscillation is not symmetric about \(r_0\) . This means that \(r_\text{avg} \gt (r_0 = 10\,\mathring{A})\)

Therefore, the average separation is shifted towards the larger-\(r\) side: \[ \boxed{r_\text{avg} = 10.5\,\mathring{A} } \]

These numbers are only illustrative. The important point is that the oscillation is not symmetric about \(r_0\) .

As the temperature is increased further, the amplitude of these microscopic vibrations increases and the average separation only increases further. Since this happens throughout the material, the cumulative effect is an increase in its macroscopic dimensions of length, area and volume.

Liquids

A liquid does not have a rigid lattice with particles vibrating about fixed lattice positions. Its molecules are continuously moving, changing neighbours and rearranging themselves.

Nevertheless, the molecules still interact with one another through intermolecular forces, and the corresponding intermolecular potential is also generally asymmetric.

As the temperature increases, the molecules acquire greater microscopic energy and explore a larger range of intermolecular separations.

Because of the asymmetry of the intermolecular potential, the average intermolecular separation increases. Unlike a solid, the liquid does not expand by stretching a fixed lattice; instead, its molecules rearrange into a configuration with a larger average separation.

The microscopic mechanism is therefore fundamentally an effect of the anharmonic nature of intermolecular interactions. The precise macroscopic behaviour depends on the material and, in some cases, can even become anomalous.

For gases, the mechanism is different and we shall study in kinetic theory of gases.
