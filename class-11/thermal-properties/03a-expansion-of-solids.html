Expansion of Solids

Mathematical formulations of solid body dimensional changes due to thermal energy.

Linear Expansion

When a solid object is subjected to a temperature change \(\Delta T\), any specific linear dimension (such as length, radius, or a diagonal) undergoes a fractional change. If the original length of the rod is \(L\), the change in length \(\Delta L\) is experimentally found to be proportional to both \(L\) and \(\Delta T\).

Coefficient of Linear Expansion (\(\alpha\)): \[ \Delta L = \alpha L \Delta T \]

Where:

  • \(\Delta L = L_{final} - L_{initial}\)
  • \(\alpha\) is a material constant known as the coefficient of linear expansion.
  • \(\Delta T\) is the change in temperature.

  • SI Unit: \(K^{-1}\) or \(^\circ\text{C}^{-1}\)

    • The new length \(L'\) after expansion is given by: \[ L' = L(1 + \alpha \Delta T) \]

    Areal (Surface) Expansion

    If we consider a two-dimensional surface area \(A\), an increase in temperature will cause both linear dimensions (length and width) to increase, leading to a proportional increase in the total area.

    Coefficient of Areal Expansion (\(\beta\)): \[ \Delta A = \beta A \Delta T \]

    Where \(\beta\) is the material's coefficient of areal (or superficial) expansion. The new area \(A'\) is:

    \[ A' = A(1 + \beta \Delta T) \]

    Volume (Cubical) Expansion

    For a three-dimensional object, thermal expansion occurs across all three axes simultaneously, resulting in a change in the total volume \(V\).

    Coefficient of Volume Expansion (\(\gamma\)): \[ \Delta V = \gamma V \Delta T \]

    Where \(\gamma\) is the coefficient of volume expansion. The expanded volume \(V'\) is:

    \[ V' = V(1 + \gamma \Delta T) \]
    Remember, thermal expansion acts exactly like a photographic enlargement. If a solid material contains a hole, the hole expands in diameter exactly as a solid plug of the same material would expand. The cavity gets larger, not smaller.

    Relation Between Alpha, Beta, and Gamma

    Because an isotropic material (a material whose properties are identical in all directions) expands equally along all its axes, the three coefficients (\(\alpha\), \(\beta\), \(\gamma\)) are mathematically related.

    Consider a square plate of side \(L\). Its area \(A = L^2\). Following linear expansion, the new length is \(L(1+\alpha\Delta T)\). The new area is \(A' = [L(1+\alpha\Delta T)]^2 = L^2(1 + 2\alpha\Delta T + \alpha^2\Delta T^2)\).

    Since \(\alpha\) is typically very small (\(\approx 10^{-5} \text{ K}^{-1}\)), the quadratic term \(\alpha^2\) is negligible. Thus: \[ A' \approx A(1 + 2\alpha\Delta T) \] Comparing this with \(A' = A(1 + \beta \Delta T)\), we define: \[ \beta = 2\alpha \]

    Similarly, for a cube of volume \(V = L^3\), applying binomial expansion and dropping higher-order \(\alpha\) terms yields: \[ \gamma = 3\alpha \]

    Fundamental Isotropic Relation: \[ \alpha : \beta : \gamma = 1 : 2 : 3 \] \[ \frac{\alpha}{1} = \frac{\beta}{2} = \frac{\gamma}{3} \]
