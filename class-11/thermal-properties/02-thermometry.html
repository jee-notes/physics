Thermometry

The science of measuring temperature objectively and accurately.

Zeroth Law and Temperature

The Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics establishes the transitive nature of thermal equilibrium. This is what allows us to assign a common temperature to systems that are in thermal equilibrium with each other and, therefore, to measure temperature.

We therefore need to look for some measurable thermometric property of matter, or some measurable physical process, that changes systematically with temperature.

\[ \boxed{X=f(T)} \]

The important point is that temperature itself does not have to be measured directly. We can measure some physical property \(X\) that is related to temperature and use it to determine the temperature.

Since temperature is a common property of thermal state, we can compare and equate the temperatures of two completely different types of matter, even if the thermometric properties used for the two systems are completely different.

For example, a temperature measured using the volume of a liquid and a temperature measured using the electrical resistance of a wire can be compared because both ultimately refer to the same physical quantity: temperature.

While human senses can roughly distinguish between "hot" and "cold", such sensations are subjective and not sufficiently accurate for scientific measurement. Thermometry is therefore concerned with the objective and quantitative measurement of temperature.

Any physical property that changes reproducibly with temperature can be used as a thermometric property. Some common examples are:

  • The volume of a liquid, as used in a mercury-in-glass thermometer.
  • The pressure of a gas at constant volume.
  • The electrical resistance of a conducting material.
  • The electromotive force generated by a thermocouple.
  • The wavelength of radiation emitted by a hot body.

If a thermometric property \(X\) varies linearly with temperature \(T\), we can write:

\[ \boxed{T=aX+b} \]

where \(a\) and \(b\) are constants determined by calibrating the thermometer using known reference temperatures or fixed points.

Temperature Scales (Celsius, Fahrenheit, Kelvin)

Once we have a way of comparing temperatures, we need to construct a temperature scale so that temperatures can be assigned numerical values.

Students often miss why we need a temperature scale. A thermometric property allows us to compare thermal states, but to express a temperature numerically, we need to define a unit of temperature, just as we need \(1\,\text{m}\) as the unit of length or \(1\,\text{kg}\) as the unit of mass. Thus, constructing a temperature scale essentially means defining how the thermal quantity is to be assigned numerical values and establishing the size of its unit.

Traditionally, this is done by identifying reproducible physical conditions called fixed points. Two important fixed points are the ice point and the steam point of water at standard atmospheric pressure (\(1\,\text{atm}\)).

As already discussed, temperature is a property of bulk matter.
Suppose we take \(1\,\text{kg}\) of ice at a particular temperature.
The entire bulk of the ice is in the same thermal state and therefore has the same temperature.
It is observed experimentally that pure substances undergo phase changes at definite temperatures under a specified pressure.
If the entire \(1\,\text{kg}\) sample of ice is heated uniformly at \(1\,\text{atm}\), its temperature rises until it reaches the melting point. At that point, the ice begins to melt into water.
Similarly, when water is heated at \(1\,\text{atm}\), it eventually reaches its boiling point and changes into steam. Thus, the ice point and steam point are identified from reproducible physical phenomena.
The thermometer is then calibrated using these known reference conditions.
In other words, the fixed points are not created by the thermometer. They are observed physical states that are then used to construct the temperature scale.
For example, we can use an uncalibrated mercury thermometer to observe that the mercury column remains at the same position while ice is melting into water.
This tells us that the thermal state of the ice-water system remains unchanged during the phase change.

Once two fixed points have been chosen, we also need to decide how the interval between them is to be divided into numerical degrees. Different temperature scales use different numerical assignments and different numbers of divisions for the same physical interval.

1. The Celsius Scale (\(^\circ\text{C}\))

  • Ice Point: \(0^\circ\text{C}\)
  • Steam Point: \(100^\circ\text{C}\)
  • The interval between the two fixed points is divided into \(100\) equal parts.

2. The Fahrenheit Scale (\(^\circ\text{F}\))

  • Ice Point: \(32^\circ\text{F}\)
  • Steam Point: \(212^\circ\text{F}\)
  • The interval between the two fixed points is divided into \(180\) equal parts.

3. The Kelvin Scale (Absolute Scale) (\(K\))

The Kelvin scale is an absolute temperature scale. Its zero is not assigned to the freezing point of water. Instead, the zero of the scale corresponds to absolute zero, the limiting lowest temperature.

  • Absolute Zero: \(0\,\text{K}\), equivalent to \(-273.15^\circ\text{C}\)
  • Triple Point of Water: \(273.16\,\text{K}\), where ice, liquid water and water vapour coexist in equilibrium.

The size of one kelvin is the same as the size of one degree Celsius:

\[ \boxed{1\,\text{K}=1^\circ\text{C}\text{ in temperature interval}} \]

Therefore, a temperature difference of \(1\,\text{K}\) is numerically equal to a temperature difference of \(1^\circ\text{C}\).

Conversion Between Scales

Different temperature scales assign different numerical values to the same physical temperature. Since the Celsius, Fahrenheit and Kelvin scales are constructed linearly, their readings can be interconverted.

The basic idea is simply the unitary method: compare how far a temperature lies between the two fixed points on one scale and use the same fractional position on another scale.

Let \(T_C\), \(T_F\), and \(T_K\) be the temperature readings on the Celsius, Fahrenheit and Kelvin scales respectively. The general conversion relation is

\[ \boxed{\frac{T_C-0}{100-0}=\frac{T_F-32}{212-32}=\frac{T_K-273.15}{373.15-273.15}} \]

From this, we obtain the practically useful relations:

Celsius to Fahrenheit: \[ T_F=\frac{9}{5}T_C+32 \]

Celsius to Kelvin: \[ T_K=T_C+273.15 \]

Note on Change in Temperature (\(\Delta T\))

The numerical value of a temperature difference depends on the size of the unit used in the scale. Since one degree Celsius and one kelvin have the same size,

\[ \boxed{\Delta T_C=\Delta T_K} \]

whereas one degree Fahrenheit is smaller than one degree Celsius. Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\Delta T_F=\frac{9}{5}\Delta T_C} \]

How Does the Conversion Formula Work?

To understand the basic idea behind the conversion formula, consider a different example.

Imagine a one-dimensional world represented by a straight line. There are two fixed points in this physical space: point \(A\) is the location of a school and point \(B\) is the location of a home.

Now imagine two people describing the same physical space using different coordinate systems. Let Person 1 be called Celsius and Person 2 be called Fahrenheit.

Celsius chooses the home, point \(B\), as \(x=0\) and Fahrenheit chooses the same physical point \(B\) as \(x=32\)

Similarly, Celsius assigns \(x=100\) to the school, point \(A\), while Fahrenheit assigns \(x=212\) to the same physical point \(A\).

Same physical points, different numerical coordinates.
The school and home are physically the same two points for both people.
Only the numerical descriptions are different.
For Celsius, the interval from home to school contains \(100\) equal units.
For Fahrenheit, the same physical interval contains \(180\) equal units.

Now consider a point exactly halfway between the school and the home. Physically, it is the same point for both observers.

Celsius describes this midpoint as \(x=50\) because it is \(50\) units from home out of a total \(100\) units.

Fahrenheit describes the same physical midpoint as \(x=122\) because it is \(90\) units from home out of a total \(180\) units: \(32+90=122\) .

The important point is that the physical position has not changed.
Only its numerical coordinate has changed.
Thus, \[ \frac{50-0}{100-0}=\frac{122-32}{212-32}=\frac12 \] Both observers are assigning the same fractional position within the same physical interval.
This is exactly the idea used when converting between temperature scales.

Temperature-scale conversion therefore does not mean converting one physical temperature into another. The physical thermal state remains the same; only its numerical representation changes from one scale to another.
