Thermometry

The science of measuring temperature objectively and accurately.

Principles of Thermometry

While human senses can roughly estimate "hot" and "cold", they are subjective and highly inaccurate. Thermometry is the branch of physics focused on the precise, objective measurement of temperature.

Any physical property that changes linearly and reproducibly with temperature can be used to construct a thermometer. Such a property is termed a thermometric property. Examples include:

  • The volume of a liquid (e.g., in a mercury-in-glass thermometer).
  • The pressure of a gas at constant volume.
  • The electrical resistance of a conductive wire.
  • The electromotive force generated by a thermocouple.

If a thermometric property \(X\) varies linearly with temperature \(T\), we can write: \[ T = aX + b \] where \(a\) and \(b\) are constants determined by calibrating the thermometer against known reference temperatures (fixed points).

Temperature Scales (Celsius, Fahrenheit, Kelvin)

To construct a scale, we traditionally define two fixed points—typically the freezing/ice point and the boiling/steam point of pure water at standard atmospheric pressure (\(1\text{ atm}\)).

1. The Celsius Scale (\(^\circ\text{C}\)):
  • Ice Point: \(0^\circ\text{C}\)
  • Steam Point: \(100^\circ\text{C}\)
  • The interval between the fixed points is divided into 100 equal parts.
2. The Fahrenheit Scale (\(^\circ\text{F}\)):
  • Ice Point: \(32^\circ\text{F}\)
  • Steam Point: \(212^\circ\text{F}\)
  • The interval is divided into 180 equal parts.
3. The Kelvin Scale (Absolute Scale) (\(K\)):

The Kelvin scale does not use the freezing point of water as its logical zero. Instead, it places zero at Absolute Zero—the theoretical temperature at which all thermal motion of atoms ceases.

  • Absolute Zero: \(0\text{ K}\) (Equivalent to \(-273.15^\circ\text{C}\))
  • Triple Point of Water (where ice, water, and vapor coexist): \(273.16\text{ K}\)

The size of one degree Kelvin is constructed to be identical to one degree Celsius.

Conversion Between Scales

Because temperature scales are linear relative to each other, they can be reliably interconverted. The general principle relies on equating fractional distances between the ice point and steam point across all scales.

Let \(T_C\), \(T_F\), and \(T_K\) be the temperature readings on the Celsius, Fahrenheit, and Kelvin scales respectively. The universal conversion formula is:

\[ \frac{T_C - 0}{100} = \frac{T_F - 32}{180} = \frac{T_K - 273.15}{100} \]

From this, we yield two practically useful formulas:

Celsius to Fahrenheit: \[ T_F = \frac{9}{5}T_C + 32 \]

Celsius to Kelvin: \[ T_K = T_C + 273.15 \]

Note on Change in Temperature (\(\Delta T\)): Because the size of the degree is the same for Celsius and Kelvin, a temperature difference is numerically identical in both scales: \[ \Delta T_C = \Delta T_K \] However, for Fahrenheit: \[ \Delta T_F = \frac{9}{5}\Delta T_C \]
