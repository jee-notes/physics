Fluid

The assumption model of fluid as an entity.

What "Fluid" Means?

In physics, a fluid is not just a substance (like water or air) — it is a mathematical model used to describe matter when it exhibits the ability to flow. This model is built on the realization that properties like "liquid" or "gas" are emergent. So our analytical approach will be "telescopic". We will talk about microscopic behaviours (and properties) and macroscopic behaviours (and properties).

At the microscopic level, matter consists of discrete molecules separated by vast empty spaces. However, tracking \(10^{23}\) individual trajectories is neither practical nor necessary. Instead, we adopt the continuum approximation, where matter is assumed to be continuously distributed throughout space, filling every "hole" in its volume.

This approximation works because, at macroscopic scales, even a "tiny" region of fluid contains an enormous number of molecules. This allows us to define physical quantities at a point by averaging the properties of the molecules within a representative tiny volume around it.

A "point" in fluid mechanics is not truly infinitesimal in the Dirac sense—it is a representative fluid parcel. It is large enough to contain enough molecules for statistical averages to be stable, yet small enough to be treated as a point coordinate \((x, y, z)\) for calculus.

Under this framework, quantities such as density and pressure cease to be about individual particle collisions and instead become smoothly varying functions of position.

To describe these variations, we use different differential elements. Depending on what we are measuring, we choose the most relevant "slice" of the bulk:

  • Point (Volume element): A differential fluid element is a cuboid of sides \(dx, dy, dz\) where the volume \(dV \to 0\). Used for defining density.
  • Line element: A differential length \(dx\) where \(dx \to 0\). Used for calculating pressure gradients along a specific path.
  • Area element: A differential surface \(dA = dx \cdot dy\). Used for calculating force distribution (pressure) across an interface.

This leads us to think of a fluid not as a collection of particles, but as a field of properties spread across space.

What is a "Field"?

A field is a way of mapping a physical quantity to every coordinate in space (and time). In fluid mechanics, we stop asking "Where is this particle going?" and start asking "What is the value of the property at this coordinate?"

Mathematically, we treat these properties as continuous, differentiable functions of position:

\[ P = P(x, y, z, t) \]

For a fluid at rest (statics), the time component \(t\) is constant, and the field depends only on spatial coordinates. For example, in a swimming pool, the pressure field varies only with depth (\(h\)):

\[ P(h) = P_{atm} + \rho gh \]
A field is not a substance—it is a way of describing how a physical quantity is distributed in space. It allows us to apply the full power of vector calculus to bulk matter.

Analogy: Stack of Solid Blocks

To visualize how a field arises from discrete matter, consider a stack of identical solid blocks. Each block has a mass \(m\) and area \(A\). The \(n^{th}\) block from the top feels a downward force \(F = n \cdot mg\).

  • The force is a normal contact force acting at the interface.
  • The force is discrete: it only changes when you cross from one block to the next.

Now, imagine the "Fluid Limit": increase the number of blocks to infinity (\(N \to \infty\)) while decreasing the thickness of each block to near-zero (\(dy \to 0\)).

In this limit, the discrete steps of force disappear. The stack transforms into a continuum where:

  • In solids: Forces act only at the specific physical interfaces of the blocks.
  • In fluids: Because every "point" is part of the continuum, internal compressive forces exist at every possible imaginary surface you could draw inside the fluid.
Pressure is the continuous limit of contact force—it is how internal forces exist when matter is treated as a continuum.
\[ P = \lim_{\Delta A \to 0} \frac{F_{\perp}}{\Delta A} \]

Why Only Normal Forces?

The core distinction between the "Solid Block" model and the "Fluid" model lies in rigidity. A solid block can be pushed sideways; it will tilt slightly but stay in one piece because it resists shear stress.

A fluid, by definition, cannot sustain shear stress at rest. If you apply even a microscopic tangential force to a fluid surface, the layers will simply slide past each other indefinitely.

Consequently, for a fluid to be at static equilibrium (at rest), the net tangential force on any internal surface must be zero.

Pressure is not just force per area—it is specifically the normal component of internal force. In a static fluid, any force exerted by or on the fluid must be perpendicular to the surface.

Fluids: Liquids and Gases

While both are fluids, their bulk behavior under the continuum model differs due to their molecular "cohesion":

  • Liquids: Possess enough intermolecular attraction to maintain a definite volume. This allows them to form a free surface—a boundary where the liquid ends and another medium (like air) begins.
  • Gases: Lack sufficient attraction to maintain volume. They expand to fill their container, meaning they do not form a free surface under normal conditions.
Liquids can sustain a free surface because they have a definite volume.
Gases expand to fill space, so they do not form a free surface.

A free surface is unique because its shape is not "forced" by a container. Instead, it is "free" to respond to the balance of gravity and internal pressure.

The Ideal Fluid Model

To simplify the complex behavior of real bulk matter (like the "stickiness" of honey or the compressibility of air), most exams follow the Ideal Fluid model. An ideal fluid is not a real substance — it is a mathematical model that majorly helps isolate the role of pressure and motion without complications. This model is the fluid counterpart to the "Rigid Body" model used in solid mechanics.

  • No Viscosity (Inviscid): Zero internal friction. Layers slide past each other without energy loss via dissipation. In a state of rest, this is naturally satisfied; in motion, it implies no tangential drag between fluid layers.
  • Incompressible: The volume of any fluid element remains fixed regardless of the pressure applied. Mathematically, this implies the Bulk Modulus (\(B\)) is infinite.
    \[ B = -V \frac{dP}{dV} \to \infty \implies dV = 0 \]
  • No Surface Tension: Surface effects and the energy associated with the interface are neglected for general dynamics.

Comparison with Ideal Solids

In an Ideal Rigid Body, all elastic moduli are infinite (\(Y \to \infty, \eta \to \infty, B \to \infty\)) because it resists all forms of deformation: linear, shear, and volumetric.

In an Ideal Fluid, only the Bulk Modulus is infinite (\(B \to \infty\)). It perfectly resists volume changes but offers zero resistance to shear (\(\eta = 0\)).

Incompressibility vs. Uniform Density

Incompressibility does not always translate to uniform \(\rho\) across the entire fluid body. It specifically means that if we consider any differential fluid chunk of volume \(dV\), that volume \(dV\) remains invariant irrespective of changes in pressure, velocity, or time.

Since the mass \(dm\) of that specific chunk is also invariant, the density of that element \(\rho = dm/dV\) remains constant as it moves through the field. However, this does not prevent different elements at different spatial coordinates from having different initial densities.

Example: In hydrostatics, we can have an ideal fluid with non-uniform density, such as a stratified liquid where density is a function of depth, yet each individual layer remains incompressible.

Mechanical Insight: The assumptions of inviscid and incompressibility are prominent and obvious in hydrodynamics, but in hydrostatics, their use seems subtle.
This is mostly because the role of pressure for a liquid in motion is different as compared to a liquid at rest. Pressure is an internal force. In Newtonian mechanics, we study "ideal" systems where the work done by internal constraint forces (like the normal force between rigid bodies, tension in an inextensible string, or static friction) is zero.
We want to achieve something similar for Pressure in hydrodynamics. By assuming an inviscid fluid, we ensure that internal forces do no work through dissipation, allowing us to apply Energy Conservation (Bernoulli).
In hydrostatics, since there is no displacement, the "inviscid" nature resembles static friction—it doesn't perform work regardless, making the assumption less critical.
The same applies to incompressibility. While we can have density as a spatial field in a hydrostatic ideal fluid (stratification), having density as a field for a dynamic moving fluid often moves it out of the "ideal" classification for standard analysis.
