Pressure

Internal Force in a continuum.

Pressure in a Fluid

In an Ideal Fluid (inviscid), the bulk matter can only exert a force perpendicular to any surface in contact with it, such as container walls or a body immersed in the fluid.

The "Grip" Test: If you can't "grip" it, you can't "pull" it. Since an ideal fluid has zero internal friction (viscosity), it has zero "grip." It can only "thump" into a surface, producing Normal Force.

Notice the emphasis on perpendicular. Because fluids (at rest or ideal) cannot sustain tangential forces, we define a specific physical quantity to describe this normal "push": Pressure.

To visualize this as a field property, imagine an arbitrary imaginary surface inside fluid bulk at rest. This surface is surrounded by fluid on both sides. For the bulk to remain in equilibrium, the surrounding fluid must exert equal and opposite normal forces on this surface.

Definition at a Point

Consider a small surface element of area \( dA \) around a point in ideal fluid. As we shrink this area (\( dA \to 0 \)), the normal force exerted by the surrounding fluid is \( F \). As \( dA \) is infinitesimal, the force is uniform (or at a point):

\[ P = \frac{F}{dA} \]

However, for a generic definition (applicable to all cases - specially for real fluids in motion where tangential forces like drag may exist), we isolate only the component of force \( F \) that is normal to the surface:

\[ P = \lim_{dA \to 0} \frac{F_{\perp}}{dA} \]
Mechanical Logic: By defining pressure using \( F_{\perp} \), we ensure that Pressure remains a scalar quantity. While the force vector depends on the orientation of the surface, the pressure value at a specific point in the bulk is isotropic—it is the same in all directions.

If pressure is uniform over a flat surface:

\[ \int dF = P \int dA \]

Thus, knowing pressure only the component of normal force can be determined. It is similar to knowing \( \omega \), only the component of velocity perpendicular to \( \vec{r} \) can be determined.

Pressure and force are different physical quantities.
Fluid pressure acts perpendicular to any surface in the fluid, no matter how that surface is oriented. Hence pressure has no intrinsic direction of its own; it is a scalar.
A larger area experiences proportionally larger force, but the pressure remains the same.

In solids, forces are described as direct contact interactions. However, in fluids, interaction is described through pressure. This is because the actual contact area between solids is much smaller than the apparent area due to surface roughness. In fluids, the contact area is effectively the same as the measured area, leading to a well-defined pressure.

Pressure and Depth

If the weight of the fluid is neglected (or there is gravity free environment), pressure would be the same throughout the fluid. Thus gravity creates a pressure gradient inside bulk fluid.

However, when the fluid's weight is considered, pressure varies with depth. For example:

  • Atmospheric pressure decreases with altitude
  • Pressure increases as we go deeper in water

To derive this variation, we consider a fluid element of geometry that will simplify calculation. Consider a cylinder of height \( h \) and cross-sectional area \( A = \pi r^2 \).

Its volume is \( V = A\,h \), mass is \( m = \rho A\,h \), and weight is:

\[ W = \rho A\,h\,g \]

Let pressure at the top surface be \( P_0 \), and at the bottom be \( P_0 + \Delta P \).

  • Downward force on top: \( P_0 A \)
  • Upward force on bottom: \( (P_0 + \Delta P) A \)

Since the fluid is in equilibrium:

\[ (P_0 + \Delta P)A - P_0 A - \rho A h g = 0 \]

Simplifying:

\[ \frac{\Delta P}{h} = \rho g \] \[ \frac{dP}{dh} = \rho g \]

Thus, pressure increases with depth.

For two points:

\[ P_2 - P_1 = \rho g (y_2 - y_1) \]
Hydrostatic Pressure Equation
\[ P = P_0 + \rho g h \]
Pressure increases with depth because each layer of fluid supports the weight of the fluid above it.
For an ideal incompressible fluid, density is independent of pressure.
Even though pressure varies with depth, the density remains constant throughout the fluid.

Atmospheric and Gauge Pressure

We live at the bottom of a vast ocean of air. The column of air above us exerts a pressure called atmospheric pressure.

\[ 1 \, \text{atm} = 1.013 \times 10^5 \, \text{Pa} \]
Gauge Pressure
\[ P_g = P - P_a \]

Gauge pressure measures pressure relative to atmospheric pressure. Most instruments (like tire gauges) measure gauge pressure.

Properties of Pressure

Pressure in a fluid at rest has certain fundamental properties that arise from the equilibrium of a continuum.

These properties are all consequences of a single condition:

A fluid at rest cannot sustain shear stress. Hence, all internal forces must be normal and balanced.

From this, the following properties of pressure emerge:

1. Pressure is isotropic 2. Pressure in accelerated container 3. Force is always perpendicular to surface 4. Pressure Walk Trick 5. Hydrostatic paradox
