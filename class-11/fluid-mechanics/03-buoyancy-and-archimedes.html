Buoyancy and Archimedes' Principle

Understanding exactly why massive ships float and dense coins sink.

Buoyant Force

When an object is submerged in a fluid, fluid pressure pushes deeply against all of its surfaces. Because fluid pressure monotonically increases with depth, the upward force violently pushing on the bottom of the object is always strictly greater than the downward force softly pushing on its top.

This continuous imbalance of vertical pressure produces a net upward force called the Buoyant Force (\( F_B \)).

Archimedes' Principle

The exact magnitude of this buoyant force was elegantly deduced by the ancient Greek mathematician Archimedes.

Archimedes' Principle
When a body is partially or completely immersed in a fluid at rest, it constantly experiences an upward buoyant force that is exactly equal to the weight of the fluid it actively displaces.
Magnitude of Buoyant Force

\[ F_B = m_{\text{fluid-displaced}} \cdot g = \rho_{\text{fluid}} V_{\text{submerged}} g \]
Where:
  • \( \rho_{\text{fluid}} \) is the density of the fluid.
  • \( V_{\text{submerged}} \) is the volume of the fluid displaced (equal to the volume of the immersed part of the object).
  • \( g \) is the acceleration due to gravity.
Notice how the nature, shape, or mass of the submerged object itself completely disappears from the formula. The buoyant force depends only on the properties of the fluid and the volume of the hole cut into it by the object.

Laws of Floatation

Whether an object ultimately floats, suspends, or sinks is determined by the tug-of-war between its true gravity weight (\( W \)) and the maximum possible fluid buoyancy (\( F_B \)).

  • Sinking (\( W > F_B \)): If the object is denser than the fluid (\( \rho_{\text{obj}} > \rho_{\text{fluid}} \)), its maximum buoyant force is eclipsed by its weight. It accelerates downward to the bottom.
  • Neutral Suspension (\( W = F_B \)): If the object's density exactly matches the fluid density (\( \rho_{\text{obj}} = \rho_{\text{fluid}} \)), it will remain suspended natively in equilibrium wherever it is completely immersed.
  • Floating (\( W = F_B \) but partially submerged): If the object is less dense than the fluid (\( \rho_{\text{obj}} < \rho_{\text{fluid}} \)), it cannot fully submerge because the buoyant force would exceed its weight. It rises until enough volume exits the fluid that the displaced fluid weight exactly counters its own weight.
Fraction of Volume Submerged
For a floating object, equating weight to buoyant force \( (\rho_{\text{obj}} V_{\text{total}} g = \rho_{\text{fluid}} V_{\text{submerged}} g) \) yields:

\[ \frac{V_{\text{submerged}}}{V_{\text{total}}} = \frac{\rho_{\text{obj}}}{\rho_{\text{fluid}}} \]

Apparent Weight

When an object is immersed, the upward buoyant force natively counteracts a portion of its true downward weight. This makes the object feel lighter. We define this effective weight as the Apparent Weight.

Apparent Weight (\( W_{\text{app}} \))

\[ W_{\text{app}} = W_{\text{true}} - F_B = ( \rho_{\text{obj}} - \rho_{\text{fluid}} ) V g \]

If the object floats, its true weight is absolutely balanced by buoyancy, rendering its apparent weight strictly zero within the fluid frame!
