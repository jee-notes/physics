Fluid Mechanics

The study of fluids at rest and in motion, exploring the macroscopic behavior arising from microscopic interactions.

Conceptual Overview

Matter essentially exists in three states: solid, liquid, and gas. Solids maintain a definite shape and volume. Liquids and gases, however, behave quite differently. They flow under the action of applied forces and take the shape of their container. Because of their ability to flow, liquids and gases are jointly called fluids. Fluid Mechanics serves as a vital bridge between classical mechanics and thermodynamics, dealing with the macroscopic properties of these continuous media.

While solid mechanics focuses on rigid bodies and point masses, fluid mechanics studies a continuum—a continuous distribution of matter where properties vary continuously from point to point in space.

This chapter is primarily divided into two large themes:

  • Fluid Statics: The study of fluids at rest. Key concepts like pressure variation with depth, Pascal's Principle, and Archimedes' Principles fall here. Forces exist in static fluids, but they operate primarily perpendicularly to surfaces as there are no tangential forces (shear stresses) without motion.
  • Fluid Dynamics: The study of fluids in motion. By treating the fluid as passing through space, sweeping out volumes over time, we harness conservation laws: Conservation of Mass (Equation of Continuity) and Conservation of Energy (Bernoulli's Principle).

We then move to real fluid properties—Viscosity and Surface Tension. Viscosity introduces internal friction between fluid layers, turning Bernoulli's idealizations into more realistic Poiseuille flows. Surface tension reveals the unique forces acting at the liquid's free surface, dominating phenomena such as droplet formation and capillarity at small scales.
