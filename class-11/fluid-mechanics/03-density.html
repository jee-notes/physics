Density

Foundational quantity used to describe matter in continuum mechanics.

Density

An important property of any material is its density, defined as mass per unit volume.

For a homogeneous material (such as ice or iron), density remains the same throughout the material.

\[ \rho = \frac{M}{V} \]
  • SI Unit: \( \text{kg m}^{-3} \)
  • Dimensions: \( [\text{M}^1 \text{L}^{-3} \text{T}^0] \)

Unit Conversions

  • CGS Unit: \( \text{g cm}^{-3} \) (also written as g/cc)
  • 1 \( \text{g cm}^{-3} \) = 1000 \( \text{kg m}^{-3} \)
  • 1 \( \text{cm}^{3} \) = 1 \( \text{cc} \) = 1 \( \text{mL} \)
  • 1 \( \text{L} \) = 1000 \( \text{mL} \) = 1000 \( \text{cc} \)
  • 1 \( \text{L} \) = 1 \( \text{dm}^{3} \)

Density can be defined for solids as well as liquids as well as gases, as long as their volume can be specified.

Why Density is Defined

Density connects two fundamental physical quantities: mass and volume (space).

Two objects made of the same material (substance) may have different masses and volumes, but they will have the same density because the ratio \( \frac{M}{V} \) remains constant.

Thus, if the material (and hence density) is known, knowing either mass or volume is sufficient to determine the other.

Density is a material property — it characterizes the substance, not the size of the object.
Because a fluid is treated as a continuous medium, it is often more useful to describe its mass purely in terms of its distribution over space rather than the total mass of the object.

Measurement and Applications

Measuring density is an important analytical technique in physics and chemistry.

For example, the state of charge of a lead battery can be determined by measuring the density of its electrolyte (sulfuric acid solution).

As the battery discharges, sulfuric acid reacts with lead plates to form lead sulfate, reducing the concentration of the solution. As a result, the density decreases:

  • Fully charged: ~1.30 × 103 kg/m3
  • Discharged: ~1.15 × 103 kg/m3

Dependence on Conditions

Density depends not only on the material but also on external conditions such as temperature and pressure.

However, for most solids and liquids, this variation is small and is often neglected unless high precision is required.

Density is approximately constant for a given material under ordinary conditions.

Density as a Field

In fluid mechanics, density is treated as a quantity defined at every point in space:

\[ \rho = \rho(x, y, z) \]

This allows us to describe how mass is distributed continuously throughout the fluid.

In continuum mechanics, density is not just a property of an object, but a field defined over space.

Relative Density (Specific Gravity)

Relative density of a substance is defined as the ratio of its density to the density of a reference substance.

\[ \text{Relative Density} = \frac{\rho_{\text{substance}}}{\rho_{\text{reference}}} \]

Specific gravity of a substance is defined as the relative density of the substance with reference to the density of water at \( 4^{\circ}\text{C} \) (which is exactly \( 10^3 \, \text{kg m}^{-3} \)).

Specific Gravity (Relative Density)
Specific gravity is the ratio of the density of a substance to the density of water at \( 4^{\circ}\text{C} \) (which is exactly \( 10^3 \, \text{kg m}^{-3} \)).

\[ \text{Specific Gravity} = \frac{\rho_{\text{substance}}}{\rho_{\text{water}}} \] SI Unit: None (it is a dimensionless ratio).
Dimensional Formula: \( [\text{M}^0 \text{L}^0 \text{T}^0] \)

Average Density of an Object

For a real object, especially one that may have cavities or non-uniform structure, we define its average density as:

\[ \rho_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\text{Total mass of object}}{\text{Total (external) volume of object}} \]

This definition considers the object as a whole, without worrying about how mass is distributed inside it.

Example: Hollow Object

Consider a hollow spherical object of outer radius \( R_2 \) and inner radius \( R_1 \), with total mass \( M \).

Density of the material (substance):

\[ \rho_{\text{substance}} = \frac{M}{\frac{4}{3}\pi (R_2^3 - R_1^3)} \]

Average density of the object:

\[ \rho_{\text{avg}} = \frac{M}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R_2^3} \]

Since the outer volume is larger than the actual material volume:

\[ \rho_{\text{avg}} \le \rho_{\text{substance}} \]
Average density depends on the overall size and shape of the object, while density of substance depends only on the material.

Key Idea

  • Density of substance: property of material
  • Average density: property of the object (may include empty space)
An object can be made of a dense material but still have low average density if it contains empty space (e.g., hollow objects, porous materials).
