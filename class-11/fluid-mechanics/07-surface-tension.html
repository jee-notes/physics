Surface Tension

The study of the "stretchy skin" of liquids arising from uncompensated molecular forces.

Contact Forces of Liquids

So far, we have studied two types of contact forces exerted by fluids:

  • Buoyant Force
  • Viscous Force

Although both forces are exerted by the same liquid, they are different types of contact force.

This is similar to normal force and friction, which are different types of contact force between the same solid surfaces.

These forces are characterised by different physical properties of the fluid.

The physical property density \(\rho\) determines the buoyant force, whereas the physical property coefficient of viscosity \(\eta\) determines the viscous force.

Both buoyancy and viscosity occur in fluids i.e. liquids as well as gases.

We now introduce a third type of contact force: surface tension. It is characteristic of only liquid surfaces and provides an important distinction between the behaviour of liquids and gases.

It is because of surface tension that a liquid surface tends to contract, allowing the liquid to maintain a definite volume while gases expand to fill their containers.

Surface Tension and its Molecular Origin

We first try to understand what surface tension is by examining some natural phenomena that demonstrate its effects. We will then explain the mechanism of surface tension using the molecular structure of a liquid.

The liquid surface is the boundary that encloses the bulk volume of the liquid, much like how our skin encloses our body.

This boundary behaves like a stretched membrane, similar to the surface of a filled balloon or a taut string. The property responsible for this behaviour is called surface tension.

It depends on the nature of the liquid and its physical conditions.

Physical Manifestations

  • Support at the Surface: An object less dense than water, such as an air-filled ball, floats due to buoyancy. However, even a dense object such as a paper clip can remain supported on the water surface if placed gently. The liquid surface behaves like a stretched membrane, and surface tension can support small loads. This is also how certain insects can stand and move on the water surface.
  • Shape of Liquid Drops: Small, freely falling liquid droplets tend to be spherical because a sphere has the minimum surface area for a given volume. This tendency arises from surface tension, which favours a reduction in surface area.
  • Detergents and Heating: Substances such as soap and detergent reduce the surface tension of water. This helps water spread over surfaces and penetrate small spaces, making cleaning more effective. Increasing the temperature of water generally reduces its surface tension as well, while dissolved impurities can also affect its value.

Molecular Origin

Deep inside the bulk of a liquid, a molecule is surrounded uniformly by neighbouring molecules. The attractive cohesive forces act in different directions and balance one another, so the molecule experiences no net cohesive force in the bulk region.

However, a molecule at the surface region experiences an asymmetric environment. There are no liquid molecules above the free surface, while molecules below and around it continue to exert attractive forces.

As a result, surface molecules experience a net inward cohesive pull towards the bulk of the liquid.

Bringing a molecule from the interior bulk region to the surface region requires work because the molecular environment at the surface is less energetically favourable.
Consequently, creating additional liquid surface requires energy.
To minimise its total energy, a liquid tends to minimise its surface area.

This tendency to reduce surface area explains why a liquid surface behaves like a stretched membrane under tension and tends to contract.

However, the membrane is only an analogy. There is no separate physical skin covering the liquid that can be torn away from it. The surface consists of the same liquid molecules as the bulk. Its membrane-like behaviour arises from the different molecular interactions at the surface, where cohesive forces are unbalanced.

Surface Tension

To describe surface tension quantitatively, we first examine what is observed experimentally and how the force associated with a liquid surface is defined.

First, we consider a free surface of a liquid, which is in contact with air or vacuum. Surface tension originates from the unbalanced intermolecular cohesive forces experienced by molecules in the surface region.

A liquid surface in contact with a solid container wall involves additional intermolecular interactions between the liquid and the solid. These interactions influence the behaviour of the liquid near the wall and will be important when we study contact angle and capillary action.

Second, just as pressure describes a normal force per unit area, surface tension describes a tangential pulling force per unit length. The force acts in the plane of the liquid surface and perpendicular to the line under consideration.

Definition: Surface tension \(S\) is the tangential force per unit length acting across an imaginary line drawn on the liquid surface, with the force perpendicular to that line.

\[ \boxed{S=\frac{F}{L}} \]

Here, \(F\) is the magnitude of the force acting across the imaginary line of length \(L\).

  • SI Unit: \(\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\)

Intensive Property

The force observed at a liquid surface depends on how much of the surface is involved.

If we draw a longer line on the surface, the total force acting along that line increases proportionally. This is similar to area and pressure.

Hence we define surface tension as : \(S = \frac{F}{L}\) to remove the dependence on length and give a quantity that depends only on the nature of the liquid.

Surface tension is not a force — it is a material property that determines how much force per unit length the surface can exert.

Surface tension is characteristic of the liquid at a given temperature. Its value also depends on the purity of the liquid.

For water under ordinary conditions, surface tension is approximately \(0.075\,\text{N/m}\). This means that a 1 m contact line can exert a surface-tension force of about \(0.075\,\text{N}\).

For a thin wire resting on the surface, surface tension can act along the contact lines on either side of the wire.

Role of Scale

The importance of surface tension depends strongly on the size of the system.

As the size decreases, the surface-to-volume ratio increases. Thus, surface effects become dominant at small scales (such as droplets and capillaries).

For large volumes of fluid, this ratio is small, and surface tension becomes negligible compared to pressure forces.

This is why surface tension was not significant in earlier topics of bulk fluid statics and dynamics.

Surface Energy

Equivalently, surface tension can be interpreted as energy.

Surface Energy
Surface tension is equal to the work done per unit increase in surface area:
\[ S = \frac{W}{\Delta A} \] SI Unit: \( \text{J m}^{-2} \)
Surface tension acts tangentially along the surface and always acts to reduce the surface area.

Angle of Contact: The Boundary Force Balance

The origin of the Angle of Contact is identical to the origin of surface tension: it is the macroscopic result of unbalanced intermolecular forces. To understand why it exists, we compare the environment of a surface molecule at the center of a tank versus one at the wall.

Case 1: Molecule at the Center (Standard Surface Tension)

Consider a water molecule at the center of the top free surface. It is surrounded by liquid molecules only from below and the sides. It experiences a net attractive pull toward the liquid bulk.

Because the pull is symmetric horizontally but unbalanced vertically, the net force is directed vertically downward. For the fluid to be at rest, the surface must stay horizontal (perpendicular to the net downward force).

Case 2: Molecule Near the Solid Wall (Contact Angle)

Now consider a molecule of the same surface, but situated right next to the solid wall of the tank. This molecule is now caught between two different bulks:

  • Liquid Bulk: Pulls the molecule toward the interior of the liquid.
  • Solid Bulk: Pulls the molecule toward the wall.

Because the molecule is pulled by both the liquid and the solid, the net unbalanced force is no longer vertically downward. It is tilted at an angle that depends on the relative strength of these two bulk attractions.

The Perpendicular Rule: Since a static fluid cannot sustain shear stress, the free surface must always align itself perpendicular to the net resultant force acting on its molecules.

The Angle of Contact (\( \theta \)) is simply the geometric orientation the liquid must adopt so that its surface is perpendicular to this lopsided resultant force at the boundary.

  • Acute Angle (\( \theta \lt 90^\circ \)): If the pull from the solid is very strong, the net force vector tilts toward the wall. To stay perpendicular, the liquid climbs the wall, forming a concave meniscus.
  • Obtuse Angle (\( \theta > 90^\circ \)): If the pull from the liquid bulk is dominant, the net force vector tilts into the liquid. To stay perpendicular, the liquid depresses away from the wall, forming a convex meniscus.
Definition: The angle of contact is measured inside the liquid between the solid surface and the tangent to the liquid surface at the point of contact.
