Surface Tension

The study of the "stretchy skin" of liquids arising from uncompensated molecular forces.

Molecular Theory of Surface Tension

Deep inside the bulk of a liquid, a molecule is surrounded completely by other molecules. It experiences attractive cohesive forces equally from all directions, so the net force on it is strictly zero.

However, a molecule situated at or very near the free surface of the liquid is in a totally different environment. There are no liquid molecules violently pulling it upward, but there are many pulling it downward and sideways. Consequently, surface molecules experience a net inward cohesive force pulling them back into the bulk.

To bring a molecule from the bulk to the surface, work must be forcefully done against this inward pull. As a result, surface molecules possess enhanced potential energy. Because all physical systems strive to minimize their potential energy, liquids naturally try to minimize their exposed surface area. This is why freely falling water drops assume a perfectly spherical shape—a sphere has the absolute minimum surface area for a given volume.

Surface Energy and Surface Tension

Because the free surface of a liquid behaves like a stretched elastic membrane trying to endlessly contract, it exerts a tension force across any imaginary line drawn on the surface.

Surface Tension (\( S \) or \( \sigma \))

Surface tension is defined as the force \( F \) acting per unit length \( L \) on either side of an imaginary line drawn on the free surface of a liquid, perpendicular to the line and tangential to the surface.

\[ S = \frac{F}{L} \] SI Unit: \( \text{N m}^{-1} \)
Dimensional Formula: \( [\text{M}^1 \text{L}^0 \text{T}^{-2}] \)

Equivalently, it can be defined thermodynamically through energy.

Surface Energy

Surface tension is also exactly equal to the work done (or energy stored) per unit increase in the surface area of the liquid under isothermal conditions.

\[ S = \frac{W}{\Delta A} \] SI Unit: \( \text{J m}^{-2} \)

Angle of Contact

When a liquid interface meets a solid surface, it relies on a fierce tug-of-war between two different intermolecular forces:

  1. Cohesive Force: The attraction strictly between molecules of the same substance (liquid-liquid).
  2. Adhesive Force: The attraction structurally between molecules of entirely different substances (liquid-solid).
Angle of Contact (\( \theta \))
The angle inside the liquid between the solid surface and the tangent drawn to the liquid meniscus at the point of contact.

If adhesive forces dominate (like water on completely clean glass), the liquid intensely "wets" the solid, the meniscus curves upward (concave), and the angle of contact is strictly acute (\( \theta < 90^{\circ} \)).

If cohesive forces dominate (like mercury on glass or water on totally waxed paper), the liquid aggressively avoids wetting the solid, the meniscus curves downward (convex), and the angle of contact is strictly obtuse (\( \theta > 90^{\circ} \)).
