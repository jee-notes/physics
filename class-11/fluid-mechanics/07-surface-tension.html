Surface Tension

The study of the "stretchy skin" of liquids arising from uncompensated molecular forces.

Molecular Origin of Surface Tension

Deep inside the bulk of a liquid, a molecule is surrounded uniformly by neighboring molecules. The attractive cohesive forces act equally in all directions, resulting in zero net force.

However, a molecule at the free surface experiences an asymmetric environment. There are no neighboring molecules above it, while molecules below and around it continue to exert attractive forces. As a result, surface molecules experience a net inward pull toward the bulk.

Bringing a molecule from the interior to the surface requires work against this inward pull. Therefore, surface molecules possess higher potential energy.
To minimize total energy, the liquid tends to minimize its surface area.

This is why a liquid surface behaves like a stretched membrane under tension, always tending to contract.

Surface Tension and Surface Energy

Surface Tension (\( S \) or \( \sigma \))
Surface tension is defined as the tangential force per unit length acting along an imaginary line drawn on the liquid surface, perpendicular to the line. \[ S = \frac{F}{L} \] SI Unit: \( \text{N m}^{-1} \)

Equivalently, surface tension can be interpreted as energy.

Surface Energy
Surface tension is equal to the work done per unit increase in surface area:
\[ S = \frac{W}{\Delta A} \] SI Unit: \( \text{J m}^{-2} \)
Surface tension acts tangentially along the surface and always acts to reduce the surface area.

Why Define Surface Tension as \( \frac{F}{L} \)?

The force observed at a liquid surface depends on how much of the surface is involved. If we draw a longer line on the surface, the total force acting along that line increases proportionally.

To isolate a property of the material itself, independent of the chosen geometry, we define:

\[ S = \frac{F}{L} \]

This removes the dependence on length and gives a quantity that depends only on the nature of the liquid and its physical conditions (such as temperature and impurities).

Surface tension is not a force — it is a material property that determines how much force per unit length the surface can exert.

This is analogous to density:

\[ \rho = \frac{m}{V} \]

While mass depends on the size of the system, density does not. Similarly, the total surface force depends on the length considered, but surface tension does not.

Thus, \(F\) is an extensive quantity (depends on size), while \(S\) is an intensive property (intrinsic to the material).

Physical Manifestations

  • Support at Surface:
    An object less dense than water (like an air-filled ball) floats due to buoyancy. However, even a dense object such as a paper clip can rest on the water surface if placed gently. This occurs because the liquid surface behaves like a stretched membrane that can support small loads.
  • Shape of Liquid Drops:
    Freely falling droplets are spherical because a sphere has the minimum surface area for a given volume.
  • Effect of Temperature and Impurities:
    Increasing temperature or adding substances like soap reduces surface tension. This makes it easier for water to spread and penetrate small spaces, which is why hot, soapy water cleans more effectively.

Role of Scale

The importance of surface tension depends strongly on the size of the system.

For a spherical drop of radius \( r \): \[ \text{Surface Area} = 4\pi r^2, \quad \text{Volume} = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \] \[ \frac{\text{Surface Area}}{\text{Volume}} = \frac{3}{r} \]

As the size decreases, the surface-to-volume ratio increases. Thus, surface effects become dominant at small scales (such as droplets and capillaries).

For large volumes of fluid, this ratio is small, and surface tension becomes negligible compared to pressure forces. This is why surface tension was not significant in earlier topics of bulk fluid statics and dynamics.

Angle of Contact

When a liquid comes in contact with a solid surface, the shape of the interface is determined by the competition between two intermolecular interactions:

  1. Cohesive Forces: Attraction between molecules of the liquid.
  2. Adhesive Forces: Attraction between liquid molecules and the solid surface.
Angle of Contact (\( \theta \))
The angle inside the liquid between the solid surface and the tangent to the liquid surface at the point of contact.

If adhesive forces dominate, the liquid tends to spread over the solid surface. The meniscus becomes concave, and the angle of contact is acute (\( \theta < 90^\circ \)).

If cohesive forces dominate, the liquid tends to contract away from the surface. The meniscus becomes convex, and the angle of contact is obtuse (\( \theta > 90^\circ \)).

The angle of contact is not just a geometric quantity—it encodes the balance of intermolecular forces at the interface.

Connection to Capillary Rise

Surface tension acts tangentially along the liquid surface. Near the wall of a container or tube, this force acts along the tangent to the meniscus.

Since the surface is inclined at an angle \( \theta \), the surface tension force has a vertical component.

Vertical component of surface tension per unit length: \[ S \cos\theta \]

This vertical component is responsible for pulling the liquid upward (or downward) along the surface.

Surface tension provides the force, while the angle of contact determines how much of that force acts vertically.

If \( \theta \lt 90^\circ \), then \( \cos\theta > 0 \), and the vertical component is upward → liquid rises (capillary rise).

If \( \theta > 90^\circ \), then \( \cos\theta \lt 0 \), and the vertical component is downward → liquid falls (capillary depression).
