Capillarity

The physics explaining how plants draw water and towels soak up spills.

Capillary Rise (Ascent Formula)

When a long tube of very fine bore (a capillary tube) is vertically dipped into a liquid, the liquid spontaneously rises or falls inside the tube relative to the surrounding level. This phenomenon is known as capillarity.

Why does it happen? If a liquid wets the glass (like water, generating a concave meniscus), the pressure just beneath the curved surface is strictly less than atmospheric pressure by precisely \( \frac{2S}{R} \) (where \( R \) is the radius of curvature of the meniscus). Consequently, the fluid natively accelerates up the tube until the hydrostatic weight of the rising liquid column aggressively balances this pressure discrepancy.

By relating the meniscus radius \( R \) to the actual tube radius \( r \) via the angle of contact \( \theta \) (\( r = R \cos \theta \)), we uniquely derive the Ascent Formula.

The Ascent Formula

The vertical height \( h \) to which a liquid of density \( \rho \) and surface tension \( S \) natively rises in a capillary tube of internal radius \( r \) is given by:

\[ h = \frac{2 S \cos \theta}{r \rho g} \]
Where:
  • \( S \) is the surface tension of the liquid.
  • \( \theta \) is the angle of contact.
  • \( r \) is the radius of the capillary tube.
  • \( \rho \) is the fluid density.
  • \( g \) is the native acceleration due to gravity.
If the angle of contact is strictly obtuse (\( \theta > 90^{\circ} \)), \( \cos \theta \) becomes aggressively negative, making \( h \) negative. This conclusively proves why non-wetting liquids like massive mercury suffer capillary depression (falling below the surrounding surface level).

Factors Affecting Capillarity

Based exactly on the Ascent Formula, we can deduce several physical dependencies:

  • Tube Radius (\( r \)): The rise is strictly inversely proportional to the tube radius (\( h \propto \frac{1}{r} \)). The impossibly thinner the tube, the colossally higher the liquid climbs.
  • Temperature: As ambient temperature climbs naturally, surface tension \( S \) definitively falls. Hence, capillary rise drops organically.
  • Impure Solutes: Highly soluble impurities (like rigid salt) explicitly increase surface tension and capillary rise, whereas insoluble impurities (like gentle detergent) drastically lower them.

Capillary Tube of Insufficient Length

What physically happens if the maximum theoretical height \( h \) is mathematically \( 10 \, \text{cm} \), but you dramatically cut the tube so it is only physically \( 5 \, \text{cm} \) long? Will the liquid erupt out the top like a glorious fountain?

No. The liquid emphatically does not overflow.

From the formula, the product \( h \times R = \frac{2S}{\rho g} = \text{Constant} \) (where \( R = \frac{r}{\cos \theta} \) is the meniscus curvature). If the tube artificially abruptly ends at a height \( h' < h \), the fluid violently reaches the top and physically pauses. To explicitly balance the equation, the meniscus wildly adjusts its radius of curvature \( R \), flattening out until it discovers a new equilibrium state. It will never overflow.
