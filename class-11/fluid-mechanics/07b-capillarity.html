Capillarity

The physics explaining how plants draw water and towels soak up spills.

Angle of Contact

The same intermolecular imbalance responsible for surface tension also determines how a liquid meets a solid surface.

At the line of contact, two types of forces compete:

  • Cohesive force (\(F_l\)): Attraction between molecules of the liquid.
  • Adhesive force (\(F_s\)): Attraction between molecules of the liquid and the solid.

The resultant of these forces determines the orientation of the liquid surface near the solid. The free surface of the liquid rests in such a way that the surface is perpendicular to this resultant.

Depending on the relative magnitudes of these forces:

  • \(F_l > F_s\): Cohesion dominates → liquid pulls inward → surface becomes convex → liquid is depressed near the solid.
  • \(F_l \lt F_s\): Adhesion dominates → liquid spreads along solid → surface becomes concave → liquid rises near the solid.
  • \(F_l = F_s\): No preference → surface remains flat.

The angle of contact (\(\theta\)) is defined as the angle between:

  • the tangent to the liquid surface at the point of contact, and
  • the solid surface,

measured inside the liquid. The tangent to the solid surface is to be drawn towards the liquid and the tangent to the liquid is to be drawn away from the solid.

It is a property of the liquid–solid pair and quantifies the tendency of a liquid to wet a surface.

A smaller contact angle means stronger adhesion (better wetting), while a larger contact angle indicates dominant cohesion (poor wetting).

For example, water on clean glass has \(\theta \approx 0^\circ\), meaning it wets the surface completely.

Derivation of the Ascent Formula

To understand the height \( h \) to which a liquid rises in a capillary tube, we can analyze the system through two complementary lenses: Pressure Fields (how the bulk responds to curvature) and Mechanical Equilibrium (how forces balance at the boundary).

Method 1: The Pressure-Curvature Approach (Field Perspective)

In this framework, the rise of the liquid is a bulk response to a pressure deficit created by the curved meniscus.

1. Geometry of the Meniscus:
As established by the Perpendicular Resultant Rule, the liquid surface meets the wall at an angle \(\theta\). For a tube of radius \(r\), the meniscus forms a spherical cap with a radius of curvature \(R\): \[ R = \frac{r}{\cos \theta} \]

2. The Laplace Pressure Jump:
The curvature creates a pressure discontinuity. For a concave meniscus, the pressure just below the surface (\(P_L\)) is lower than the atmospheric pressure (\(P_{atm}\)) above it: \[ P_{atm} - P_L = \frac{2S}{R} \implies P_L = P_{atm} - \frac{2S \cos \theta}{r} \]

3. Hydrostatic Balance:
Consider two points at the same horizontal level of the flat liquid surface outside the tube. The pressure at the flat surface is \(P_{atm}\). Inside the tube, at the same level, the pressure must also be \(P_{atm}\). This pressure is provided by the low-pressure bulk under the meniscus plus the weight of the raised column: \[ P_{atm} = P_L + \rho gh \] \[ P_{atm} = \left( P_{atm} - \frac{2S \cos \theta}{r} \right) + \rho gh \]

4. Final Result: \[ \rho gh = \frac{2S \cos \theta}{r} \implies h = \frac{2S \cos \theta}{r \rho g} \]

Method 2: The Force Balancing Approach (Mechanical Perspective)

This method treats surface tension as a distributed force acting along the contact line between the liquid and the solid.

1. Total Surface Tension Force:
Surface tension \(S\) acts tangentially to the liquid surface along the entire circumference of the tube (\(2\pi r\)). \[ F_{total} = S \times (2\pi r) \]

2. Vertical Component:
The horizontal components of this force cancel out due to axial symmetry. Only the vertical component contributes to supporting the weight of the liquid: \[ F_{up} = F_{total} \cos \theta = (2\pi r S) \cos \theta \]

3. Balancing the Weight:
This upward force supports the weight of the liquid column of height \(h\). Ignoring the tiny volume of the meniscus itself: \[ W = m \cdot g = (\rho \cdot \text{Volume}) \cdot g = \rho (\pi r^2 h) g \]

4. Equilibrium:
Setting \(F_{up} = W\): \[ 2\pi r S \cos \theta = \rho \pi r^2 h g \] \[ h = \frac{2S \cos \theta}{r \rho g} \]

Jurin's Law: Both methods yield the same result, proving that the height \(h\) is inversely proportional to the tube radius \(r\).

Summary of Ascent/Descent

  • If \(\theta \lt 90^\circ\) (Concave): \(\cos \theta\) is positive, \(h\) is positive \(\to\) Capillary Rise.
  • If \(\theta > 90^\circ\) (Convex): \(\cos \theta\) is negative, \(h\) is negative \(\to\) Capillary Depression.

Factors Affecting Capillarity

Based exactly on the Ascent Formula, we can deduce several physical dependencies:

  • Tube Radius (\( r \)): The rise is strictly inversely proportional to the tube radius (\( h \propto \frac{1}{r} \)). The impossibly thinner the tube, the colossally higher the liquid climbs.
  • Temperature: As ambient temperature climbs naturally, surface tension \( S \) definitively falls. Hence, capillary rise drops organically.
  • Impure Solutes: Highly soluble impurities (like rigid salt) explicitly increase surface tension and capillary rise, whereas insoluble impurities (like gentle detergent) drastically lower them.

Capillary Tube of Insufficient Length

What physically happens if the maximum theoretical height \( h \) is mathematically \( 10 \, \text{cm} \), but you dramatically cut the tube so it is only physically \( 5 \, \text{cm} \) long? Will the liquid erupt out the top like a glorious fountain?

No. The liquid emphatically does not overflow.

From the formula, the product \( h \times R = \frac{2S}{\rho g} = \text{Constant} \) (where \( R = \frac{r}{\cos \theta} \) is the meniscus curvature). If the tube artificially abruptly ends at a height \( h' < h \), the fluid violently reaches the top and physically pauses. To explicitly balance the equation, the meniscus wildly adjusts its radius of curvature \( R \), flattening out until it discovers a new equilibrium state. It will never overflow.
