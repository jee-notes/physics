Isotropy of Pressure

Isotropy comes from static equilibrium in continuum.

Pressure is Isotropic

At a given point in a fluid at rest, pressure is the same for all orientations of surfaces passing through that point.

Operational Meaning

Consider a point \( X \) inside a fluid where the pressure is \( P \).

Take a small surface element \( dA_1 \) at that point, oriented along some direction (say \( \hat{i} \)). The force exerted by the fluid on this surface is:

\[ d\vec{F}_1 = -P \, d\vec{A}_1 \]

Now take another surface element \( dA_2 \) at the same point, but oriented in a different direction (say \( \hat{j} \)):

\[ d\vec{F}_2 = -P \, d\vec{A}_2 \]

Even though the orientation of the surface changes, the same pressure \( P \) relates force and area.

Isotropy means that pressure at a point does not depend on the orientation of the surface. The same value of pressure applies in all directions.

What Changes and What Does Not

  • The direction of force changes (it is always normal to the surface)
  • The area vector changes with orientation
  • The pressure \( P \) remains the same
Changing the orientation of the surface changes the direction of force, but not the value of pressure at that point.

Why This Must Be True

If pressure were different in different directions at a point, then different surface orientations would experience different magnitudes of force. This would create an unbalanced force on a small fluid element, causing motion.

Since the fluid is at rest, such imbalance cannot exist. Hence, pressure must be the same in all directions.

This is where application of "hydrostatics" condition is understood. It means, all fluid particles are at rest with respect to each other. This condition is like a constraint (like rigid body constraint etc.). Pressure is about internal forces, and they have to be such that all particles are at rest wrt each other.
Pressure has no intrinsic direction. The direction of force depends on the orientation of the surface.

Pressure is Isotropic (Wedge Argument)

To test whether pressure depends on direction, consider a very small wedge-shaped fluid element at a point.

Forces on the Element

  • Pressure \( P_x \) acts on vertical face (area \( A_x \))
  • Pressure \( P_y \) acts on horizontal face (area \( A_y \))
  • Pressure \( P_n \) acts on inclined face (area \( A_n \))

For equilibrium, net force on the element must be zero.

Neglecting Weight (Key Step)

Pressure forces scale with area:

\[ F_P \sim P \cdot l^2 \]

Weight scales with volume:

\[ F_g = \rho g \cdot l^3 \]

Comparing:

\[ \frac{F_g}{F_P} \sim l \rightarrow 0 \]
As the element becomes infinitesimally small, weight becomes negligible compared to pressure forces. Hence, body forces can be ignored while establishing isotropy.

Force Balance

Resolving forces along x and y directions:

\[ P_x A_x = P_n A_n \cos\theta \]
\[ P_y A_y = P_n A_n \sin\theta \]

From geometry:

\[ A_x = A_n \cos\theta, \quad A_y = A_n \sin\theta \]

Substituting:

\[ P_x = P_n, \quad P_y = P_n \]

Final Result

\[ P_x = P_y = P_n = P \]
Pressure at a point is independent of direction. The same scalar pressure relates force and area for all surface orientations.
