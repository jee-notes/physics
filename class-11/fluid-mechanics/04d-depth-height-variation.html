Pressure Walk

Technique to add or subtract height component.

Pressure Walk Technique

Consider two points in a liquid:

  • \( A(x_1, y_1, z_1) \)
  • \( B(x_2, y_2, z_2) \)

Even if the points are in 3D, pressure difference depends only on displacement along \( g_{eff} \) (vertical). x and z do not matter.

Diagram

Take \( y \) positive downward. If \( B \) is deeper than \( A \), then \( y_2 > y_1 \).

Relation

\[ P_B - P_A = \rho g (y_2 - y_1) \]

Let \( h = y_2 - y_1 \)

\[ P_B = P_A + \rho g h \]

This works because pressure gradient exists only along \( g_{eff} \). No gradient exists perpendicular to it.

Pressure Walking Trick

Move between points and update pressure based on vertical displacement. The sign comes purely from direction of movement relative to \( g_{eff} \).

  • Move along \( g_{eff} \) (down) → add \( \rho g h \)
  • Move opposite to \( g_{eff} \) (up) → subtract \( \rho g h \)
  • Move perpendicular to \( g_{eff} \) → no change

The path does not matter. Only net vertical displacement matters.

Reverse

\[ P_A = P_B - \rho g h \]

Same Level

\[ y_1 = y_2 \Rightarrow P_A = P_B \]

Points at same vertical coordinate have same pressure because no component of displacement exists along \( g_{eff} \).

Multiple Liquids

The relation \( P_B - P_A = \rho g h \) is valid only when \( \rho \) is constant.

If density changes, the same idea still applies — but must be used piecewise.

  • Split the path into segments where \( \rho \) is constant
  • Apply add/subtract in each segment
  • Change \( \rho \) at each interface

\[ P = P_0 + \rho_1 g h_1 + \rho_2 g h_2 \]

Each term comes from displacement along \( g_{eff} \) within that layer.

Reverse

\[ P_A = P_B - \rho_2 g h_2 - \rho_1 g h_1 \]
