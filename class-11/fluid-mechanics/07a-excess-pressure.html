Excess Pressure

Why the pressure inside curved liquid surfaces is always higher than outside.

Curvature and Pressure Differential

Surface tension acts aggressively to shrink a liquid surface. For a highly curved surface like a sphere, the surface tension forces along the perimeter yield a net inward resultant force directed specifically toward the center of curvature.

To prevent the drop from spontaneously crushing itself to a point under this inward pull, the thermodynamic pressure strictly inside the drop must be fundamentally greater than the pressure outside. This difference is called the excess pressure.

Excess Pressure Inside a Liquid Drop

Consider a spherical liquid drop of radius \( R \) and surface tension \( S \). Let the excess pressure inside be \( \Delta P = P_{\text{in}} - P_{\text{out}} \).

If we imagine the drop meticulously cut in half, the net outward force due to excess pressure acting on the equatorial area \( \pi R^2 \) is fiercely balanced by the surface tension acting along the boundary circumference \( 2\pi R \):

\[ (\Delta P) (\pi R^2) = S (2\pi R) \implies \Delta P = \frac{2S}{R} \]
Excess Pressure for One Free Surface

A liquid drop has exactly one free surface (liquid-air interface).

\[ P_{\text{in}} - P_{\text{out}} = \frac{2S}{R} \]

Excess Pressure Inside a Soap Bubble

A soap bubble floating gently in the air is hollow. Therefore, it has two free surfaces—an inner spherical boundary and an outer spherical boundary—both in contact with the air. Because the film is microscopically thin, we assume both radii are essentially \( R \).

The total surface tension force pulling the bubble together is effectively doubled.

Excess Pressure for Two Free Surfaces (Soap Bubble)

\[ P_{\text{in}} - P_{\text{out}} = 2 \times \left(\frac{2S}{R}\right) = \frac{4S}{R} \]
Notice the inverse relationship with radius \( R \). The smaller the bubble, the astronomically higher the internal pressure! If a tiny soap bubble is connected by a tiny tube to a large soap bubble, the air will spontaneously flow from the small bubble into the large bubble, shrinking the small one until it vanishes.

Excess Pressure Inside an Air Bubble in Liquid

An air bubble violently trapped inside a pool of liquid is physically identical to a liquid drop in air—it simply consists of exactly one liquid-gas interface.

Excess Pressure for Submerged Air Bubble

\[ P_{\text{in}} - P_{\text{liquid}} = \frac{2S}{R} \]

Keep in mind, to definitively find the true absolute pressure \( P_{\text{in}} \) inside the bubble, you must also meticulously add the hydrostatic fluid pressure \( (\rho g h) \) if the bubble is situated deep underwater.
