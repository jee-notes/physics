Excess Pressure

Why the pressure inside curved liquid surfaces is always higher than outside.

Curvature and Pressure Differential

Surface tension acts along the liquid surface and tends to reduce its area.

For a flat surface, these forces cancel each other due to isotropy and collinearity, producing no net effect. However, for a curved surface (such as a spherical drop), the surface tension forces are no longer collinear. Their components combine to produce a net inward resultant directed toward the center of curvature.

Curvature breaks collinearity. As a result, surface tension generates a net inward force on the interface.

If unopposed, this inward pull would cause the surface to contract continuously. To maintain equilibrium, the pressure inside the drop must be greater than the pressure outside, providing an outward force that balances the effect of surface tension.

This pressure difference is called the excess pressure:

\[ \Delta P = P_{\text{in}} - P_{\text{out}} \]
Excess pressure is the mechanical response required to balance the inward pull of surface tension on a curved surface.

Excess Pressure Inside a Fluid Drop

Consider a spherical liquid drop of radius \( R \) and surface tension \( S \). Let the pressure difference across the surface be:

\[ \Delta P = P_{\text{in}} - P_{\text{out}} \]

To determine this excess pressure, imagine the drop divided into two hemispheres.

The pressure inside the drop acts over the flat circular cross-section of area \( \pi R^2 \), producing an outward force:

\[ F_{\text{pressure}} = (\Delta P)(\pi R^2) \]

Surface tension acts along the circular boundary of this cross-section. Since surface tension is force per unit length, the total inward force along the circumference \( 2\pi R \) is:

\[ F_{\text{surface}} = S (2\pi R) \]

In equilibrium, these forces balance:

\[ (\Delta P)(\pi R^2) = S (2\pi R) \]

Solving,

\[ \Delta P = \frac{2S}{R} \]
Excess Pressure for a Liquid Drop (One Surface)

\[ P_{\text{in}} - P_{\text{out}} = \frac{2S}{R} \]

Liquid Drop vs Gas Bubble

A liquid drop (e.g., a water droplet in air) and a gas bubble (e.g., an air bubble in water) are governed by the same surface tension relation, but their physical behavior differs due to compressibility.

In both cases, surface tension imposes a mechanical constraint:

\[ P_{\text{in}} = P_{\text{out}} + \frac{2S}{R} \]

Here, \( S \) is the surface tension (a material property), and \( P_{\text{out}} \) is the external pressure set by the surroundings.

Liquid Drop (Incompressible)

For a liquid drop, the amount of liquid is fixed and the liquid is effectively incompressible. Therefore, the volume—and hence the radius \( R \)—is fixed.

As a result, the excess pressure relation determines the internal pressure:

\[ P_{\text{in}} = P_{\text{out}} + \frac{2S}{R} \]
In a liquid drop, the radius is fixed.
The internal pressure adjusts to satisfy the surface tension constraint.

Gas Bubble (Compressible)

For a gas bubble, the situation is different because the gas is compressible.

Surface tension still imposes the mechanical condition:

\[ P_{\text{in}} = P_{\text{liquid}} + \frac{2S}{R} \]

However, the gas inside must also satisfy the thermodynamic relation:

\[ P_{\text{in}} V = nRT \]

Since the volume \( V \propto R^3 \), the internal pressure and radius are not independent. They adjust together to satisfy both the mechanical constraint (surface tension) and the thermodynamic constraint (gas law).

In a gas bubble, neither \( P_{\text{in}} \) nor \( R \) is fixed independently. They are coupled through the gas equation of state.
Also, even for gas bubble, surface tension \( S \) is to be taken of the surrounding liquid only. The gas only provides pressure; it does not contribute to surface tension.

Excess Pressure Inside a Soap Bubble

A soap bubble floating gently in the air is hollow. Therefore, it has two free surfaces—an inner spherical boundary and an outer spherical boundary—both in contact with the air. Because the film is microscopically thin, we assume both radii are essentially \( R \).

The total surface tension force pulling the bubble together is effectively doubled.

Excess Pressure for Two Free Surfaces (Soap Bubble)

\[ P_{\text{in}} - P_{\text{out}} = 2 \times \left(\frac{2S}{R}\right) = \frac{4S}{R} \]
Notice the inverse relationship with radius \( R \). The smaller the bubble, the astronomically higher the internal pressure! If a tiny soap bubble is connected by a tiny tube to a large soap bubble, the air will spontaneously flow from the small bubble into the large bubble, shrinking the small one until it vanishes.

Excess Pressure Inside an Air Bubble in Liquid

An air bubble violently trapped inside a pool of liquid is physically identical to a liquid drop in air—it simply consists of exactly one liquid-gas interface.

Excess Pressure for Submerged Air Bubble

\[ P_{\text{in}} - P_{\text{liquid}} = \frac{2S}{R} \]

Keep in mind, to definitively find the true absolute pressure \( P_{\text{in}} \) inside the bubble, you must also meticulously add the hydrostatic fluid pressure \( (\rho g h) \) if the bubble is situated deep underwater.

The "Split-Pressure" Bubble

Two separate bubbles of radius \( R_1 \) and \( R_2 \) of same material \( S \) merge to form a combined bubble. Determine radius of curvature of common interface.

Solution:

For each bubble, the excess pressure due to surface tension is given by:

\[ \Delta P = \frac{4S}{R} \]

Thus, before merging:

\[ P_1 = P_0 + \frac{4S}{R_1}, \quad P_2 = P_0 + \frac{4S}{R_2} \]

Since \( R_1 \lt R_2 \), we have \( P_1 > P_2 \). Therefore, after merging, the common interface forms such that it balances this pressure difference.

Assumptions used in this solution:
1. The radii of the two bubbles remain unchanged after merging: \[ R_1' = R_1, \quad R_2' = R_2 \] 2. Gas compressibility and thermodynamic effects (e.g., \( PV = nRT \)) are ignored.
3. Each outer surface remains spherical and obeys: \[ P = P_0 + \frac{4S}{R} \] 4. The result is obtained from mechanical equilibrium of interfaces only, not from the actual merging process.

Let the radius of curvature of the common interface be \( R \). The excess pressure across this interface is:

\[ P_1 - P_2 = \frac{4S}{R} \]

Substituting:

\[ \left(P_0 + \frac{4S}{R_1}\right) - \left(P_0 + \frac{4S}{R_2}\right) = \frac{4S}{R} \] \[ \frac{4S}{R_1} - \frac{4S}{R_2} = \frac{4S}{R} \]

Cancelling \( 4S \):

\[ \frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2} = \frac{1}{R} \]
\[ R = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_2 - R_1} \]
The curvature of the common interface is determined by the difference in excess pressures of the two bubbles.
The interface bends toward the larger bubble (lower pressure side).
