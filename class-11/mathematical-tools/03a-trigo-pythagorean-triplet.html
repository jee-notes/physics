Pythagorean Triplets

Generating Pythagorean Triplets.

Pythagorean Triplets

A Pythagorean triplet is a set of three positive integers \((a,b,c)\) that satisfy the Pythagorean theorem:

\[ a^2+b^2=c^2 \]

Such numbers can therefore represent the lengths of the three sides of a right-angled triangle, with the largest number being the hypotenuse.

Some commonly used Pythagorean triplets are:

Triplet Verification
(3, 4, 5) \(3^2+4^2=5^2\)
(5, 12, 13) \(5^2+12^2=13^2\)
(7, 24, 25) \(7^2+24^2=25^2\)
(8, 15, 17) \(8^2+15^2=17^2\)
(9, 40, 41) \(9^2+40^2=41^2\)

Primitive and Non-Primitive Triplets

If \((3,4,5)\) is a Pythagorean triplet, then multiplying each number by the same positive integer produces another Pythagorean triplet.

\[ (3,4,5)\longrightarrow(6,8,10)\longrightarrow(9,12,15)\longrightarrow\cdots \]

This is because multiplying all three sides by the same factor scales the right-angled triangle without changing its shape.

Hence, every multiple of a Pythagorean triplet is also a Pythagorean triplet.

A Pythagorean triplet whose three numbers have no common factor greater than 1 is called a primitive Pythagorean triplet. Such triplets are the fundamental or unique triplets from which all other triplets can be generated by scaling.

A triangle has three sides, all of which are scaled by the same factor \(n\).
However, a triangle is still a two-dimensional figure, not a three-dimensional one.
Therefore, scaling all three sides by \(n\) scales its area only by \(n^2\), just like a rectangle, and not by \(n^3\) like a cube.
The scaling power depends on the dimension of the object, not on the number of its sides.
Similarly, a pentagon has five sides, all of which must be scaled by the same factor. Yet, since a pentagon is also a two-dimensional figure, its area still scales by \(n^2\), not by \(n^5\).

Generating Pythagorean Triplets

A simple way to generate many primitive Pythagorean triplets is to begin with an odd positive integer \(n\). The corresponding triplet is

\[ \boxed{\left(n,\;\frac{n^2-1}{2},\;\frac{n^2+1}{2}\right)} \]

The idea behind this construction comes from the Pythagorean theorem. Starting with

\[ a^2+b^2=c^2 \] \[ \Longrightarrow\quad c^2-a^2=b^2 \] \[ \Longrightarrow\quad (c-a)(c+a)=b^2 \]

Suppose \(b\) is an odd positive integer. Then \(b^2\) is also odd. We now choose the two factors of \(b^2\) to differ by 1:

\[ c-a=1,\qquad c+a=b^2 \]

Adding and subtracting these two equations gives

\[ c=\frac{b^2+1}{2}, \qquad a=\frac{b^2-1}{2}. \]

Renaming \(b\) as \(n\), we obtain the required triplet:

\[ \boxed{\left(n,\;\frac{n^2-1}{2},\;\frac{n^2+1}{2}\right)} \]

For example, if \(n=5\),

\[ 5^2=25=1\times25=(13-12)(13+12). \]

Hence,

\[ c-a=1,\qquad c+a=25, \] \[ c=13,\qquad a=12, \]

giving the Pythagorean triplet

\[ (5,\,12,\,13). \]

Similarly, every odd positive integer generates a primitive Pythagorean triplet.

Odd Number (\(n\)) Pythagorean Triplet
3 (3, 4, 5)
5 (5, 12, 13)
7 (7, 24, 25)
9 (9, 40, 41)
11 (11, 60, 61)
The construction is based on a simple algebraic idea: factor the difference of two squares and choose the two factors of \(n^2\) to differ by 1. This immediately determines the remaining two sides of the right-angled triangle.
