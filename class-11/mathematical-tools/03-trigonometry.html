Trigonometry

Trigonometry is the study of triangles and the mathematical relationships between their lengths and angles. A triangle is one of the most fundamental figures in geometry because any three distinct non-collinear points always lie in a single plane and uniquely determine a triangle with three sides and three angles.

Since the triangle is the simplest geometric figure that naturally relates lengths and angles, trigonometry begins with triangles. The ideas developed there are then generalized to more complex geometric situations through the Unit Circle.

In Physics, angles are primarily used to measure the rotation between directions. For example, when we say that the wind is blowing 30° north of east, we first point in the east direction and then rotate it by \(30^\circ\) towards the north. Thus, an angle provides a quantitative measure of the change from one direction to another.

Angles are also used to represent phase. Whenever a physical phenomenon varies periodically in space or time, an angle specifies its stage within one complete cycle. For example, the waxing and waning of the Moon is a periodic phenomenon in time. Hence, we may assign a phase to describe its state, with the New Moon and the Full Moon representing two distinct phases of the lunar cycle.

In this chapter, we first study trigonometric ratios using a right-angled triangle and then extend these ideas to the more general definition based on the Unit Circle, allowing trigonometric functions to be defined for angles of any magnitude.

Lengths and angles are fundamentally different mathematical quantities.
Lengths are ordinary numerical quantities on which arithmetic operations directly represent physical processes, whereas angles possess their own mathematical properties and operations.
Trigonometric functions establish mathematical relationships between lengths and angles, allowing information to be translated from one form to the other whenever the geometry permits.
This is particularly important in Physics because a vector combines both quantities — a magnitude (length) and a direction (angle).
Trigonometry provides the bridge between these two descriptions, allowing vectors to be resolved into components or reconstructed from their components.
In this sense, trigonometric functions play a role similar to logarithms and anti-logarithms, which relate ordinary numbers to their exponents and allow us to work in the most convenient mathematical domain.

Trigonometric Ratios and Identities

Trigonometric ratios are defined using a right-angled triangle. This is a special triangle because one of its angles is \(90^\circ\), making the side opposite to it the longest side, called the hypotenuse.

The remaining two sides are identified with respect to one of the acute angles, say \(\theta\). The side opposite to \(\theta\) is called the perpendicular, while the side adjacent to \(\theta\) (other than the hypotenuse) is called the base.

Using these three sides, we define the six trigonometric ratios. The three primary ratios are

\[ \sin\theta=\frac{P}{H} \qquad \cos\theta=\frac{B}{H} \qquad \tan\theta=\frac{P}{B} \]

The remaining three ratios are simply the reciprocals of these:

\[ \csc\theta=\frac{H}{P} \qquad \sec\theta=\frac{H}{B} \qquad \cot\theta=\frac{B}{P} \]

These definitions form the foundation of trigonometry. In the next section, we shall generalize them using the Unit Circle, allowing trigonometric functions to be defined for angles of any magnitude.

Trigonometric Identities

The trigonometric ratios are not independent of one another. They satisfy several mathematical relationships, known as trigonometric identities, which remain true for every right-angled triangle having the same acute angle.

The most fundamental identities are obtained directly from the Pythagorean Theorem. Since every right-angled triangle satisfies

\[ P^2+B^2=H^2, \]

we can divide the equation by different sides of the triangle to obtain different identities involving the trigonometric ratios.

Dividing throughout by \(H^2\),

\[ \frac{P^2}{H^2}+\frac{B^2}{H^2}=1 \] \[ \boxed{\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1} \]

Dividing throughout by \(B^2\),

\[ \frac{P^2}{B^2}+1=\frac{H^2}{B^2} \] \[ \boxed{\tan^2\theta+1=\sec^2\theta} \]

Dividing throughout by \(P^2\),

\[ 1+\frac{B^2}{P^2}=\frac{H^2}{P^2} \] \[ \boxed{1+\cot^2\theta=\csc^2\theta} \]

These three identities are called the fundamental trigonometric identities.

Since they are all derived from the same Pythagorean theorem, knowing any one of them is sufficient to derive the other two.

All non rt angled triangles can be evaluated as a combination of rt angled triangles. this is used in sine rule - important for lamis theorem.

Angle

In physics, an angle is more than just a shape; it represents rotation or a phase.

We define the angle \(\theta\) as the ratio of arc length \(s\) to the radius \(r\):

\[ \theta = \frac{s}{r} \]

Since it is a ratio of lengths, the Radian is a "dimensionless" unit. In Physics, always assume \(\theta\) is in radians unless specified in degrees.

Conversion: \(180^\circ = \pi \text{ radians}\). For small angles (common in Paraxial Optics), \(\sin \theta \approx \theta\) and \(\tan \theta \approx \theta\).

Unit Circle is a Wrapped Number Line

Think of the unit circle (radius \(= 1\)) as a number line that has been wrapped around a cylinder.

Every point on this circle is defined by coordinates \((x, y)\). Because the radius is \(1\), these coordinates directly give us the fundamental ratios:

  • The \(x\)-coordinate is \(\cos \theta\).
  • The \(y\)-coordinate is \(\sin \theta\).

This "wrapping" explains periodicity. As you keep moving along the number line, you keep passing the same points on the circle every \(2\pi\) interval. This is why waves repeat themselves.

Trigonometric Ratios

While Right Triangle Trigonometry is the starting point, Circular Trigonometry is what we use in Physics for vectors.

For a vector \(\vec{A}\) making an angle \(\theta\) with the x-axis:

  • Horizontal Component: \(A_x = A \cos \theta\)
  • Vertical Component: \(A_y = A \sin \theta\)
  • The Identity: \(\sin^2 \theta + \cos^2 \theta = 1\) (This is just the Pythagorean theorem \(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\) in disguise).

Compound Angle

Physics often involves the "Superposition" of two different waves or forces. To find the result, we use compound angle formulas:

\[ \sin(A \pm B) = \sin A \cos B \pm \cos A \sin B \] \[ \cos(A \pm B) = \cos A \cos B \mp \sin A \sin B \]

Double Angle Formulas (Crucial for Projectile Motion range calculations):

  • \(\sin 2\theta = 2 \sin \theta \cos \theta\)
  • \(\cos 2\theta = \cos^2 \theta - \sin^2 \theta = 2\cos^2 \theta - 1 = 1 - 2\sin^2 \theta\)
