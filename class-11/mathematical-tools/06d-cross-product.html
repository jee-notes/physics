Cross Product: Generating Rotation

Creating a perpendicular axis to describe rotational effects.

Cross Product of Vectors

If the dot product measures how much two vectors overlap, the cross product measures how much they are independent.

Instead of asking "How much of one lies along the other?", we now ask:

"How much area do these two directions create together?"

The Dot Product measures how much vectors align. The Cross Product measures how much they are perpendicular.

Dot product measures directional overlap. Cross product measures directional independence.

Geometric Definition

Consider two vectors \( \vec{A} \) and \( \vec{B} \) making an angle \( \theta \).

The magnitude of their cross product is defined as:

\[ |\vec{A} \times \vec{B}| = |\vec{A}|\,|\vec{B}| \sin\theta \]

Notice the sine term.

If the vectors are parallel, then \( \theta = 0^\circ \) and:

\[ \sin 0^\circ = 0 \]

So the cross product becomes zero. Parallel directions do not create area.

If the vectors are perpendicular, then \( \theta = 90^\circ \) and:

\[ \sin 90^\circ = 1 \]

Now the cross product is maximum. Perpendicular directions create maximum area.

There is a beautiful geometric interpretation of the magnitude \( |\vec{A} \times \vec{B}| \). It represents the area of the parallelogram formed by the two vectors.

As the vectors become more perpendicular, the area they enclose increases. This is why the cross product measures the "spread" or "openness" between two directions.

Direction of Cross Product

Unlike the dot product, the cross product is not a scalar. It produces another vector.

This new vector is perpendicular to the plane containing \( \vec{A} \) and \( \vec{B} \).

Its direction is determined by the Right-Hand Rule:

Since there are two directions perpendicular to a plane (up or down), we need a convention to decide which way the cross product points. We use the Right-Hand Rule.

  • Point your fingers along \( \vec{A} \).
  • Rotate them toward \( \vec{B} \).
  • Your thumb points in the direction of \( \vec{A} \times \vec{B} \).

This perpendicular direction encodes orientation of the plane formed by the two vectors.

The cross product generates an axis of rotation.

While the dot product results in a scalar (a number), the cross product results in a brand new vector. This new vector is unique: it is mutually perpendicular to both original vectors.

Component Form

If:

\[ \vec{A} = A_x \hat{i} + A_y \hat{j} + A_z \hat{k} \] \[ \vec{B} = B_x \hat{i} + B_y \hat{j} + B_z \hat{k} \]

Then:

\[ \vec{A} \times \vec{B} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ A_x & A_y & A_z \\ B_x & B_y & B_z \end{vmatrix} \]

This determinant formula automatically computes the perpendicular vector and its magnitude.

The algebraic structure encodes geometric independence.

Physical Meaning

One of the most important uses of the cross product is in rotational physics.

For example, torque is defined as:

\[ d\vec{\tau} = \vec{r} \times d\vec{F} \]

Torque depends on how much of the force acts perpendicular to the lever arm.

If the force acts along the rod, there is no rotation. If it acts perpendicular, rotation is maximum.

The "twist" (torque) is perpendicular to both the lever arm (\( \vec{r} \)) and the applied force (\( \vec{F} \)). If you push directly toward the hinge (\( \theta = 0^\circ \)), you get no twist. You get the most rotation when you push perpendicularly.

Again, the sine factor naturally captures this.

Commutative:
\( \vec{A}\cdot\vec{B} = \vec{B}\cdot\vec{A} \) 		Anti-commutative:

The minus sign in the cross product is not accidental. It encodes orientation (handedness) of space. Reversing the order reverses the perpendicular direction.

Structural Insight

Vector addition combines directions.

Dot product measures similarity of directions.

Cross product measures how strongly two directions create a new plane.

Together, they capture two fundamental geometric relationships:

  • Parallel interaction (dot)
  • Perpendicular interaction (cross)

These are not arbitrary formulas. They arise from the geometry of space itself.

Rotation vs Rotational Effect

The cross product is often associated with rotation. But we must carefully distinguish between two ideas:

  • Rotation — the geometric orientation between two vectors.
  • Rotational effect — the magnitude of their cross product.

These are not the same thing.

Consider:

\[ \vec{A} = 5\hat{i}, \quad \vec{B} = 5\hat{j} \]

These vectors are perpendicular. Their cross product is:

\[ \vec{A} \times \vec{B} = 25\hat{k} \]

Now consider another pair:

\[ \vec{A} = 3\hat{i} + 4\hat{j}, \quad \vec{B} = -4\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} \]

These vectors are also perpendicular (their dot product is zero), and each has magnitude 5.

Their cross product is again:

\[ \vec{A} \times \vec{B} = 25\hat{k} \]

Geometrically, the two pairs are oriented differently in the plane. The first pair lies exactly along the x and y axes. The second pair is rotated relative to the axes.

So the rotation between the vectors (their actual orientation in space) is different.

But the rotational effect is identical.

Why?

Because cross product magnitude depends only on:

\[ |\vec{A}|\,|\vec{B}| \sin\theta \]

In both cases:

  • Magnitudes = 5
  • Angle = 90°
  • Area of parallelogram = 25

The cross product measures area — not absolute orientation in space.

Cross product does not measure “which way” the vectors are pointing in space. It measures how strongly they create area together.

Rotation describes orientation. Rotational effect describes magnitude of twisting tendency. They are related, but not identical.

Invariance Under Rotation of Axes

Both dot product and cross product are independent of the coordinate system used to calculate them.

If we rotate the coordinate axes, the components of the vectors change. But the geometric relationship between the vectors does not change.

For the dot product:

\[ \vec{A}\cdot\vec{B} = |\vec{A}|\,|\vec{B}|\cos\theta \]

Since magnitudes and the angle between vectors do not change under rotation of axes, the dot product remains the same.

Similarly, the magnitude of the cross product:

\[ |\vec{A}\times\vec{B}| = |\vec{A}|\,|\vec{B}|\sin\theta \]

depends only on magnitudes and the angle between them. Therefore it is also unchanged under rotation of the coordinate system.

Even the direction of \( \vec{A}\times\vec{B} \) changes consistently with the rotation of axes, preserving its geometric meaning.

Dot and cross products depend on geometric relationships — not on how we choose our coordinate axes.

This property is called rotational invariance. It shows that these operations are not algebraic accidents. They arise from the intrinsic geometry of space.
