Algebra

Arithmetic with variables.

Algebra vs Arithmetic

Before discussing math tools, we must clearly understand the difference between arithmetic and algebra — because this distinction is not just technical, it is philosophical.

Arithmetic deals with specific numbers and their operations — addition, subtraction, multiplication, division. Every arithmetic statement produces a definite numerical result.

Algebra, on the other hand, introduces symbols (variables) into arithmetic. These symbols do not represent one fixed number — they represent a whole class of possible values.

So algebra is not just "arithmetic with letters" — it is arithmetic that has been lifted from the specific to the general.

Arithmetic answers the question: "What is the value?"

Algebra answers a deeper question: "What is the structure of the relationship?"

The Power of Generalization

In arithmetic, you might calculate: \(5 + 5 + 5 = 15\).

In algebra, we generalize this as: \(x + x + x = 3x\).

This looks simple, but something profound has happened: we have moved from a single computation to a rule that works for infinitely many cases.

For a physics student, this is the difference between calculating the speed of one specific car and deriving a formula that works for every moving object in the universe.

This is why algebra is the language of physics — because nature does not operate on specific numbers, it operates on relationships.

Constants vs. Variables

  • Constants: Fixed values that do not change within a given context. They often represent universal properties (like \(\pi\)) or fixed conditions (like \(g \approx 9.8 \, m/s^2\) near Earth).
  • Variables: Symbols (usually \(x, y, t\)) representing quantities that can change. They encode freedom — allowing the same equation to describe many different physical situations.

A key mental shift: a variable is not "unknown" — it is allowed to vary. Only in equations do variables sometimes become unknowns to be solved.

Algebraic Expressions & Equations

An expression is a mathematical "phrase" without an equals sign (e.g., \(2x + 5\)). It represents a quantity, but does not assert anything.

An equation is a mathematical "sentence" stating that two expressions are equal (e.g., \(2x + 5 = 15\)). It represents a condition or constraint.

This distinction is crucial in physics:

  • Expressions describe quantities (energy, force, velocity).
  • Equations describe laws (Newton’s laws, conservation principles).

In Physics, equations are our primary tools for modeling reality — they are not just calculations, they are statements about how the universe behaves.

Theory of Equations

In arithmetic, we compute answers.

In algebra, we determine the value(s) of variables that make a statement true. These values are called roots or solutions.

So solving an equation is not calculation — it is finding conditions under which a relationship holds.

Degree of an Equation

The highest power of the variable determines the degree.

In physics, the degree is not just mathematical — it reflects the nature of change in the system.

  • Linear (\(ax + b = 0\)): Degree 1. Represents constant rate of change. Example: uniform motion \(v = u + at\).
  • Quadratic (\(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\)): Degree 2. Represents changing rate of change (acceleration). Example: \(s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2\).

The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra tells us that a polynomial of degree \(n\) has exactly \(n\) roots (counting multiplicity and complex roots).

In physics, this often translates into multiple possible events.

For example, a quadratic equation in time may give two values:

  • One corresponding to when an object reaches a certain point on the way up
  • Another when it reaches the same point on the way down

So multiple roots are not "extra solutions" — they represent different physical possibilities consistent with the same law.

System of Equations

Often, a single generalization is not enough, because multiple variables interact simultaneously. This leads to a system of equations (simultaneous equations).

The Constraint Rule: To solve for \(n\) unknown variables, you need at least \(n\) independent equations.

The word independent is critical — repeating the same information in different forms does not help. Each equation must add a new constraint.

Why this matters for Exams:

Consider two blocks connected by a string (pulley system). You have two unknowns:

  • Tension (\(T\))
  • Acceleration (\(a\))

Arithmetic fails here because there is no single number to compute — there are interdependent quantities.

So we build a system:

  1. For Block 1: \(m_1g - T = m_1a\)
  2. For Block 2: \(T - m_2g = m_2a\)

Now something powerful happens: we use algebra to eliminate variables.

Elimination is not just a trick — it is a way of isolating one relationship from a network of constraints.

This is the real role of algebra in physics:

It translates physical laws into solvable structures and extracts numerical predictions from them.

Algebra is therefore the bridge between:

  • Physical Laws (conceptual understanding)
  • Numerical Results (measurable outcomes)
