Dot Product: The Scalar Projection

Measuring how much of one vector lies in the direction of another.

Dot Product of Vectors

So far, we have learned how to add vectors and how to resolve them into components. Now we ask a different kind of question:

Instead of combining two vectors, can we measure how much one vector lies along another?

That measurement is called the Dot Product.

The dot product measures directional overlap.

Geometric Definition

Consider two vectors \( \vec{A} \) and \( \vec{B} \) making an angle \( \theta \) between them.

The dot product is defined as:

\[ \vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = |\vec{A}|\,|\vec{B}| \cos\theta \]

Notice what this formula says.

It multiplies the magnitudes of the vectors, but it also includes \( \cos\theta \). That cosine term tells us how aligned the vectors are.

Understanding the Cosine Factor

If the two vectors point in exactly the same direction, then \( \theta = 0^\circ \).

\[ \cos 0^\circ = 1 \]

So the dot product becomes:

\[ \vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = |\vec{A}|\,|\vec{B}| \]

This is maximum overlap.

If the vectors are perpendicular, then \( \theta = 90^\circ \).

\[ \cos 90^\circ = 0 \]

So the dot product becomes zero.

This means perpendicular vectors have no directional overlap.

If the angle is greater than 90°, the cosine becomes negative. This indicates that the vectors point partially in opposite directions.

Dot product is positive for similar directions, zero for perpendicular directions, and negative for opposite tendencies.

Dot Product as Projection

Another way to understand the dot product is through projection.

Suppose we project vector \( \vec{A} \) onto the direction of \( \vec{B} \).

The length of this projection is:

\[ |\vec{A}| \cos\theta \]

Now multiply that projected length by \( |\vec{B}| \). That gives:

\[ |\vec{B}| (|\vec{A}| \cos\theta) \]

Which is exactly the dot product.

So the dot product measures how much of one vector acts along the direction of the other.

Component Form

If vectors are written in component form:

\[ \vec{A} = A_x \hat{i} + A_y \hat{j} \] \[ \vec{B} = B_x \hat{i} + B_y \hat{j} \]

Then their dot product becomes:

\[ \vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = A_x B_x + A_y B_y \]

Why does this work?

Because perpendicular directions are independent. Only corresponding components contribute to overlap. The x-component overlaps only with x, and y with y.

Physical Meaning: Work Done

One of the most important uses of the dot product in physics is in calculating work.

\[ W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{s} \]

Work depends only on the component of force along the displacement.

If force is perpendicular to displacement, no work is done. If force acts in the same direction as displacement, work is maximum.

Again, the dot product captures exactly this idea of directional effectiveness.

Structural Insight

Vector addition builds a new direction from two vectors.

Dot product does something different. It collapses two directions into a single number.

That number measures similarity, alignment, or overlap.

Dot product turns two vectors into a scalar that measures directional similarity.

Applications of Dot Product

The dot product is not just a definition. It has powerful applications in geometry, algebra, and physics.

1. Finding the Angle Between Two Vectors

From the geometric definition:

\[ \vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = |\vec{A}|\,|\vec{B}| \cos\theta \]

We can rearrange this to find the angle between two vectors:

\[ \cos\theta = \frac{\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B}}{|\vec{A}|\,|\vec{B}|} \]

Here something important happens.

The dot product can be calculated in two ways:

  • Geometric form: \( |\vec{A}|\,|\vec{B}| \cos\theta \)
  • Algebraic form: \( A_x B_x + A_y B_y + A_z B_z \)

Since both represent the same quantity, we equate them. That is why the algebraic dot product allows us to calculate the geometric angle.

Algebraic dot product = Geometric dot product. This equality allows us to compute angles using components.

2. Component of a Vector Along a Direction

Suppose we want to find how much of vector \( \vec{A} \) lies along the direction of vector \( \vec{B} \).

The projection (scalar component) of \( \vec{A} \) along \( \vec{B} \) is:

\[ \text{Component of } \vec{A} \text{ along } \vec{B} = \frac{\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B}}{|\vec{B}|} \]

Why does this work?

Because:

\[ \vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = |\vec{A}|\,|\vec{B}| \cos\theta \]

Dividing by \( |\vec{B}| \) leaves:

\[ |\vec{A}| \cos\theta \]

Which is exactly the projection of \( \vec{A} \) onto \( \vec{B} \).

So the dot product naturally extracts the effective component along a direction.

3. Dot Product in Integration: \( \int \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{s} \)

In calculus and physics, the dot product appears in line integrals:

\[ W = \int \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{s} \]

Here, \( d\vec{s} \) is an infinitesimal displacement along a path.

Geometrically:

\[ \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{s} = |\vec{F}|\,|d\vec{s}| \cos\theta \]

This means only the component of force along the direction of motion contributes to work.

However, when we write everything in component (algebraic) form:

\[ \vec{F} = F_x \hat{i} + F_y \hat{j} \] \[ d\vec{s} = dx\,\hat{i} + dy\,\hat{j} \]

Then:

\[ \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{s} = F_x dx + F_y dy \]

Notice something important:

There is no explicit cosine term here.

Why? Because the cosine is already built into the component multiplication.

The sign of \( d\vec{s} \) is determined by the direction of motion and the limits of integration. The algebra automatically accounts for orientation.

In component form, cosine is hidden inside coordinate multiplication. Algebra already encodes geometric alignment.

Structural Summary

  • Dot product measures directional overlap.
  • It allows calculation of angle between vectors.
  • It extracts the component of one vector along another.
  • In integration, it ensures only effective directional contribution is counted.

Dot product converts geometric alignment into algebraic computation.
