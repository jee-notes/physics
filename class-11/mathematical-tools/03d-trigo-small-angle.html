Small Angle Approximation

Many physical systems involve very small angular displacements. In such situations, several trigonometric functions can be replaced by much simpler expressions, known as the small-angle approximations.

The geometric idea behind these approximations is that, for a sufficiently small angle \(d\theta\), the corresponding arc and chord of a circle become nearly equal in length.

Consider a circle of radius \(R\) subtending a very small angle \(d\theta\) at its centre.

The length of the arc is

\[ ds = R\,d\theta. \]

Now join the two end points of the arc by a chord. Together with the two radii, this forms an isosceles triangle. For a very small angle, the chord is almost indistinguishable from the arc.

If the chord is resolved into a right-angled triangle, its perpendicular side is

\[ R\sin(d\theta). \]

Since the chord and the arc are nearly equal for small angles,

\[ R\sin(d\theta)\approx Rd\theta. \]

Cancelling the common factor \(R\),

\[ \boxed{\sin(d\theta)\approx d\theta.} \]

Using this result together with the fundamental trigonometric identities, we obtain the commonly used small-angle approximations:

\[ \boxed{\sin\theta\approx\theta,\qquad \tan\theta\approx\theta,\qquad \cos\theta\approx1} \]
Major confusion here is that students replace everything with 0 (zero).
They think if \(\theta \to 0\) , then \(\sin \theta \approx \theta\) . But as \(\theta \to 0\) , \(\sin \theta \approx 0\) .
This argument is correct, but it is not to be used directly.
Like if \(\sin 3^\circ\) is asked, then its \( = 3\pi / 180\) and not 0.

Proof using the Sandwich Theorem

The small-angle approximation can be proved rigorously using the Sandwich Theorem and the geometry of the Unit Circle.

Consider a unit circle with centre \(O\). Let \(A\) and \(C\) be two points on the circle subtending an angle \(\theta\) at the centre. Draw the radius \(OC\), drop a perpendicular from \(A\) to \(OC\) meeting it at \(D\), and draw the tangent at \(C\). Extend \(OA\) to meet this tangent at \(B\).

The three enclosed regions satisfy the inequality

\[ \text{Area}(\triangle AOD) \lt \text{Area}(\text{Sector }AOC) \lt \text{Area}(\triangle BOC). \]

Substituting the expressions for the three areas gives

\[ \frac12\sin\theta \lt \frac12\theta \lt \frac12\tan\theta, \]

or equivalently,

\[ \boxed{\sin\theta \lt \theta \lt \tan\theta.} \]

Dividing throughout by \(\sin\theta\) and applying the Sandwich Theorem as \(\theta\to0\) gives

\[ \boxed{\lim_{\theta\to0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}=1} \]

This result forms the mathematical basis of the small-angle approximations used throughout Physics.

From the derivation we can see that, these approximations are valid only when the angle is measured in radians.
They arise from the geometric fact that, for very small angles, the arc, the chord, and the tangent segment become nearly identical.
