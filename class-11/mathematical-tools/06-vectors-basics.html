Vectors: Directional Quantities

Managing entities with both magnitude and orientation.

Scalars vs. Vectors

In physics, quantities describe measurable aspects of nature. Some quantities are completely described by a single number, while others require both magnitude and direction.

  • Scalars: Quantities described only by magnitude. Examples include Mass, Time, Energy, Temperature. They are independent of orientation in space.
  • Vectors: Quantities that require both magnitude and direction. Examples include Displacement, Velocity, Acceleration, Force, and Electric Field.

The distinction is not merely mathematical — it reflects physical behavior. A force of 10 N east is physically different from 10 N west, even though the magnitude is identical.

A scalar changes value; a vector changes both value and orientation.

What is Direction Really?

Vectors cannot be understood without clarifying what we mean by direction. Let us first examine what we mean by a quantity that "needs" direction.

Suppose I ask you to add mass to my hand — you would ask, "How much?" Here, only magnitude is required to describe the quantity.

Now suppose I am clicking your group photo and I ask you to move 1 meter — you would immediately ask, "1 meter in which direction?" The magnitude alone is incomplete. To describe displacement, we must specify both "how much" and "where".

This "where" is what we call orientation in space. Direction can be described in many ways: 1 m left, forward, east, 30° above the horizontal, etc. These are different representations of the same underlying idea like how length can be described in centi-meter, light year etc.

Direction refers to orientation in space. Mathematically, it is described using plane angles or unit vectors. This is because a vector is geometrically a 1-dimensional oriented ray in space, and the angle between two rays is always a plane angle — even in three dimensions.

Mathematical representation of Vectors

A vector is represented geometrically as a directed line segment — often idealized as a ray. The length of this segment represents the magnitude of the vector, and the arrow indicates its direction.

Geometrically, the vector tells us two things: how much (length) and where (orientation in space). This picture is intuitive and physically meaningful.

However, geometry alone is not sufficient for calculation. To perform algebraic operations, we need a way to encode direction symbolically. For this purpose, we introduce the concept of a unit vector.

Unit Vector and Vector Magnitude

From earlier examples, we expressed vectors verbally as:

Displacement is 5 m east. Force is 5 N right.

In these statements: 5 m and 5 N represent magnitudes, while east and right represent directions.

To describe a vector algebraically, we separate these two components explicitly:

\[ \vec{A} = A \hat{a} \]

Here:

  • \( \vec{A} \) is the vector quantity (denoted by an arrow).
  • \( A = |\vec{A}| \) is its magnitude (a scalar).
  • \( \hat{a} \) is a unit vector in the direction of \( \vec{A} \).

A unit vector has magnitude 1 and carries only directional information. Thus, a vector is fundamentally the product of a scalar magnitude and a pure direction.

For example, when we say "Force is 5 N right", we write:

\[ \vec{F} = 5 \hat{f} \]

Here:

  • \( |\vec{F}| = 5 \text{ N} \)
  • \( \hat{f} \) represents the unit vector pointing toward the right.

Instead of repeatedly writing "right", we just encode that direction once and for all using \( \hat{f} \). Once defined (expressed mathematically), this unit vector can be reused consistently for other physical quantities.

For example:

\[ \vec{S} = 5 \hat{f} \quad (\text{displacement of 5 m right}) \] \[ \vec{V} = -5 \hat{f} \quad (\text{velocity of 5 m/s left}) \]

Notice the minus sign in the second expression. Left is exactly 180° opposite to right. Since these two directions lie along the same straight line, they are linearly related. Changing direction along the same line simply introduces a negative sign.

Thus, once a reference direction is fixed, opposite directions are handled algebraically through sign. This is the simplest example of how geometry becomes algebra in vector representation.

Vector Algebra in Physics

In physics, we define physical quantities in such a way that they can be manipulated mathematically. Laws of physics are expressed as mathematical relations between quantities, and therefore the operations performed on those quantities must be mathematically well-defined.

For scalars, the familiar operations of addition, subtraction, multiplication and division are sufficient. However, vectors carry direction in addition to magnitude. Because of this additional structure, scalar algebra is no longer adequate.

Vectors obey their own rules of combination — collectively known as Vector Algebra. These rules are not arbitrary; they arise from the geometric nature of vectors in space. This is a full separate chapter in 12th Maths.

In physics, we focus on vector operations as they appear in physical laws: how forces combine, how projections determine work, and how perpendicular interactions generate rotation.

Scalar algebra is sufficient for quantities without direction. Vector algebra is required when geometry becomes part of the physics.
