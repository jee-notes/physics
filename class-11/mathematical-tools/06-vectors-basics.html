Vectors: Directional Quantities

Managing entities with both magnitude and orientation.

Scalars vs. Vectors

In physics, quantities describe measurable aspects of nature. Some quantities are completely described by a single number, while others require both magnitude and direction.

  • Scalars: Quantities described only by magnitude. Examples include Mass, Time, Energy, Temperature. They are independent of orientation in space.
  • Vectors: Quantities that require both magnitude and direction. Examples include Displacement, Velocity, Acceleration, Force, and Electric Field.

The distinction is not merely mathematical — it reflects physical behavior. A force of 10 N east is physically different from 10 N west, even though the magnitude is identical.

A scalar changes value; a vector changes both value and orientation.

What is Direction Really?

Vectors cannot be understood without clarifying what we mean by direction. Let us first examine what we mean by a quantity that "needs" direction.

Suppose I ask you to add mass to my hand — you would ask, "How much?" Here, only magnitude is required to describe the quantity.

Now suppose I am clicking your group photo and I ask you to move 1 meter — you would immediately ask, "1 meter in which direction?" The magnitude alone is incomplete. To describe displacement, we must specify both "how much" and "where".

This "where" is what we call orientation in space. Direction can be described in many ways: 1 m left, forward, east, 30° above the horizontal, etc. These are different representations of the same underlying idea like how length can be described in centi-meter, light year etc.

Direction refers to orientation in space. Mathematically, it is described using plane angles or unit vectors. This is because a vector is geometrically a 1-dimensional oriented ray in space, and the angle between two rays is always a plane angle — even in three dimensions.

Geometric Representation of Vectors

A vector is represented geometrically as a directed line segment. The length of the segment represents magnitude, and the arrow indicates direction.

In two dimensions, we write:

\[ \vec{A} = A_x \hat{i} + A_y \hat{j} \]

where \( \hat{i} \) and \( \hat{j} \) are unit vectors along the x and y axes. This representation converts geometric direction into algebraic components.

The magnitude of the vector is:

\[ |\vec{A}| = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2} \]

Vector Addition and Resolution

Vectors cannot be added like ordinary numbers. They obey geometric rules such as the triangle law and parallelogram law.

If two vectors are placed head-to-tail, their resultant is the vector drawn from the tail of the first to the head of the second.

Resolution of Vectors

Any vector making an angle \( \theta \) with the x-axis can be resolved into rectangular components:

\[ A_x = A \cos \theta, \quad A_y = A \sin \theta \]

This converts a geometric problem into algebra. Component-wise addition greatly simplifies force analysis, projectile motion and field calculations.

Resolution transforms geometry into algebra — one of the most powerful strategies in physics.

Unit Vectors and Direction Cosines

A unit vector has magnitude 1 and specifies only direction. Any vector can be written as:

\[ \hat{A} = \frac{\vec{A}}{|\vec{A}|} \]

In three dimensions:

\[ \vec{A} = A_x \hat{i} + A_y \hat{j} + A_z \hat{k} \]

The orientation of a vector in space can be described using direction cosines:

\[ \cos \alpha = \frac{A_x}{|\vec{A}|}, \quad \cos \beta = \frac{A_y}{|\vec{A}|}, \quad \cos \gamma = \frac{A_z}{|\vec{A}|} \]

These satisfy the identity:

\[ \cos^2 \alpha + \cos^2 \beta + \cos^2 \gamma = 1 \]

Vector Products

Multiplication of vectors produces different physical meanings, depending on how they interact.

  • Dot Product (Scalar Product): Measures the extent to which one vector projects onto another.
\[ \vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = AB \cos \theta \]

It is maximum when vectors are parallel and zero when perpendicular. Used in Work, Flux and Power.

  • Cross Product (Vector Product): Measures the perpendicular interaction between two vectors.
\[ \vec{A} \times \vec{B} = (AB \sin \theta)\,\hat{n} \]

Its direction is determined by the right-hand rule. Used in Torque, Angular Momentum, and Magnetic Force.

Dot Product → Measures parallel effect (projection). Cross Product → Measures perpendicular effect (rotation).

Why Vectors Matter in Physics

Most fundamental physical laws are vector equations:

\[ \vec{F} = m\vec{a} \]
\[ \vec{E} = \frac{\vec{F}}{q} \]

Ignoring direction leads to incorrect conclusions. Vectors embed geometry directly into equations.

Understanding vectors is foundational to mechanics, electromagnetism, wave physics, and rotational dynamics. They are not just mathematical tools — they are the language of spatial interaction.
