Vector Addition: Composition of Effects

Determining the net result of multiple simultaneous directional influences.

The Geometry of Addition

Unlike scalars, where \( 3 + 4 \) is always \( 7 \), the sum of two vectors depends on their relative orientation. We define the "sum" as a single Resultant Vector \( (\vec{R}) \) that produces the same physical effect as the individual vectors combined.

Geometrically, we use two primary laws:

  • Triangle Law: Used for vectors acting in sequence (head-to-tail). If \( \vec{A} \) and \( \vec{B} \) are two sides of a triangle, \( \vec{R} \) is the third side closing the triangle.
  • Parallelogram Law: Used for vectors acting at the same point (co-initial). The resultant is the diagonal of the parallelogram formed by \( \vec{A} \) and \( \vec{B} \).

Analytical Method (The Resultant Formula)

To calculate the magnitude and direction of the resultant \( \vec{R} \) for two vectors \( \vec{A} \) and \( \vec{B} \) inclined at an angle \( \theta \), we project the vectors onto a common coordinate system.

\[ R = |\vec{R}| = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2 + 2AB \cos \theta} \]

The direction of the resultant is specified by the angle \( \alpha \) it makes with vector \( \vec{A} \):

\[ \tan \alpha = \frac{B \sin \theta}{A + B \cos \theta} \]

Physical Significance

In mechanics, vector addition is the foundation for finding the Net Force \( (\sum \vec{F}) \). If multiple forces act on a mass, the acceleration is determined only by the resultant direction and magnitude.

A common pitfall is to add magnitudes directly. Remember: the magnitude of the sum is not the sum of the magnitudes unless the vectors are perfectly parallel \( (\theta = 0^\circ) \).
\[ |A - B| \leq R \leq |A + B| \]

The resultant magnitude is bounded by the difference and the sum of the individual magnitudes.
