Oblique Doppler, Echoes & Shock Waves

Two-dimensional oblique relative motion, circular source kinematics, acoustic echo reflections, and supersonic Mach cones.

Oblique Doppler Effect (Non-Collinear Motion and Line of Sight)

When the source and observer move along lines that are not collinear, the Doppler shift is determined strictly by the components of velocity along the instantaneous line of sight (line joining the source and observer).

Let \( \theta \) be the angle between the source velocity vector \( \vec{v}_s \) and the line joining the source to the observer. The effective velocity component of the source toward the observer is \( v_s \cos\theta \).

Oblique Doppler Formula: \[ f' = f_0 \left( \frac{v}{v - v_s \cos\theta} \right) \]
Variation of Observed Pitch as Source Passes By:
  • When far away approaching (\( \theta \to 0^\circ \)): \( \cos\theta \approx 1 \implies f' \approx f_0 \left(\frac{v}{v - v_s}\right) \) (Maximum constant pitch).
  • At the point of closest approach (\( \theta = 90^\circ \)): \( \cos 90^\circ = 0 \implies f' = f_0 \) (True source frequency is heard at this precise instant!).
  • When far away receding (\( \theta \to 180^\circ \)): \( \cos 180^\circ = -1 \implies f' \approx f_0 \left(\frac{v}{v + v_s}\right) \) (Minimum constant pitch).

Source Moving in a Circular Trajectory

Consider a sound source of frequency \( f_0 \) moving with uniform circular speed \( v_s = \omega R \) in a horizontal circle of radius \( R \), observed by a stationary observer located in the same plane at a distance \( D > R \) from the circle center.

The maximum and minimum observed frequencies occur when the velocity vector (tangent to the circle) aligns directly along the line of sight:

\[ f_{\text{max}} = f_0 \left( \frac{v}{v - v_s} \right) \quad \text{(At point of maximum approach along tangent)} \] \[ f_{\text{min}} = f_0 \left( \frac{v}{v + v_s} \right) \quad \text{(At point of maximum recession along tangent)} \]

Acoustic Reflection from Moving Boundaries and Echo Doppler

When sound emitted by a stationary source is reflected by a moving target (e.g., a moving car, wall, or bat echolocating an insect), the Doppler shift occurs in two sequential stages:

Step 1: Moving Reflector Acts as an Observer

The moving wall/target of speed \( u \) receives sound waves with an apparent frequency \( f_1 \):

\[ f_1 = f_0 \left( \frac{v + u}{v} \right) \quad (\text{Target moving toward source}) \]

Step 2: Moving Reflector Acts as a Re-emitting Source

The reflector instantly re-radiates (reflects) this frequency \( f_1 \) while continuing its forward motion at speed \( u \). The sound travels back to the original observer at the source, who detects:

\[ f_2 = f_1 \left( \frac{v}{v - u} \right) = f_0 \left( \frac{v + u}{v} \right) \left( \frac{v}{v - u} \right) \]
Echo Frequency Detected by Source: \[ f_{\text{echo}} = f_0 \left( \frac{v + u}{v - u} \right) \]

Beat Frequency Heard by Source Operator

\[ f_b = f_{\text{echo}} - f_0 = f_0 \left( \frac{v + u}{v - u} - 1 \right) = f_0 \left( \frac{2u}{v - u} \right) \approx \left( \frac{2u}{v} \right) f_0 \quad (\text{for } u \ll v) \]

Supersonic Motion, Mach Number, and Shock Waves

When a source of sound travels faster than the speed of sound in the medium (\( v_s > v \)), it outruns its own acoustic wavefronts.

1. Mach Number (\( M \))

\[ M = \frac{v_{\text{source}}}{v_{\text{sound}}} = \frac{v_s}{v} \]
  • \( M < 1 \): Subsonic motion
  • \( M = 1 \): Transonic motion (sound barrier)
  • \( M > 1 \): Supersonic motion
  • \( M > 5 \): Hypersonic motion

2. Mach Cone & Shock Wave Envelope

The spherical wavefronts emitted at successive earlier times overlap and interfere constructively along a common conical tangent envelope known as the Mach Cone.

The semi-vertical angle \( \alpha \) of the Mach cone is given by:

Mach Cone Half-Angle: \[ \sin\alpha = \frac{v \cdot t}{v_s \cdot t} = \frac{v}{v_s} = \frac{1}{M} \]
The concentrated envelope of constructive pressure interference carries a massive, abrupt pressure discontinuity known as a Shock Wave. When this shock wave sweeps past an observer on the ground, it produces a sudden, thunderous acoustic explosion called a Sonic Boom.
