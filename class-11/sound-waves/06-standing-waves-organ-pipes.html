Standing Waves in Organ Pipes & Air Columns

Longitudinal acoustic resonance in open and closed pipes, harmonic overtone spectra, and end corrections.

Formation of Standing Sound Waves in Air Columns

An organ pipe is a cylindrical acoustic tube enclosing a column of air. When a source of sound (such as a vibrating tuning fork or a jet of air directed against a sharp lip) excites the air inside the pipe, longitudinal traveling waves reflect back and forth between the ends.

The linear superposition of these counter-propagating longitudinal waves sets up stationary standing sound waves characterized by distinct nodes and antinodes.

Displacement Nodes/Antinodes vs Pressure Nodes/Antinodes

In describing standing sound waves, it is vital to specify whether nodes and antinodes refer to particle displacement or excess pressure:

Boundary Location Displacement Condition Pressure Condition
Rigid Closed End Displacement Node (N)
Air molecules cannot move past the barrier (\( s = 0 \)).		 Pressure Antinode (A)
Maximum pressure compression and rarefaction (\( \Delta P = \pm \Delta P_0 \)).
Open End Displacement Antinode (A)
Air molecules oscillate freely in and out with maximum amplitude.		 Pressure Node (N)
Pressure is clamped to ambient atmospheric pressure (\( \Delta P = 0 \)).

Open Organ Pipe: Normal Modes, Harmonics, and Overtones

An open organ pipe is open to the atmosphere at both ends. Therefore, Displacement Antinodes (Pressure Nodes) form at both ends:

The length \( L \) of the pipe accommodates an integer number of half-wavelengths:

\[ L = n \left( \frac{\lambda_n}{2} \right) \implies \lambda_n = \frac{2L}{n} \quad (n = 1, 2, 3, \dots) \]
Frequencies of Open Organ Pipe: \[ f_n = \frac{n v}{2L} = n f_1 \quad (n = 1, 2, 3, \dots) \] \[ f_1 : f_2 : f_3 : f_4 : \dots = 1 : 2 : 3 : 4 : \dots \]
In an open organ pipe, all harmonics (both even and odd) are present. The fundamental frequency is \( f_1 = \frac{v}{2L} \).

Closed Organ Pipe: Odd Harmonic Mode Spectrum

A closed organ pipe is closed at one end and open at the other. Thus, it must have a Displacement Node at the closed end and a Displacement Antinode at the open end:

The length \( L \) of the pipe contains an odd multiple of quarter-wavelengths:

\[ L = (2n - 1) \frac{\lambda_n}{4} \implies \lambda_n = \frac{4L}{2n - 1} \quad (n = 1, 2, 3, \dots) \]
Frequencies of Closed Organ Pipe: \[ f_n = \frac{(2n - 1)v}{4L} = (2n - 1) f_1 \quad \text{where } f_1 = \frac{v}{4L} \] \[ f_1 : f_3 : f_5 : f_7 : \dots = 1 : 3 : 5 : 7 : \dots \]
In a closed organ pipe, only odd harmonics are produced; all even harmonics are completely absent. Because open organ pipes produce all harmonics, their acoustic quality (timbre) is much richer and sweeter than closed pipes.

End Correction (Rayleigh Correction e = 0.6 r)

In real physical pipes, air molecules at an open end do not experience a sudden transition to unconstrained free space at the exact geometric boundary. Instead, the displacement antinode forms slightly outside the physical rim of the pipe at an acoustic distance \( e \), known as the End Correction.

According to Lord Rayleigh:

Rayleigh's End Correction: \[ e \approx 0.6 \, r \approx 0.3 \, d \]

where \( r \) is the internal radius and \( d = 2r \) is the internal diameter of the pipe.

Effective Acoustic Lengths:

  • Open Organ Pipe (Two Open Ends): \[ L_{\text{eff}} = L + 2e = L + 1.2 \, r \implies f_n = \frac{n v}{2(L + 2e)} \]
  • Closed Organ Pipe (One Open End): \[ L_{\text{eff}} = L + e = L + 0.6 \, r \implies f_n = \frac{(2n - 1) v}{4(L + e)} \]
