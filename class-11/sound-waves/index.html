Sound Waves

Longitudinal mechanical compressional waves, acoustic pressure dynamics, organ pipes, beats, and Doppler effect.

Conceptual Overview

Sound represents the primary auditory medium through which information and acoustic energy travel in our everyday physical environment. Fundamentally, a sound wave is a longitudinal mechanical wave that propagates through an elastic medium (solid, liquid, or gas) via collective, coupled oscillations of the constituent molecules parallel to the direction of energy propagation.

Unlike transverse waves on a string where particles oscillate perpendicular to the wave velocity and require shear elasticity, sound waves propagate via alternating spatial variations in volume, density, and pressure. When a vibrating source (such as a tuning fork prong, loudspeaker cone, or vibrating vocal cord) moves forward, it compresses the adjacent fluid layer, forming a compression (region of high density and pressure). When it moves backward, the fluid expands into the rarefied space, forming a rarefaction (region of low density and pressure).

Sound propagation is fundamentally a traveling pressure and density oscillation in an elastic medium, not a net translational migration of air particles.

Nature of Longitudinal Compressional Waves

Because fluids (liquids and gases) lack shear rigidity (shear modulus \( \eta = 0 \)), transverse mechanical waves cannot propagate through their interior bulk. Fluids can, however, resist volume changes and compressions through their Bulk Modulus (\( B \)).

Consequently, sound waves inside fluids are strictly longitudinal. In solids, which possess both volume elasticity (Bulk Modulus \( B \)) and shear elasticity (Shear Modulus \( \eta \)), sound can propagate both as longitudinal compressional waves and as transverse shear waves.

Mechanical sound waves strictly require a physical material medium possessing both Elasticity (Bulk Modulus \( B \)) and Inertia (Mass Density \( \rho \)). Sound cannot propagate through an absolute vacuum.

Displacement and Pressure Formulations

A sound wave can be described equivalently using two distinct mathematical representations:

  • Longitudinal Displacement Wave \( s(x,t) \): Tracks the instantaneous longitudinal displacement of medium particles from their equilibrium positions: \[ s(x,t) = s_0 \sin(kx - \omega t) \]
  • Excess Pressure Wave \( \Delta P(x,t) \): Tracks the local instantaneous deviation of pressure from ambient atmospheric pressure \( P_0 \): \[ \Delta P(x,t) = \Delta P_0 \cos(kx - \omega t) = \Delta P_0 \sin\left(kx - \omega t + \frac{\pi}{2}\right) \] where the pressure amplitude is \( \Delta P_0 = B k s_0 = \rho v \omega s_0 \).
Fundamental Phase Shift: The acoustic pressure wave leads the displacement wave by a phase angle of \( \frac{\pi}{2} \) radians (\( 90^\circ \)). Points of zero displacement (displacement nodes) correspond to points of maximum pressure variation (pressure antinodes).

Speed of Sound and Thermodynamic Corrections

Newton originally formulated the speed of sound in fluids using an isothermal compression assumption (\( v = \sqrt{P/\rho} \)), which predicted a speed of \( \approx 280 \text{ m/s} \) in air at STP—underestimating the experimental value (\( 332 \text{ m/s} \)) by about \( 15\% \).

Laplace resolved this historic discrepancy by recognizing that sound compressions and rarefactions occur so rapidly that negligible heat is conducted between adjacent layers. Sound propagation is strictly an adiabatic process governed by the adiabatic bulk modulus \( B_S = \gamma P \):

Laplace's Formula for Speed of Sound in Gases: \[ v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma P}{\rho}} = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma R T}{M}} \]

Acoustic Intensity, Organ Pipes, Beats & Doppler Shift

The study of sound encompasses diverse physical phenomena central to JEE Main and Advanced:

  • Sound Intensity & Decibels: Acoustic power transmitted per unit area \( I = \frac{\Delta P_0^2}{2\rho v} \), and logarithmic loudness scaling \( \beta = 10\log_{10}(I/I_0) \).
  • Organ Pipes & Air Columns: Standing sound waves in open pipes (all harmonics \( f_n = \frac{nv}{2L} \)) and closed pipes (odd harmonics \( f_n = \frac{(2n-1)v}{4L} \)), along with end corrections.
  • Acoustic Beats: Periodic waxing and waning of sound intensity produced by the superposition of two waves with close frequencies (\( f_b = |f_1 - f_2| \)).
  • Doppler Effect: The apparent shift in observed acoustic frequency due to relative motion between source, observer, and medium.
