Speed of Sound & Thermodynamic Corrections

Newton's elastic propagation law, speed of sound across different states of matter, and bulk elasticity.

Newton's Elastic Formulation v = sqrt(E/rho)

Sir Isaac Newton established that the speed of propagation of a mechanical longitudinal wave through any continuous elastic medium is governed universally by the ratio of the medium's restoring elasticity to its inertial mass density:

Newton's General Wave Speed Formula: \[ v = \sqrt{\frac{E}{\rho}} \]

where:

  • \( E \): Modulus of elasticity of the medium \([\text{N/m}^2 \text{ or Pa}]\), Dimension: \([M^1 L^{-1} T^{-2}]\).
  • \( \rho \): Mass density of the medium \([\text{kg/m}^3]\), Dimension: \([M^1 L^{-3} T^0]\).
  • \( v \): Resulting wave speed \([\text{m/s}]\), Dimension: \([M^0 L^1 T^{-1}]\).

Speed of Sound in Solids, Liquids, and Gases

The specific modulus of elasticity \( E \) applicable depends on the physical state and geometric constraints of the medium:

1. Long Thin Solid Rod

When sound propagates along a thin solid rod or wire, lateral expansion and contraction occur freely without significant transverse stress. The relevant elastic modulus is Young's Modulus (\( Y \)):

\[ v_{\text{rod}} = \sqrt{\frac{Y}{\rho}} \]

2. Extended 3D Bulk Solid

In an extended, bulk 3-dimensional solid, longitudinal compressions involve both volume compression (Bulk Modulus \( B \)) and shearing rigidity (Shear Modulus \( \eta \)):

\[ v_{\text{solid (bulk)}} = \sqrt{\frac{B + \frac{4}{3}\eta}{\rho}} \]

3. Fluids (Liquids)

Liquids have no shear modulus (\( \eta = 0 \)) but resist volumetric compression through their Bulk Modulus (\( B \)):

\[ v_{\text{liquid}} = \sqrt{\frac{B}{\rho}} \]

4. Gases

Gases undergo adiabatic volumetric compression governed by the Adiabatic Bulk Modulus (\( B_S = \gamma P \)):

\[ v_{\text{gas}} = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma P}{\rho}} = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma R T}{M}} \]

Bulk Modulus and Medium Elasticity

Comparing sound speeds across the three states of matter reveals a key physical trend:

\[ v_{\text{solids}} > v_{\text{liquids}} > v_{\text{gases}} \]
Medium Typical Density \( \rho \, (\text{kg/m}^3) \) Elastic Modulus \( E \, (\text{N/m}^2) \) Speed of Sound \( v \, (\text{m/s}) \)
Steel (Solid) \( 7800 \) \( Y \approx 2 \times 10^{11} \) \( \approx 5100 \text{ m/s} \)
Water (Liquid) \( 1000 \) \( B \approx 2.2 \times 10^9 \) \( \approx 1480 \text{ m/s} \)
Air (Gas, 20°C) \( 1.2 \) \( \gamma P \approx 1.4 \times 10^5 \) \( \approx 343 \text{ m/s} \)
Why is sound fastest in solids despite solids having the highest densities?
Although solids are much denser than gases, their elastic moduli (restoring interatomic forces) are greater by a factor of over \( 10^6 \). Because elasticity \( E \) in the numerator vastly overwhelms density \( \rho \) in the denominator, sound travels fastest in rigid solids and slowest in compressible gases.
