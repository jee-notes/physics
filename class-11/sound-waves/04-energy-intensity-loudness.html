Energy, Intensity and the Decibel Scale

Acoustic energy density, intensity formulations, geometric spreading laws, and logarithmic decibel scaling.

Energy Density of Sound Waves

As a longitudinal sound wave travels through an elastic medium, the fluid elements simultaneously possess kinetic energy (due to longitudinal particle velocity \( v_p = \frac{\partial s}{\partial t} \)) and potential energy (due to elastic volumetric compression and excess pressure \( \Delta P \)).

1. Kinetic Energy Density (\( u_k \))

For a differential fluid element of volume \( dV = A \, dx \) and mass \( dm = \rho \, dV \):

\[ u_k = \frac{dK}{dV} = \frac{1}{2}\rho \left( \frac{\partial s}{\partial t} \right)^2 = \frac{1}{2} \rho \omega^2 s_0^2 \cos^2(kx - \omega t) \]

2. Potential Energy Density (\( u_p \))

The work done in compressing the fluid volume against the bulk modulus is:

\[ u_p = \frac{dU}{dV} = \frac{\Delta P^2}{2B} = \frac{1}{2} B k^2 s_0^2 \cos^2(kx - \omega t) = \frac{1}{2} \rho \omega^2 s_0^2 \cos^2(kx - \omega t) \]

3. Average Total Energy Density (\( \langle u \rangle \))

Summing \( u_k \) and \( u_p \) and taking the time average over one period (\( \langle \cos^2\theta \rangle = 1/2 \)):

Average Energy Density (\( \langle u \rangle \)): \[ \langle u \rangle = \frac{1}{2} \rho \omega^2 s_0^2 = \frac{\Delta P_0^2}{2\rho v^2} = \frac{\Delta P_0^2}{2B} \quad [\text{SI Unit: J/m}^3] \]

Sound Intensity Formula I = Delta P0^2 / (2 rho v)

Sound Intensity (\( I \)) is defined as the average acoustic energy flowing per unit time across a unit surface area perpendicular to the direction of sound propagation.

\[ I = \langle u \rangle \cdot v \]

Substituting the energy density expressions:

Acoustic Intensity Formulas: \[ I = \frac{1}{2} \rho v \omega^2 s_0^2 = 2\pi^2 \rho v f^2 s_0^2 \] \[ I = \frac{\Delta P_0^2}{2\rho v} = \frac{\Delta P_0^2 v}{2B} \quad [\text{SI Unit: W/m}^2] \]

Inverse Square Law for Point and Line Acoustic Sources

In an ideal, non-absorbing medium, acoustic power \( P \) emitted by a source spreads geometrically across expanding wavefronts:

1. Isotropic Point Source (Spherical Wavefronts)

A point source emits sound uniformly in all directions over a spherical surface of area \( A = 4\pi r^2 \):

\[ I(r) = \frac{P_{\text{source}}}{4\pi r^2} \propto \frac{1}{r^2} \] \[ \text{Amplitude: } s_0(r) \propto \frac{1}{r}, \quad \Delta P_0(r) \propto \frac{1}{r} \]

2. Line Source (Cylindrical Wavefronts)

A long, uniform line source (such as a busy highway or long vibrating wire) emits cylindrical wavefronts of area \( A = 2\pi r L \):

\[ I(r) = \frac{P/L}{2\pi r} \propto \frac{1}{r} \] \[ \text{Amplitude: } s_0(r) \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}, \quad \Delta P_0(r) \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \]

Loudness, Weber-Fechner Law, and the Decibel (dB) Scale

Loudness (\( L \)) is the subjective physiological sensation of sound magnitude perceived by the human auditory system. According to the Weber-Fechner Law, the physiological sensation is proportional to the logarithm of the physical stimulus intensity:

\[ L \propto \log_{10} I \]

The Decibel (dB) Sound Level Scale

The Sound Level (\( \beta \)) of a sound wave of intensity \( I \) is defined logarithmically relative to the reference threshold of human hearing (\( I_0 \)):

Sound Intensity Level in Decibels: \[ \beta = 10 \log_{10} \left( \frac{I}{I_0} \right) \quad [\text{dB}] \]

where \( I_0 = 10^{-12} \text{ W/m}^2 \) is the standard Threshold of Hearing at \( 1000 \text{ Hz} \).

Useful Decibel Relationships:
  • If sound intensity doubles (\( I \to 2I \)): \( \Delta\beta = 10\log_{10}(2) \approx \mathbf{+3.01 \text{ dB}} \).
  • If sound intensity increases by a factor of 10 (\( I \to 10I \)): \( \Delta\beta = 10\log_{10}(10) = \mathbf{+10 \text{ dB}} \).
  • If sound intensity increases by a factor of 100 (\( I \to 100I \)): \( \Delta\beta = \mathbf{+20 \text{ dB}} \).

Sound Level Addition for Incoherent and Coherent Sources

1. Superposition of Incoherent Sources (Independent Sounds)

When multiple independent, random sound sources (such as multiple people speaking or independent engines) sound simultaneously, their physical intensities add directly:

\[ I_{\text{total}} = I_1 + I_2 + \dots + I_n = \sum_{i=1}^n I_i \]

For \( n \) identical, independent sources each having sound level \( \beta_1 \):

\[ \beta_n = 10 \log_{10}\left( \frac{n I_1}{I_0} \right) = \beta_1 + 10 \log_{10}(n) \]

Solved JEE Problem: Decibel Addition

Problem: A single person speaking normally produces a sound level of \( 60 \text{ dB} \) at a certain distance. If \( 100 \) identical people speak simultaneously from the same location, what is the resulting sound level?

Solution:

\[ \beta_{100} = \beta_1 + 10\log_{10}(100) = 60 + 10(2) = 60 + 20 = 80 \text{ dB} \]
