Molecular Model and Postulates

The Molecular Model and its foundational assumptions.

Concept

All studies in physics are either about matter or fields interacting in space and time as per conservation laws. Hence, a model is very important.

On its own, matter has different properties like mass (\(m\)), charge (\(q\)), temperature (\(T\)), etc. But depending on what dynamics (processes/behaviour) is being studied, we highlight the relevant properties and ignore the others.

In thermodynamics, we study the behaviour of bulk matter in various thermodynamic processes.

Bulk matter behaviour mainly concerns elasticity (mechanical behaviour) and heat transfer. Temperature and elastic moduli are both bulk matter properties.

In mechanics, we studied point masses or perfectly rigid bodies, for which temperature and elasticity were considered irrelevant. A point mass cannot have temperature or elasticity.

For perfectly rigid bodies, we did not care what was inside the body. We assumed it was made of the same perfectly rigid material throughout.

It's like meeting a person in real life.
We don't really care how his/her internal organs look.

But for bulk matter, what it is made of becomes relevant. A copper rod is a better conductor of heat than a wooden rod.

In the elasticity chapter, we studied that when we compress a solid rod, stress develops in the rod. This is due to intermolecular interactions getting disturbed. We also learned about thermal expansion and thermal stress.

In gases, the bulk response to compression is described by the bulk modulus (\(\beta\)), while compressibility is its reciprocal. Unlike a solid rod, a gas at equilibrium cannot sustain longitudinal tensile stress.

Why do we struggle to pull the plunger of a sealed syringe?
When we pull the plunger outward, the gas inside expands and its pressure decreases.
If the pressure inside becomes lower than atmospheric pressure, the greater external pressure exerts a net force inward on the plunger, resisting our pull.
The gas is not pulling the plunger through tensile stress.
The resistance arises from the difference between the external atmospheric pressure and the internal gas pressure.
If we pull a sealed syringe in vacuum, we can do it with no effort.

Through KTG, we postulate why gases behave this way at the molecular level. We explain the thermal as well as mechanical behaviour.

Macroscopic vs Microscopic Perspective

As bulk matter is involved, we are concerned with what the sample of matter is from the inside as well as from the outside. So we have developed two perspectives to study bulk matter based on the level of defining its properties.

The physical state of a gas can be described from two fundamentally different philosophical and scientific perspectives:

  • The Macroscopic Perspective
  • The Microscopic Perspective

The Macroscopic Perspective :

It treats the gas as a continuous medium without reference to its molecular structure.

The thermodynamic state of the system is described using macroscopic properties such as pressure \( P \), volume \( V \) and temperature \( T \).

These quantities are directly measured by laboratory instruments like pressure gauges, thermometers and manometers.

The Microscopic Perspective :

Recognizes that a macroscopic volume of gas contains an astronomical number of discrete particles (\(\sim 10^{23}\) molecules per mole).

It tracks their mechanical properties (mass \( m \), velocity \( \vec{v} \), momentum \( \vec{p} \), kinetic energy \( E_k \)) using Newtonian mechanics and statistical distributions.

The existence of two levels of perspective is not about one ignoring the other.
As a matter of fact, its quite the opposite. The idea is to relate the macroscopic and microscopic parameters.
The primary achievement of the Kinetic Theory of Gases is establishing the connection between macroscopic properties (\(P,T,U\)) and the underlying microscopic dynamics through statistical reasoning.
A remarkable historical example is Josef Loschmidt, who in 1865 used kinetic theory, along with measurements related to gas density and mean free path, to obtain the first reasonable estimate of molecular diameter.
This demonstrated how the microscopic size of molecules could be estimated from macroscopic measurements.

Postulates of KTG

Why do we need postulates at all? We want to construct an internal model of gaseous bulk matter even though we cannot directly observe the complete microscopic state of the gas. We can measure macroscopic properties such as pressure, volume and temperature, but the behaviour of individual molecules is not directly accessible through ordinary macroscopic measurements.

We therefore make certain assumptions about the molecular constitution and behaviour of gases. Using these assumptions, we develop a microscopic model that can explain the observed macroscopic behaviour. The usefulness of the model lies in how successfully its predictions agree with experimental observations.

The kinetic theory of gases (KTG), in its classical ideal-gas model, is established on the following physical postulates.

  1. Discrete Composition:

    A gas consists of an extremely large number of tiny, discrete particles called molecules. In a monoatomic gas, the constituent particles are individual atoms.

    For a pure gas, we initially consider all the molecules to be identical, having the same molecular mass \(m\). Instead of treating the gas as an indivisible continuous substance, we model it as a collection of individual particles.

    A macroscopic sample contains an enormous number of these particles. One mole of a substance contains approximately \(6.022\times10^{23}\) constituent particles, which explains why the collective behaviour of the gas can appear continuous at the macroscopic level.

  2. Point-Mass Approximation:

    The dimensions of individual molecules are extremely small compared with the average distance separating them. Consequently, the total volume actually occupied by the molecules is negligible compared with the volume of the container:

    \[ V_{\text{molecules,total}} \ll V_{\text{container}} \]

    This allows us to treat the molecules approximately as point particles when studying the distribution of gas throughout the container.

    However, negligible molecular volume does not mean that collisions between molecules are irrelevant. The effective molecular dimensions still matter when describing collisions and estimating the mean free path. The approximation is that the volume occupied by the molecules is negligible on the scale of the container.

  3. Perpetual Random Motion:

    The molecules of a gas are in continuous, random motion. They move in different directions with a distribution of speeds, and their velocities change as a result of collisions.

    For a gas in equilibrium, there is no preferred direction of molecular motion in the macroscopic rest frame. This property is called isotropy. Although individual molecular velocities point in different directions, the average velocity of random motion is zero:

    \[ \langle \vec v\rangle=\vec 0 \]

    This does not mean that individual molecules are stationary. It means that their random motions cancel on average, so the gas has no net bulk flow in its equilibrium rest frame.

    Does random motion mean that all the air molecules in my lungs can randomly move out and make me choke?
    No. Random molecular motion does not mean that all molecules may collectively move in one direction.
    Molecules may cross a boundary individually, but in conditions of equilibrium, without a pressure or concentration gradient driving a net flow, random motion does not produce a sustained one-way movement of the gas.
  4. Newtonian Mechanics:

    The motion of individual gas molecules is described using classical Newtonian mechanics. Each molecule possesses mass, velocity and momentum, and its motion changes when a force acts on it.

    Between collisions, an ideal-gas molecule moves approximately in a straight line with constant velocity. During a collision, its momentum and velocity may change. We use these changes in molecular momentum to explain macroscopic effects such as gas pressure.

    This postulate assumes that classical mechanics provides an adequate description of the molecular motion under the conditions being considered.

  5. Perfectly Elastic Collisions:

    Collisions between gas molecules and collisions between molecules and the container walls are assumed to be perfectly elastic. The total kinetic energy of the colliding particles is conserved during a collision.

    For a molecule colliding elastically with a stationary, rigid wall, the component of velocity perpendicular to the wall reverses direction, while the parallel components remain unchanged.

    Thus, the molecule transfers momentum to the wall without losing kinetic energy in the collision. The cumulative momentum transferred by an enormous number of molecular collisions produces the macroscopic pressure exerted by the gas.

  6. Negligible Collision Duration:

    The time during which a collision takes place is assumed to be negligibly small compared with the time a molecule spends travelling freely between successive collisions.

    Consequently, molecular motion can be described approximately as a sequence of free flights interrupted by brief collisions. During a free flight, the molecule travels without significant intermolecular forces acting on it.

    This approximation makes it easier to calculate molecular motion, collision frequency and mean free path.

  7. Negligible Intermolecular Forces:

    In the ideal-gas model, attractive and repulsive intermolecular interactions are neglected during the free motion of molecules. Intermolecular interactions are relevant only during the brief collision events represented in the model.

    As a result, the intermolecular interaction potential energy is taken to be zero or negligible:

    \[ U_{\text{intermolecular}}\approx 0 \]

    This assumption allows us to relate the internal energy of an ideal gas to the energies of its individual molecules, without including intermolecular interaction energy.

    For a monoatomic ideal gas in the simplest classical model, the internal energy consists entirely of the translational kinetic energies of the molecules:

    \[ U=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac12 m v_i^2 \]

    For polyatomic ideal gases, rotational and vibrational molecular energies may also contribute to internal energy. Neglecting intermolecular potential energy does not imply that the internal energy of every molecule consists exclusively of translational kinetic energy.

  8. Negligible Gravitational Effects:

    Under ordinary laboratory conditions, the gravitational potential-energy difference of a molecule across the height of a typical container is generally negligible compared with its characteristic thermal energy.

    For a molecule of mass \(m\) in a container of characteristic vertical height \(H\), this condition can be expressed as

    \[ mgH\ll k_B T \]

    where \(k_B\) is Boltzmann's constant and \(T\) is the absolute temperature.

    Under this approximation, gravity produces a negligible variation in the molecular distribution across the container. We can therefore ignore gravitational effects when developing the basic kinetic theory of gases.

Scope of the model
These postulates describe an idealized gas, not every property of every real gas under all conditions.
Real molecules have finite dimensions and interact with one another.
KTG uses this simplified molecular model to connect the microscopic motion of particles with the macroscopic behaviour of gases.
And in Physics Thermodynamics syllabus, we only have ideal gas.

Molecular Model of an Ideal Gas

In the framework of the Kinetic Theory of Gases (KTG), we do not measure a gas merely by its total bulk mass or volume as we do with solids and liquids.

Instead, we define the amount of substance, a concept originally developed in chemistry to track chemical combinations.

How the "Mole" was born:
Early chemists discovered that a Helium atom is roughly four times heavier than a Hydrogen atom (giving Hydrogen an atomic weight of 1, and Helium 4).
They reasoned that if they scaled these atomic weights directly into grams, the resulting bulk samples would contain the exact same number of atoms.
Therefore, 1 gram of Hydrogen atom sample contains the same number of particles as 4 grams of Helium atom sample.
They called this specific, massive quantity of particles a "mole" by scaling the relative weights to grams.

Key Parameters of the Ideal Gas Model

To link the microscopic motion of individual particles to the macroscopic bulk of a gas, we rely on a specific set of parameters:

  • Avogadro's Number (\(N_A\)): The bridge between the microscopic and macroscopic worlds. It defines number of particles in one mole. \[ N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23} \, \text{mol}^{-1} \]
  • Molar Mass (\(M\)): The total mass of exactly one mole of molecules. The standard SI unit is \(\text{kg/mol}\). \[ M = m \cdot N_A \]
    In physics, we do not calculate Molar Mass from scratch.
    We simply borrow it from chemistry.
    You just take the chemical molecular weight (based on the periodic table) and attach the SI unit (\(\times 10^{-3} \, \text{kg/mol}\)).
    For example, chemistry tells us the atomic weight of Nitrogen is 14, so Nitrogen gas (\(N_2\)) has a molecular weight of 28.
    In KTG, we just take that 28 and write \(M = 28 \times 10^{-3} \, \text{kg/mol}\) for molar mass of Nitrogen gas.
  • Molecular Mass (\(m\)): The mass of one single, individual molecule: \[ m = \frac{M}{N_A} \]

    where \( M \) is the molar mass and \( N_A\) is Avogadro's number.

  • Total Number of Molecules (\( N \)): The absolute count of molecules in a given sample. If total mass of the gas sample (\(m_{\text{gas}}\)) and its molar mass (\(M\)) is known, we can find the number of moles (\(n\)). \[ N = n \cdot N_A \quad \text{where} \quad n = \frac{m_{\text{gas}}}{M} \]
  • Number Density (\( n_0 \)): A measure of how "crowded" the molecules are in the container, defined as the number of molecules per unit volume. It is often more useful in KTG than standard mass density (\(\rho\)). \[ n_0 = \frac{N}{V} = \frac{\rho}{m} \]

    (SI Unit: \(\text{m}^{-3}\))

Conceptual Example

At Standard Temperature and Pressure (STP: \( T = 273.15 \, \text{K}, P = 1.013 \times 10^5 \, \text{Pa} \)), the volume of \( 1 \, \text{mole} \) of gas is \( 22.4 \, \text{litres} = 0.0224 \, \text{m}^3 \).Estimate the fraction of volume occupied by the molecules themselves if the molecular radius is \( r \approx 10^{-10} \, \text{m} \).

Solution:

Volume occupied by \( N_A \) molecules:

\[ V_{\text{actual}} = N_A \times \left( \frac{4}{3} \pi r^3 \right) = (6.022 \times 10^{23}) \times \left( \frac{4}{3} \pi (10^{-10})^3 \right) \approx 2.52 \times 10^{-6} \, \text{m}^3 \]

Fraction of container volume occupied by molecules:

\[ \frac{V_{\text{actual}}}{V_{\text{total}}} = \frac{2.52 \times 10^{-6}}{0.0224} \approx 1.1 \times 10^{-4} \approx 0.01\% \]

The molecules occupy only about \( 0.01\% \) of the container space at STP, thoroughly validating the point-mass postulate under standard conditions.
