Mean Free Path & Molecular Collisions

Collision cross-sections, kinetic derivation of mean free path, parametric variations, and transport phenomena.

Concept of Molecular Diameter and Collision Cross-Section

Although ideal gas molecules are treated as point masses in early KTG derivations, real molecules possess a finite physical size. When two spherical molecules, each of effective hard-sphere diameter \( d \), approach each other, a collision occurs whenever the center-to-center distance between them becomes less than or equal to \( d \).

The circular area of radius \( d \) centered on a moving molecule is its Collision Cross-Section (\( \sigma \)):

\[ \sigma = \pi d^2 \] where \( d \) is the effective molecular diameter (typically \( \sim 10^{-10} \, \text{m} = 1 \, \text{Å} \)).

Collision Frequency and Collision Rate

Let \( n = N/V \) be the molecular number density (molecules per unit volume).

If a single molecule moves with average speed \( v_{\text{avg}} \) for a time interval \( \Delta t \), it sweeps out an imaginary cylindrical volume of cross-sectional area \( \sigma = \pi d^2 \) and length \( v_{\text{avg}} \Delta t \):

\[ V_{\text{swept}} = (\pi d^2) (v_{\text{avg}} \Delta t) \]

The single molecule will collide with every other molecule whose center lies within this swept volume. The number of collisions it experiences in time \( \Delta t \) is:

\[ \text{Collisions} = n \cdot V_{\text{swept}} = n \, (\pi d^2) \, v_{\text{avg}} \, \Delta t \]

The Collision Frequency (\( Z_1 \)) for a single molecule (collisions per second per molecule):

\[ Z_1 = \frac{\text{Collisions}}{\Delta t} = \pi n d^2 v_{\text{avg}} \]

Relative Velocity Correction:

In reality, the target molecules are not stationary; all molecules are in simultaneous random thermal motion. The effective collision speed is the average relative speed between any two colliding molecules:

\[ v_{\text{rel}} = \sqrt{\overline{|\vec{v}_1 - \vec{v}_2|^2}} = \sqrt{\overline{v_1^2} + \overline{v_2^2} - 2\overline{\vec{v}_1\cdot\vec{v}_2}} = \sqrt{\overline{v^2} + \overline{v^2} - 0} = \sqrt{2} \, v_{\text{avg}} \]
Corrected Collision Frequency for a Single Molecule (\( Z_1 \)): \[ Z_1 = \sqrt{2} \pi n d^2 v_{\text{avg}} \] Total Collision Rate per Unit Volume (\( Z_{11} \)): Since each collision involves two molecules, multiplying \( Z_1 \) by \( n \) and dividing by \( 2 \) avoids double-counting: \[ Z_{11} = \frac{1}{2} n Z_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \pi n^2 d^2 v_{\text{avg}} \]

Derivation of the Mean Free Path Formula

The Mean Free Path (\( \lambda \)) is defined as the average distance traversed by a molecule between two successive collisions.

\[ \lambda = \frac{\text{Total distance traveled in time } \Delta t}{\text{Total number of collisions in time } \Delta t} = \frac{v_{\text{avg}} \, \Delta t}{Z_1 \, \Delta t} = \frac{v_{\text{avg}}}{\sqrt{2} \pi n d^2 v_{\text{avg}}} \] \[ \lambda = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2} \pi n d^2} \] where:
  • \( \lambda \): Mean Free Path (SI Unit: meter (\( \text{m} \)))
  • \( n = \frac{N}{V} = \frac{P}{k_B T} \): Number density of gas molecules (\( \text{m}^{-3} \))
  • \( d \): Molecular collision diameter (\( \text{m} \))

In Terms of Macroscopic State Variables (\( P, T \)):

Substituting \( n = \frac{P}{k_B T} \):

\[ \lambda = \frac{k_B T}{\sqrt{2} \pi d^2 P} \] In terms of mass density \( \rho = n m \): \[ \lambda = \frac{m}{\sqrt{2} \pi d^2 \rho} \]

Variation of Mean Free Path with Temperature, Pressure, and Density

Analyzing the dependence of \( \lambda \) across various thermodynamic conditions:

Thermodynamic Condition Constraint Formula Dependence Variation of \( \lambda \)
Constant Volume (Isochoric) \( V = \text{const} \implies n = \frac{N}{V} = \text{const} \) \( \lambda = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\pi n d^2} \) \( \lambda \) is Constant (Independent of \( T \) or \( P \))
Constant Pressure (Isobaric) \( P = \text{const} \) \( \lambda = \frac{k_B T}{\sqrt{2}\pi d^2 P} \) \( \lambda \propto T \) (\( \lambda \) increases with temperature)
Constant Temperature (Isothermal) \( T = \text{const} \) \( \lambda = \frac{k_B T}{\sqrt{2}\pi d^2 P} \) \( \lambda \propto \frac{1}{P} \) (\( \lambda \) decreases with pressure)
Density Variation General \( \lambda = \frac{m}{\sqrt{2}\pi d^2 \rho} \) \( \lambda \propto \frac{1}{\rho} \) (Inversely proportional to density)
Classic JEE Trap Question: If a gas enclosed in a rigid container is heated from \( 300 \, \text{K} \) to \( 600 \, \text{K} \), what happens to its mean free path?
Answer: Because the container is rigid, \( V \) is constant \( \implies n = N/V \) is constant. Therefore, \( \lambda \) remains strictly unchanged! (The molecules move faster and collide more frequently, but the average spatial distance between collisions remains invariant).

Qualitative Overview of Transport Phenomena

When a gas is in a non-equilibrium state, molecular collisions transport physical quantities across layers, generating three fundamental Transport Phenomena:

  • 1. Viscosity (Transport of Momentum): When adjacent layers of gas move with different drift velocities, molecular exchange between layers transports linear momentum, generating an internal shear stress: \[ \eta_{\text{visc}} \approx \frac{1}{3} \rho \, v_{\text{avg}} \, \lambda \propto \sqrt{T} \quad (\text{Independent of pressure at moderate } P) \]
  • 2. Thermal Conduction (Transport of Thermal Energy): When a temperature gradient \( \frac{dT}{dx} \) exists across a gas, energetic molecules colliding with slower molecules transport internal energy: \[ K_{\text{thermal}} \approx \frac{1}{3} \rho \, C_v \, v_{\text{avg}} \, \lambda \propto \sqrt{T} \]
  • 3. Molecular Diffusion (Transport of Mass): When a concentration gradient \( \frac{dn}{dx} \) exists, random molecular migration transports mass: \[ D \approx \frac{1}{3} v_{\text{avg}} \, \lambda \propto \frac{T^{3/2}}{P} \]

Brownian Motion and Evidence for Molecular Reality

First observed by Robert Brown in 1827 (pollen grains suspended in water) and theoretically explained by Albert Einstein in 1905:

Microscopic Mechanism:
Microscopic colloidal particles suspended in a fluid undergo continuous, erratic, zig-zag random motion due to unbalanced statistical bombardment from surrounding fluid molecules.
Because the suspended particle is tiny, the number of molecular collisions on its left and right faces does not balance perfectly at every microsecond, creating a net fluctuating force.

Jean Perrin's experimental verification of Einstein's Brownian motion equations provided the conclusive, undeniable proof of the atomic and molecular reality of matter, earning Perrin the 1926 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Solved Example: Calculating Mean Free Path

Estimate the mean free path of Nitrogen molecules at standard temperature and pressure (\( T = 273 \, \text{K}, P = 1.013 \times 10^5 \, \text{Pa} \)), assuming the molecular diameter is \( d = 2.0 \times 10^{-10} \, \text{m} = 2.0 \, \text{Å} \).

Solution:

Using \( \lambda = \frac{k_B T}{\sqrt{2} \pi d^2 P} \):

\[ \lambda = \frac{(1.38 \times 10^{-23}) \times 273}{\sqrt{2} \times 3.1416 \times (2.0 \times 10^{-10})^2 \times (1.013 \times 10^5)} \] \[ \lambda = \frac{3.7674 \times 10^{-21}}{1.4142 \times 3.1416 \times (4.0 \times 10^{-20}) \times 1.013 \times 10^5} = \frac{3.7674 \times 10^{-21}}{1.800 \times 10^{-14}} \approx \mathbf{2.09 \times 10^{-7} \, \text{m}} \approx 209 \, \text{nm} \]

Comparison: The mean free path (\( \sim 200 \, \text{nm} \)) is roughly \( 1000 \) times larger than the molecular diameter (\( \sim 0.2 \, \text{nm} \)), corroborating the point-mass assumption of kinetic theory.
