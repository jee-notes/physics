Maxwell-Boltzmann Speed Distribution

Statistical mechanics of molecular velocities: probability density functions, peak shifts, and temperature broadening.

Maxwell-Boltzmann Speed Distribution Function

In 1859, James Clerk Maxwell derived the theoretical probability distribution governing the speeds of ideal gas molecules in thermal equilibrium at temperature \( T \).

The number of molecules \( dN \) having speeds between \( v \) and \( v + dv \) out of a total of \( N \) molecules is given by the Maxwell-Boltzmann Speed Distribution Law:

\[ \frac{dN}{dv} = N \, f(v) = 4\pi N \left( \frac{m}{2\pi k_B T} \right)^{3/2} v^2 \exp\left( -\frac{m v^2}{2 k_B T} \right) \] In terms of molar mass \( M = m N_A \) and universal gas constant \( R = N_A k_B \): \[ f(v) = 4\pi \left( \frac{M}{2\pi R T} \right)^{3/2} v^2 e^{-\frac{M v^2}{2 R T}} \] where:
  • \( f(v) = \frac{1}{N} \frac{dN}{dv} \): Speed probability density function (SI Unit: \( \text{s/m} \))
  • \( v \): Molecular speed (in \( \text{m/s} \))
  • \( m \): Mass of a single molecule
  • \( k_B \): Boltzmann constant
  • \( T \): Absolute temperature in Kelvin

Graphical Analysis of the Maxwell Distribution Curve

Plotting the probability density \( f(v) \) versus speed \( v \) yields the characteristic asymmetric Maxwell-Boltzmann curve:

Geometric Properties of the Curve:
  1. Origin Behavior (\( v \to 0 \)): \( f(v) \propto v^2 \). The curve starts at \( (0,0) \) with zero slope and parabolic curvature. Zero molecules have strictly zero speed.
  2. High Speed Behavior (\( v \to \infty \)): As \( v \) becomes large, the exponential decay factor \( e^{-\frac{mv^2}{2k_BT}} \) dominates the polynomial \( v^2 \) factor, causing the curve to approach the horizontal axis asymptotically.
  3. Total Area Under Curve: The total area under the probability density curve is normalized to unity: \[ \int_0^\infty f(v) \, dv = 1 \quad \text{or} \quad \int_0^\infty \left(\frac{dN}{dv}\right) dv = N \quad (\text{Total Molecules}) \]

Physical Significance of Peak and Distribution Tail

  • The Peak (\( v_{\text{mp}} \)): The highest point on the curve represents the Most Probable Speed. Finding the maximum: \[ \frac{df(v)}{dv} = 0 \implies \frac{d}{dv}\left[ v^2 e^{-\frac{mv^2}{2k_BT}} \right] = 0 \implies 2v - \frac{m v^3}{k_BT} = 0 \implies v_{\text{mp}} = \sqrt{\frac{2k_BT}{m}} \]
  • Asymmetric Tail: Because speeds can extend to large positive values but cannot be negative, the curve is skewed to the right. Consequently: \[ v_{\text{mp}} < v_{\text{avg}} < v_{\text{rms}} \]
  • Activation Tail: The high-speed tail contains the energetic molecules capable of surmounting chemical activation barriers or escaping planetary atmospheres (Jeans escape).

Effect of Temperature Changes on Velocity Distribution

When the absolute temperature of a gas is increased from \( T_1 \) to \( T_2 \) (\( T_2 > T_1 \)):

  1. Rightward Shift of Peak: Since \( v_{\text{mp}} = \sqrt{\frac{2RT}{M}} \propto \sqrt{T} \), the peak shifts toward higher speeds.
  2. Decrease in Peak Height: The maximum value of \( f(v) \) is inversely proportional to \( \sqrt{T} \): \[ f(v_{\text{mp}}) \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{T}} \]
  3. Flattening and Broadening: Because the total number of molecules \( N \) (total area under the curve) is strictly conserved, the curve must broaden and flatten out across a wider range of speeds.

Effect of Molecular Mass at Constant Temperature

At a fixed temperature \( T \), lighter gases have higher average and most probable speeds than heavier gases (\( v \propto 1/\sqrt{M} \)):

  • Lighter Gas (e.g., \( \text{H}_2, \text{He} \)): The distribution curve is stretched far to the right, flat, and wide.
  • Heavier Gas (e.g., \( \text{O}_2, \text{CO}_2, \text{Xe} \)): The distribution curve is tall, sharply peaked at low speeds, and narrow.
This explains why light atmospheric gases like Hydrogen and Helium have escaped Earth's gravitational pull into space, while heavier gases (Nitrogen and Oxygen) remain gravitationally bound.

Fraction of Molecules within a Speed Range

The fraction of molecules having speeds in a finite interval between \( v_1 \) and \( v_2 \) is given by the integral:

\[ \frac{\Delta N}{N} = \int_{v_1}^{v_2} f(v) \, dv = \text{Area under the } f(v) \text{ curve between } v = v_1 \text{ and } v = v_2 \]

For a very narrow speed range \( \Delta v \) centered around speed \( v \): \[ \Delta N \approx N \, f(v) \, \Delta v \]

Solved Example: Ratio of Peak Heights at Different Temperatures

A gas sample is heated from \( T_1 = 300 \, \text{K} \) to \( T_2 = 1200 \, \text{K} \).
Find:
(a) The ratio of the most probable speed \( \frac{v_{\text{mp}, 2}}{v_{\text{mp}, 1}} \).
(b) The ratio of the maximum value of the distribution function \( \frac{f(v_{\text{mp}, 2})}{f(v_{\text{mp}, 1})} \).

Solution:

(a) \( v_{\text{mp}} \propto \sqrt{T} \):

\[ \frac{v_{\text{mp}, 2}}{v_{\text{mp}, 1}} = \sqrt{\frac{T_2}{T_1}} = \sqrt{\frac{1200}{300}} = \sqrt{4} = \mathbf{2} \]

(b) Substituting \( v_{\text{mp}} = \sqrt{\frac{2k_BT}{m}} \) into \( f(v) \):

\[ f(v_{\text{mp}}) = 4\pi \left(\frac{m}{2\pi k_BT}\right)^{3/2} \left(\frac{2k_BT}{m}\right) e^{-1} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{\pi} e} \sqrt{\frac{m}{2k_BT}} \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{T}} \] \[ \frac{f(v_{\text{mp}, 2})}{f(v_{\text{mp}, 1})} = \sqrt{\frac{T_1}{T_2}} = \sqrt{\frac{300}{1200}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{4}} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{2}} \]

As temperature quadruples, the most probable speed doubles, and the peak height is halved.
