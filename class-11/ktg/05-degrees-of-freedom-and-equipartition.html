Degrees of Freedom & Law of Equipartition

Energy storage coordinates in molecules, the equipartition theorem, and thermal activation of internal modes.

Concept and Definition of Degrees of Freedom

The Degrees of Freedom (\( f \)) of a dynamic system is defined as the total number of independent coordinates or independent velocity components required to completely specify the position and configuration of all constituent atoms in space.

Equivalently in statistical mechanics, \( f \) represents the total number of independent quadratic terms appearing in the total energy expression of a molecule:

\[ E_{\text{total}} = \sum_{i=1}^f \alpha_i q_i^2 \]

For a molecule consisting of \( N_a \) atoms:

\[ f_{\text{total}} = 3 N_a - k \] where \( N_a \) is the number of atoms in the molecule and \( k \) is the number of fixed geometric constraints (fixed bond lengths or fixed angles).

Translational, Rotational, and Vibrational Degrees of Freedom

The total energy of a molecule is partitioned across three independent mechanical modes:

\[ f_{\text{total}} = f_{\text{trans}} + f_{\text{rot}} + f_{\text{vib}} \]

1. Translational Modes (\( f_{\text{trans}} = 3 \)):

The center of mass of any molecule can translate freely along the three independent Cartesian directions (\( x, y, z \)): \[ E_{\text{trans}} = \frac{1}{2} m v_x^2 + \frac{1}{2} m v_y^2 + \frac{1}{2} m v_z^2 \implies f_{\text{trans}} = 3 \quad \text{(Universal for all gases)} \]

2. Rotational Modes (\( f_{\text{rot}} \)):

  • Linear Molecules: Can rotate about two mutually perpendicular axes orthogonal to the internuclear bond axis. Rotation about the bond axis has an imperceptibly tiny moment of inertia (\( I \approx 0 \)), which is quantum-mechanically inactive at ordinary temperatures. Thus, \( f_{\text{rot}} = 2 \): \[ E_{\text{rot}} = \frac{1}{2} I_x \omega_x^2 + \frac{1}{2} I_y \omega_y^2 \]
  • Non-Linear Molecules: Possess three distinct non-zero principal moments of inertia about three mutually orthogonal axes. Thus, \( f_{\text{rot}} = 3 \): \[ E_{\text{rot}} = \frac{1}{2} I_x \omega_x^2 + \frac{1}{2} I_y \omega_y^2 + \frac{1}{2} I_z \omega_z^2 \]

3. Vibrational Modes (\( f_{\text{vib}} \)):

At elevated temperatures, atoms vibrate periodically about their equilibrium bond distances. Each vibrational mode involves two quadratic terms in the energy (kinetic energy of oscillation + potential energy of bond stretching):

\[ E_{\text{vib, mode}} = \frac{1}{2} \mu \dot{x}^2 + \frac{1}{2} k_{\text{bond}} x^2 \]
Critical Rule for Vibrational Modes:
Each active vibrational mode contributes 2 degrees of freedom (or \( 2 \times \frac{1}{2}k_BT = 1 k_BT \) energy per molecule), unlike translational and rotational modes which contribute only \( 1 \) quadratic term (\( \frac{1}{2}k_BT \)) each!

Temperature Dependence and Activation of Energy Modes

In classical physics, degrees of freedom were expected to be constant at all temperatures. However, quantum mechanics dictates that energy levels of rotation and vibration are discrete (quantized):

  • Low Temperatures (\( T < 100 \, \text{K} \)): Thermal energy \( k_B T \) is insufficient to excite even the first rotational quantum level (\( \Delta E_{\text{rot}} > k_B T \)). Rotation freezes out; the gas behaves as a pure monoatomic gas with \( f = 3 \).
  • Moderate / Room Temperatures (\( 100 \, \text{K} < T < 750 \, \text{K} \)): Rotational modes are fully activated (\( k_B T \gg \Delta E_{\text{rot}} \)), but vibrational energy spacings remain too wide (\( \Delta E_{\text{vib}} \gg k_B T \)). Diatomic gases exhibit \( f = 5 \).
  • High Temperatures (\( T > 1000 \, \text{K} \)): Vibrational modes become thermally accessible (\( k_B T \ge \Delta E_{\text{vib}} \)), unlocking \( f = 7 \) (or higher).

Law of Equipartition of Energy

Formulated by James Clerk Maxwell and generalized by Ludwig Boltzmann, the Law of Equipartition of Energy governs thermal distribution in classical statistical mechanics.

Formal Statement:

For any dynamic system in thermal equilibrium at absolute temperature \( T \), the total internal thermal energy is distributed equally among all active quadratic degrees of freedom, and the average energy associated with each active degree of freedom is exactly equal to:

\[ \overline{\epsilon} = \frac{1}{2} k_B T \quad (\text{per molecule}) = \frac{1}{2} R T \quad (\text{per mole}) \] where:
  • \( k_B \approx 1.38 \times 10^{-23} \, \text{J/K} \): Boltzmann constant
  • \( R \approx 8.314 \, \text{J}/(\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}) \): Universal gas constant
  • \( T \): Absolute temperature in Kelvin

Average Total Energy of a Molecule with \( f \) Active Degrees of Freedom:

\[ \overline{E}_{\text{molecule}} = f \left( \frac{1}{2} k_B T \right) = \frac{f}{2} k_B T \]

Classical Limitations and Quantum Freezing of Degrees of Freedom

The classical equipartition theorem assumes that energy modes can absorb arbitrarily small amounts of continuous energy. This classical assumption led to historic failures:

  1. Specific Heat of Hydrogen at Low Temperatures: Below \( 50 \, \text{K} \), the molar heat capacity of \( \text{H}_2 \) drops from \( \frac{5}{2}R \) to \( \frac{3}{2}R \), exactly matching a monoatomic gas.
  2. Dulong-Petit Law Violations: The molar heat capacity of diamond and silicon at room temperature is far below the classical \( 3R \) prediction because high-frequency lattice vibrations are frozen.
These discrepancies were resolved only by Einstein and Debye's Quantum Theories of Specific Heat, proving that energy is absorbed in discrete quanta \( \hbar \omega \). Below a threshold characteristic temperature \( \Theta = \frac{\hbar \omega}{k_B} \), degrees of freedom "freeze out".

Solved Example: Total Energy Calculation

Calculate the total thermal energy contained in \( 0.5 \, \text{moles} \) of Oxygen gas (\( \text{O}_2 \)) at \( 300 \, \text{K} \) assuming rigid rotator behavior.

Solution:

For rigid diatomic \( \text{O}_2 \) at \( 300 \, \text{K} \), \( f = 5 \) (\( 3 \) translational + \( 2 \) rotational):

\[ U = n \left(\frac{f}{2} R T\right) = (0.5) \left( \frac{5}{2} \times 8.314 \times 300 \right) = 0.5 \times (6235.5) \approx \mathbf{3117.75 \, \text{J}} \approx 3.12 \, \text{kJ} \]
