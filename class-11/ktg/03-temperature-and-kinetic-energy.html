Temperature, Kinetic Energy, & Gas Laws

The molecular interpretation of absolute temperature and the microscopic deduction of empirical gas laws.

Kinetic Interpretation of Absolute Temperature

From the kinetic derivation of pressure, we have:

\[ P V = \frac{1}{3} m N \overline{v^2} = \frac{2}{3} N \left( \frac{1}{2} m \overline{v^2} \right) \]

Comparing this microscopic expression with the macroscopic Ideal Gas Equation of State:

\[ P V = n R T = N k_B T \quad \left(\text{since } k_B = \frac{R}{N_A} \text{ and } N = n N_A\right) \]

Equating the two expressions for \( P V \):

\[ \frac{2}{3} N \left( \frac{1}{2} m \overline{v^2} \right) = N k_B T \]
\[ \overline{E_k} = \frac{1}{2} m \overline{v^2} = \frac{3}{2} k_B T \] where:
  • \( \overline{E_k} \): Average translational kinetic energy per molecule (SI Unit: Joule (\( \text{J} \)))
  • \( k_B \approx 1.3806 \times 10^{-23} \, \text{J/K} \): Boltzmann Constant (Dimension: \( [M L^2 T^{-2} K^{-1}] \))
  • \( T \): Absolute temperature of the gas on the Kelvin scale (SI Unit: Kelvin (\( \text{K} \)))
Profounds Physical Insight:
Absolute temperature \( T \) is directly proportional to the average translational kinetic energy of the gas molecules: \[ T = \frac{2}{3 k_B} \left( \frac{1}{2} m \overline{v^2} \right) \] Temperature is NOT a property of an isolated single molecule; it is a statistical ensemble average over many particles. Two different gases (e.g., light Helium and heavy Xenon) at the same temperature have identical average translational kinetic energy per molecule, even though their individual molecular speeds differ greatly!

Average Translational Kinetic Energy per Molecule

Because molecular motion occurs in three-dimensional space with three independent translational degrees of freedom (\( x, y, z \)):

\[ \overline{E_k} = \frac{1}{2} m \overline{v_x^2} + \frac{1}{2} m \overline{v_y^2} + \frac{1}{2} m \overline{v_z^2} = \frac{3}{2} k_B T \]

By spatial isotropy, the average kinetic energy associated with motion along any single Cartesian axis is:

\[ \overline{E_{kx}} = \frac{1}{2} m \overline{v_x^2} = \frac{1}{2} k_B T, \quad \overline{E_{ky}} = \frac{1}{2} k_B T, \quad \overline{E_{kz}} = \frac{1}{2} k_B T \]

Average Kinetic Energy per Mole of Gas

For one mole of an ideal gas containing Avogadro's number \( N_A \) of molecules, multiplying the average molecular kinetic energy by \( N_A \) yields:

\[ E_{\text{trans, molar}} = N_A \times \left( \frac{3}{2} k_B T \right) = \frac{3}{2} (N_A k_B) T = \frac{3}{2} R T \] where \( R = N_A k_B \approx 8.314 \, \text{J}/(\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}) \) is the Universal Gas Constant.

For \( n \) moles of an ideal gas:

\[ E_{\text{trans, total}} = \frac{3}{2} n R T \]

Average Translational Kinetic Energy per Unit Mass (Specific Kinetic Energy):

\[ E_{\text{trans, mass}} = \frac{E_{\text{trans, molar}}}{M} = \frac{3 R T}{2 M} = \frac{3}{2} r T \] where \( M \) is the molar mass (in \( \text{kg/mol} \)) and \( r = R/M \) is the characteristic gas constant.

Physical Meaning of Absolute Zero in Classical KTG

Setting \( T = 0 \, \text{K} \) in the fundamental relation:

\[ \frac{1}{2} m \overline{v^2} = \frac{3}{2} k_B (0) = 0 \implies \overline{v^2} = 0 \implies v_{\text{rms}} = 0 \]
In the framework of classical kinetic theory, Absolute Zero (\( 0 \, \text{K} \)) is the absolute floor of temperature at which all translational molecular motion ceases entirely, and the pressure of an ideal gas drops to zero.
(Quantum Note: In quantum mechanics, zero-point vibrational energy persists at \( 0 \, \text{K} \) due to Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle, but classical thermal motion vanishes).

Microscopic Derivation of Gas Laws

Starting purely from the kinetic equation \( P = \frac{1}{3} \frac{m N}{V} \overline{v^2} = \frac{2}{3} \frac{N}{V} \left( \frac{1}{2} m \overline{v^2} \right) = \frac{2}{3} \frac{N}{V} \left(\frac{3}{2} k_B T\right) \), all classical empirical gas laws follow rigorously:

1. Boyle's Law (\( T = \text{constant}, n = \text{constant} \)):

Since \( T \) is constant, \( \overline{v^2} = \text{constant} \). Therefore: \[ P V = \frac{2}{3} N \left(\frac{3}{2} k_B T\right) = \text{constant} \implies P_1 V_1 = P_2 V_2 \]

2. Charles's Law (\( P = \text{constant}, n = \text{constant} \)):

\[ V = \left( \frac{N k_B}{P} \right) T \implies V \propto T \implies \frac{V_1}{T_1} = \frac{V_2}{T_2} \]

3. Gay-Lussac's Law (\( V = \text{constant}, n = \text{constant} \)):

\[ P = \left( \frac{N k_B}{V} \right) T \implies P \propto T \implies \frac{P_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2}{T_2} \]

4. Avogadro's Law (Equal \( P, V, T \)):

Consider two different gases \( 1 \) and \( 2 \) at the same \( P, V, T \): \[ P_1 = \frac{1}{3} \frac{m_1 N_1}{V} \overline{v_1^2}, \qquad P_2 = \frac{1}{3} \frac{m_2 N_2}{V} \overline{v_2^2} \] Since \( P_1 = P_2 \implies m_1 N_1 \overline{v_1^2} = m_2 N_2 \overline{v_2^2} \).
Since \( T_1 = T_2 \implies \frac{1}{2} m_1 \overline{v_1^2} = \frac{1}{2} m_2 \overline{v_2^2} \).
Dividing the two equations:

\[ N_1 = N_2 \] Equal volumes of all gases under identical temperature and pressure contain an identical number of molecules!

5. Dalton's Law of Partial Pressures:

For a mixture of non-reacting gases with molecular counts \( N_1, N_2, \dots \) and masses \( m_1, m_2, \dots \) in volume \( V \) at temperature \( T \): \[ P_{\text{total}} = \frac{1}{3V} \left( m_1 N_1 \overline{v_1^2} + m_2 N_2 \overline{v_2^2} + \dots \right) = \frac{k_B T}{V} (N_1 + N_2 + \dots) = P_1 + P_2 + \dots \]

Solved Example: Ratio of Molecular Energies

Vessel \( A \) contains \( 1 \, \text{g} \) of Hydrogen (\( M = 2 \, \text{g/mol} \)) at \( 300 \, \text{K} \), and Vessel \( B \) contains \( 1 \, \text{g} \) of Oxygen (\( M = 32 \, \text{g/mol} \)) at \( 600 \, \text{K} \).
Find:
(a) The ratio of average kinetic energy per molecule between Hydrogen and Oxygen.
(b) The ratio of total translational kinetic energy of the gas in Vessel \( A \) to Vessel \( B \).

Solution:

(a) Average kinetic energy per molecule depends solely on temperature (\( \overline{E_k} = \frac{3}{2} k_B T \)):

\[ \frac{\overline{E_{k, \text{H}_2}}}{\overline{E_{k, \text{O}_2}}} = \frac{T_{\text{H}_2}}{T_{\text{O}_2}} = \frac{300 \, \text{K}}{600 \, \text{K}} = \frac{1}{2} \]

(b) Total translational kinetic energy is \( E_{\text{total}} = \frac{3}{2} n R T = \frac{3}{2} \left(\frac{m_{\text{sample}}}{M}\right) R T \):

\[ \frac{E_{\text{total, H}_2}}{E_{\text{total, O}_2}} = \frac{\frac{m_H}{M_H} T_H}{\frac{m_O}{M_O} T_O} = \frac{\frac{1}{2} \times 300}{\frac{1}{32} \times 600} = \frac{150}{\frac{600}{32}} = \frac{150 \times 32}{600} = \frac{32}{4} = 8 \]

Total translational kinetic energy in the Hydrogen vessel is \( 8 \) times greater due to the much higher mole count of Hydrogen for the same mass.
