Elasticity

How materials respond to applied forces.

Why this Chapter Matters?

In mechanics so far, we quietly made a powerful but unrealistic assumption — that material bodies do not deform under applied forces. This idealization is called rigid body dynamics.

In reality, every applied force on the material body produces two effects:

  • Change in motion — the driving effect.
  • Change in shape — the deforming effect.

Even materials like steel deform under force — the deformation is simply too small to notice in everyday situations.

Elasticity is the hidden layer of mechanics that determines whether structures withstand forces or fail.

From bridges to machine components like rails and axles, understanding elasticity is essential for connecting ideal physics to real-world behavior.

So far, mechanics has largely focused on how forces produce motion. But in many real situations, the goal is the opposite — to ensure that bodies remain stable and do not fail under applied forces.

The idea of a perfectly rigid body is only an approximation. In reality, all materials deform when forces act on them — some visibly, others imperceptibly.

These deformations are not just side effects; they play a critical role in determining whether a structure remains intact or breaks.

From flexible airplane wings to earthquake-resistant buildings, materials must deform in a controlled way — enough to absorb forces, but not enough to fail.

Elasticity is the framework that allows us to understand and predict this behavior through concepts like stress, strain, and elastic moduli.

Conceptual Overview

When an external force acts on a body, it can change its dimensions (shape and size). This change is called deformation.

In response, internal restoring forces develop within the material body across its entire bulk volume. These forces oppose deformation and attempt to bring the body back to its original configuration.

The ability of a material body to resist deformation and recover its original shape after the removal of external forces is called elasticity. Thus, elasticity is the study of these internal restoring effects.

Unlike rigid body mechanics, we now treat objects as deformable systems. Here, both geometry and material properties together determine how a material body responds to applied stress.

Hooke’s Law provides the first approximation — within the elastic limit, deformation varies linearly with applied force. Beyond this limit, the response becomes non-linear and eventual failure occurs.

Thus, elasticity is not just about deformation — it is about limits, stability, and material behavior under stress.
