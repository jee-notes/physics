Elasticity

How materials respond to applied forces.

Why this Chapter Matters?

In mechanics so far, we have modelled everything on the basis of an unrealistic assumption — that material bodies do not deform under applied forces. This idealization is called rigid body dynamics.

In reality, every applied force on an actual real life material body produces two effects:

  • Change in motion — the driving effect.
  • Change in shape — the deforming effect.

Even materials like steel, which are considered to be among the strongest and the hardest, deform under force. It's just that the deformation is simply too small to notice in everyday situations.

Every contact force interaction between bodies causes deformation—like in cricket, when the bat hits the ball, temporary deformation occurs in both bodies.

Elasticity is the branch of mechanics that determines whether structures withstand forces or fail.

Understanding elasticity is essential for connecting ideal physics to real-world behavior because deformation is entirely inevitable.

From bridges and machine components like rails and axles to flexible airplane wings and earthquake-resistant buildings, materials must be designed to deform in a controlled way — enough to absorb external forces, but safely within limits that ensure structures remain stable and do not break or fail catastrophically.

Elasticity is the precise framework that allows us to understand, quantify, and predict this critical structural behavior through concepts like stress, strain, and elastic moduli.

Conceptual Overview

When an external force acts on a body, it can change its geometric form (shape and size). This structural change is called deformation.

In response to the deformation, internal restoring forces develop within the material body across its entire bulk volume to oppose the deformation and attempt to bring the body back to its original geometric configuration.

This original geometric form is sought not because it is "remembered" by the material, but because the undeformed state represents the system's most stable configuration of minimum potential energy.

The ability of a material body to resist deformation and completely recover its original shape after the removal of external forces is called elasticity. Thus, elasticity is fundamentally the study of these internal restoring effects.

Unlike rigid body mechanics, we now treat objects as deformable systems where both geometry and intrinsic material properties together determine how a body responds to applied stress.

Hooke’s Law provides our first linear approximation—within the elastic limit, deformation varies linearly with the applied force. Beyond this limit, the response becomes non-linear, permanent deformation sets in, and eventual structural failure occurs.

Thus, elasticity is not just about the mechanics of deformation — it is the study of operational limits, structural stability, and material behavior under stress.
