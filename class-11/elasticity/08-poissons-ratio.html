Poisson's Ratio

Relating axial elongation to lateral contraction.

Longitudinal vs Lateral Strain

When a wire or rod is pulled, it doesn't just get longer; it also gets thinner. This is because materials generally attempt to maintain their volume to some extent.

  • Longitudinal Strain (\(\varepsilon_L\)): The fractional change in length. \(\varepsilon_L = \Delta L / L\).
  • Lateral Strain (\(\varepsilon_d\)): The fractional change in diameter or width. \(\varepsilon_d = \Delta d / d\).
Notice that if length increases (\(\Delta L > 0\)), diameter decreases (\(\Delta d < 0\)). Thus, longitudinal and lateral strains typically have opposite signs in natural materials.

Poisson's Ratio Definition (\(\sigma\))

Poisson's Ratio is defined as the ratio of lateral strain to longitudinal strain within the elastic limit.

\[ \sigma = -\frac{\text{Lateral Strain}}{\text{Longitudinal Strain}} = -\frac{\Delta d / d}{\Delta L / L} \]

Since it is the ratio of two strains (both dimensionless), Poisson's Ratio is also a **dimensionless and unitless quantity**. It effectively describes how "squishy" or "rubbery" a material is in response to being pulled.

Theoretical and Practical Limits

The theoretical value of Poisson's Ratio (\(\sigma\)) for an isotropic solid lies between: \[ -1 \leq \sigma \leq 0.5 \]

Physical Significance of the Limits:

  • \(\sigma = 0.5\): This represents a perfectly incompressible material (volume remains exactly constant during deformation). Rubber is very close to this value (\(\sigma \approx 0.49\)).
  • \(\sigma = 0\): This represents a material that does not contract laterally when pulled longitudinally. Cork is a classic example of this behavior.
  • Negative \(\sigma\): These are called auxetic materials. When you pull them, they actually get thicker. Such materials are usually specially engineered foams or honeycombs; they are rarely found in simple natural solids.
For most common engineering materials (steels, aluminum, etc.), the practical value of \(\sigma\) is around **0.25 to 0.35**.
