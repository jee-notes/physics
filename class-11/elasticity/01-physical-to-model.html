Model of Elasticity

Understanding elasticity of what?

From Ideal to Real Material Bodies

So far, we have used simplified models of matter to study mechanics:

  • Particle Dynamics : The body upon which forces are acting is treated as a point. Only the net force matters; internal distribution of forces is ignored.
  • Rigid Body Dynamics : The extended body upon which forces are acting is treated as perfectly rigid. Forces can act at different points, introducing torque. For equilibrium, both net force and net torque must be zero — but the body is assumed not to deform.

These models are sufficient to study motion and equilibrium. However, they do not describe what happens inside a material body.

In reality, external forces and torques are balanced by internal forces distributed throughout the body. These internal forces can also change the shape or size of the body.

To study this internal response and resulting deformation, we must go beyond perfectly rigid bodies.

Limitations of the Rigid Body Assumption

In practical situations, forces are usually applied through contact between bodies — by pushing or pulling. These contact forces act over surfaces and generate internal force distributions within the material.

Thus, even when a body is in equilibrium, internal forces are continuously present throughout it, making deformation an inherent aspect of real material behavior.

Real material bodies cannot maintain perfect rigidity. Under force, their shape or size changes — even if the change is very small.

To understand this behavior, we must study how internal forces produce deformation throughout the body.

This is also called as study of mechanical properties of bulk matter — how materials respond to deformation.

Material and Form

When an external force acts on an extended body, the resulting deformation depends on two distinct factors:

  • Material — the internal structure of matter, which resists deformation.
  • Form (Geometry) — the size and shape of the body, which determine how deformation appears.

At a microscopic level, a material can be modeled as a collection of particles connected by elastic interactions — called as spring-ball model.

When the body is deformed, these inter-particle separations change, and restoring forces arise due to intermolecular interactions. The strength of these restoring forces determines how strongly the material resists deformation.

Material response originates from intermolecular forces.
Stronger interactions → greater resistance to deformation.

Form, on the other hand, is a way to describe how the material body occupies space — also called as its geometric configuration.

Form has two independent aspects:
  • Shape
  • Size

The same material can deform differently depending on its geometry, and different materials respond differently even with identical geometry.

Thus, deformation is governed jointly by:

  • Material — sets the internal resistance.
  • Geometry — determines how that resistance translates into actual deformation.
Deformation is a geometric change;
Elastic modulus isolates the material response.

Continuum Model of Matter

Internal forces are not concentrated at a single point — they are distributed throughout the body.

To describe this distribution, we treat matter as a continuous medium instead of a collection of discrete particles.

This allows physical quantities like stress and strain to be defined at every point inside the body.

The continuum model assumes:

  • Continuity — no gaps in matter.
  • Homogeneity — properties are uniform throughout.
  • Isotropy — properties are identical in all directions.

These are idealizations. In reality, materials may satisfy some of these assumptions but not all.

For example, graphite is homogeneous but not isotropic. Its internal structure leads to different responses along different directions, so the resulting stress and deformation need not be uniform in all directions.

Thus, the continuum model provides a framework, while the actual material behavior depends on how closely these assumptions are satisfied.

Cause of Elasticity

Elastic behavior arises from the internal structure of materials. The underlying mechanism depends on how the constituents of the material interact.

Solids (Intermolecular Forces)

In a solid, atoms or molecules are arranged in a stable configuration where each particle is in equilibrium under forces due to its neighbors. These interactions are called intermolecular forces.

When no external force is applied, each particle remains at its equilibrium position and the internal forces are balanced. This is the state of minimum potential energy.

When a deforming force is applied, the relative positions of particles change — they either move closer or farther apart. This disturbs the equilibrium configuration and alters the intermolecular forces.

As a result, restoring forces develop, which tend to bring the particles back to their original positions. When the external force is removed, these restoring forces return the body to its original shape.

Fluids (No Shape Restoration)

In fluids, particles are not fixed in position — they can move relative to each other.

As a result, fluids cannot sustain restoring forces against shear deformation. When a shear force is applied, the fluid continues to flow instead of returning to its original shape.

However, fluids do resist changes in volume. When compressed or expanded, intermolecular forces generate restoring effects, leading to bulk elasticity.

Rubber (Polymer Chain Mechanism)

In materials like rubber, elasticity arises from a different mechanism. Rubber consists of long, flexible polymer chains that are randomly coiled in the natural state.

When stretched, these chains uncoil and align in the direction of the applied force. When the force is removed, the chains tend to return to their original coiled configuration, producing a restoring effect.

Solids resist both shape and volume change.
Fluids resist volume change but not shape change.
Different materials exhibit elasticity through different microscopic mechanisms.
