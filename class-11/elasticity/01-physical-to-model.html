Model of Elasticity

Changing the Mechanical Model of Matter

From Ideal to Real Material Bodies

So far in mechanics, we have relied on simplified, idealized models of matter to analyze physical systems. While these abstractions do not exist in reality, they are intentionally structured to make mathematical analysis easier.

  • Particle Dynamics: The body experiencing external forces is treated as a geometric point. Only the net vector force matters; the internal distribution of forces and the physical dimensions of the body are completely ignored.
  • Rigid Body Dynamics: The object is treated as an extended body with a fixed geometric form. Forces can act at distinct spatial coordinates, introducing torque. For complete mechanical equilibrium, both net external force and net torque must vanish — yet the body itself is assumed to be absolutely undeformable.

While these models are ideal for studying motion of systems and external equilibrium, they fail to describe the physical realities occurring inside a material body.

In actual physical systems, external forces and torques do not act on abstract unyielding boundaries; they are transmitted and balanced by internal forces distributed throughout the bulk volume.

These internal distributed forces are described by stress, and they are directly responsible for altering the geometric form of the body. This resulting geometric deformation is quantified by strain. In this chapter, we need to study the interplay between stress and strain, and so we must give up the idea of rigid body idealization.

Limitations of the Rigid Body Assumption

In practical scenarios, forces are applied via physical contact over finite boundary surfaces. These contact interactions generate localized stress fields that propagate internally.

Consequently, even when a body achieves absolute static equilibrium (where the net external force is zero), intense internal forces remain continuously active throughout its structure, making deformation an intrinsic property of real material behavior.

In medieval times, there was a form of punishment in which the arms and legs of a criminal were tied to horses pulling in opposite directions. Even if the net external force on the body is zero, enormous internal stresses develop throughout the body, causing severe deformation and failure of tissues, ultimately leading to death.

Net External Force Zero: One of the main confusions faced by students in this chapter is to assume no stress when net external force is zero.
It cannot be "stressed" enough that zero net external force does not imply zero internal stress.

Real material bodies cannot maintain perfect rigidity under external loads; their shape or size changes, even if the deformation is microscopic.

The study of how these distributed internal forces map to geometric changes throughout a body defines the mechanics of deformable bodies — traditionally categorized as the mechanical properties of bulk matter.

Particle Model of Bulk Matter

In chemistry, we have already learned that matter is particulate by nature. There has been an attempt by scientists to design a model based on particulate nature that can elegantly explain the macroscopic mechanical and thermal properties of bulk matter.

The most immediate requirement of such a model is to justify the distinct physical states of matter: solids, liquids, and gases. These microscopic investigations laid the foundation for modern solid-state (condensed matter) physics and statistical thermodynamics.

  • The Solid State: Microscopically modeled as a spring-ball system, where the balls represent constituent particles (atoms or molecules) and the springs represent the localized interatomic forces. The particles are locked into a periodic lattice and can only vibrate about their fixed equilibrium coordinates.
  • The Gaseous State: Modeled using the exact same particulate "balls," but now in a state of continuous, chaotic, and random motion inside a container. In the ideal gas limit, we assume interatomic forces between these particles are completely negligible.
  • The Liquid State: Represents an intermediate regime, possessing localized short-range order but lacking long-range structural rigidity.

Modeling bulk matter through this microscopic lens uncovers the true physical cause of elasticity.

The profound advantage of analyzing elasticity at this atomic level is that it isolates intrinsic material properties—such as density or elastic moduli—which remain entirely independent of extensive macroscopic features like a body's specific geometry.

This isolation allows scientists and standardizing organizations to definitively measure a physical constant—such as documenting the density of pure iron as \(7.87 \text{ g/cm}^3\) or its Young's Modulus as \(210 \text{ GPa}\)—creating universally verified data that can be applied directly to any structural layout, regardless of shape or scale.

Material and Form

When an external force acts on an extended body, the resulting deformation is governed by the interplay of two distinct components:

  • Material: The microscopic configuration of matter, which dictates the intrinsic physical resistance to deformation.
  • Form (Geometry): The macroscopic size and shape of the body, which determine how that internal resistance is spatially distributed and how the deformation manifests.
The Interplay of Material and Form:

Phase 1 (Isolating Material): Consider bending a steel rod with your hands versus bending a wooden stick of identical length and thickness. The wooden stick snaps easily, while the steel rod resists. Because the macroscopic geometry (form) is identical in both cases, this variation proves that internal restoring forces depend fundamentally on the nature of the material.

Phase 2 (Isolating Form): Now, take that same wooden material, but bundle multiple identical sticks together in parallel to significantly increase the overall thickness. If you attempt to bend this combined bundle, it will not break. Because the intrinsic material remains exactly the same (wood), this drastic change in structural resistance proves that internal behavior also depends on the material's structural quantity and spatial form.

Material

At the microscopic level, when a material lattice is deformed, its constituent particles are forced away from their natural equilibrium spacing.

This displacement disrupts the localized electrostatic balance, giving rise to powerful internal restoring forces due to intermolecular interactions.

The absolute strength and gradient of these microscopic interactions determine how vigorously the material resists external alteration.

Material response originates from intermolecular forces.
Stronger interatomic interactions → greater intrinsic resistance to deformation.

Form

Form represents the spatial configuration of the body—the specific way a given amount of matter occupies three-dimensional space.

Form has two independent aspects:
  • Shape (Geometric configuration)
  • Size (Dimensional scale)

Because of these spatial parameters, a single material will exhibit vastly different deforming behaviors depending entirely on its cross-sectional geometry or length. Conversely, two entirely distinct materials cast into identical geometries will yield completely different structural responses under identical loads.

Thus, macroscopically observed deformation is governed jointly by:

  • Material: Dictates the fundamental internal stiffness parameter.
  • Geometry: Coordinates how that parameter scales into a measurable, dimensional change.
Deformation is a geometric change;
Elastic modulus isolates the material response.

Cause of Elasticity

Elastic behavior is the macroscopic manifestation of microscopic atomic and molecular configurations.

The precise physical restoring mechanism depends heavily on the state, arrangement and bonding characteristics of the constituent matter.

Solids (Intermolecular Forces)

In a crystalline solid, constituent atoms or molecules are locked into a stable periodic lattice.

Each particle resides in a state of local static equilibrium governed by the vector sum of forces exerted by its neighbors.

When no external agent acts on the system, particles occupy their natural equilibrium spacing (\(r_0\)), which corresponds to the system's absolute minimum potential energy state.

When an external deforming force is applied, inter-particle distances are forced away from \(r_0\) — either compressing them closer together or stretching them farther apart.

This change disrupts the local balance of electrostatic interactions, causing immediate variations in the intermolecular forces.

Moving the particles closer triggers a powerful short-range electron cloud repulsion, while pulling the particles apart triggers a long-range cohesive attraction.

Both actions increase the internal potential energy of the lattice. Because the system naturally minimizes its potential energy, these induced internal forces act as a restoring mechanism that drives the particles back to their original coordinates once the external load is removed.

Fluids (No Shape Restoration)

In fluid mediums (liquids and gases), the constituent particles possess translational mobility and lack fixed lattice coordinates.

Because they are free to move relative to one another, fluids cannot sustain static internal restoring forces when subjected to parallel tangential constraints (shear deformation).

Under a shear load, the molecular layers simply slide past one another perpetually, resulting in continuous flow rather than shape recovery.

However, fluids fiercely resist variations in absolute volume. When subjected to uniform bulk compression, the reduction in intermolecular spacing forces the particles down their potential energy wells, triggering a robust repulsive response that gives rise to bulk elasticity.

Rubber (Polymer Chain Mechanism)

In elastomers like rubber, elastic behavior arises from an entropic mechanism rather than a purely energetic lattice displacement.

Vulcanized rubber consists of long, highly flexible hydrocarbon polymer chains that are randomly coiled, twisted, and tangled in their natural state. This disordered configuration represents a state of high thermodynamic entropy.

When an external tensile force stretches the material, these polymer chains uncoil, straighten, and align parallel to the force vector, drastically reducing the system's conformational entropy.

Upon removal of the external load, the thermal kinetic energy of the chain segments causes them to spontaneously recoil and return to their highly disordered, maximum-entropy state, generating the macroscopically observed elastic contraction.

Solids resist both shape and volume change.
Fluids resist volume change but not shape change.
Different materials exhibit elasticity through fundamentally different microscopic mechanisms.

Continuum Model of Matter

The continuum model is to provide the formal mathematical framework for study of elasticity.

However, this mathematical abstraction is introduced to support and calculate the physical realities that are already established in the previous sections. Mathematics follows the Physics.

Internal restoring forces are not concurrent vectors concentrated at a single, isolated coordinate point; they are continuously distributed throughout the entire bulk volume of the body. In physics and mathematics, such a spatial distribution is formally defined as a field.

A field means that a physical quantity can be uniquely indexed as a function of continuous spatial coordinates. For example, a scalar temperature field implies that the temperature at any location can be mathematically mapped as a function of position, \(T(x, y, z)\).

Similarly, to accommodate field-based mathematics for mechanical forces, the bulk matter inside a body's boundaries must be treated as a continuous medium rather than a collection of discrete, isolated particles separated by empty space.

Transitioning to this continuous framework allows localized physical parameters — such as stress (a force field intensity) and strain (a deformation field) — to be mathematically defined at every single differential point inside the material body.

To make this field calculus doable, the foundational continuum model relies on three core idealizations:

  • Continuity: Matter completely and uninterruptedly fills its geometric boundaries, containing zero internal gaps, voids, or vacuum spaces.
  • Homogeneity: The physical and structural properties of the medium are spatially invariant, meaning they are identical at every point throughout the bulk volume.
  • Isotropy: The material parameters are directionally invariant, meaning the mechanical response is identical across any chosen directional vector.

It is vital to recognize that these properties are idealizations. Real engineering materials frequently satisfy some of these criteria while completely violating others.

For instance, crystalline graphite is structurally homogeneous (its repeating pattern is uniform throughout), but it is strictly non-isotropic (anisotropic).

Because of its layered atomic arrangement, graphite's internal bonds yield a vastly different mechanical resistance along its parallel sheets compared to its perpendicular axis. Consequently, the resulting internal stress fields and geometric deformations are rarely uniform in all directions.

Thus, the continuum model provides the necessary field-theory framework, while the actual, real-world response of an object depends entirely on how closely its atomic structure satisfies these foundational assumptions.
