Stress (Model)

Stress as a second order tensor physical quantity.

What is Stress?

Stress arises from internal forces within a material body. When external forces act on a body, internal forces develop throughout the material to maintain equilibrium.

These internal forces cannot be measured directly. Instead, they are inferred using methods such as the cut-body (section) method, where imaginary cuts are made to analyze internal force distribution.

Stress is a way to describe how these internal forces are distributed over surfaces inside the material.

Internal Forces

Internal forces in an extended body are similar in nature to normal reaction and tension forces studied in Newton’s laws using the system approach.

Within a system, these forces appear as action–reaction pairs and cancel out when considering the motion of the whole body. Hence, they do not affect the net external force.

However, these internal forces are still present inside the material and are distributed throughout it.

For example, if a rod is pulled with equal forces of 10 N at both ends, the rod remains in equilibrium (net force is zero), but internal forces develop within the rod.

Thus, even when the net external force is zero, internal force distribution can exist, leading to stress inside the body.

Equilibrium does not imply absence of internal forces.
Stress measures how these internal forces are distributed within the material.

Directional Nature of Stress

Stress depends on direction. The internal force acting at a point depends not only on the location but also on the orientation of the surface on which it acts.

Thus, at a given point inside a body, different surfaces passing through that point can experience different internal forces.

Because of this, stress cannot be described by a single number or even a simple vector. It requires multiple components corresponding to different directions and surface orientations.

Unlike vectors, stress does not follow the usual rules of vector addition. Instead, it transforms in a more complex way under change of direction.

Such quantities are described using tensors. Stress is a second-order tensor.

For exam syllabus, we deal with simple cases (like uniaxial loading or isotropic conditions), where only one component of stress is relevant. In such cases, stress can be treated as a scalar quantity for calculation.

Stress vs Pressure

Stress represents how internal forces are distributed over surfaces inside a material. These forces can act in different directions depending on the orientation of the surface.

In general, stress can have both:

  • Normal components — acting perpendicular to a surface (tensile or compressive).
  • Shear components — acting tangentially along a surface.

Thus, in solids, stress is directional and can vary with orientation. Different surfaces passing through the same point can experience different stress components.

Pressure, on the other hand, is a much more restricted case. It is defined as a normal compressive force per unit area and acts equally in all directions.

Pressure is a special case of stress.
Stress can be tensile, compressive, or shear.
Pressure is only compressive and always normal to a surface.

In a fluid at rest, shear stress cannot exist because fluid particles can move relative to each other. Any shear would cause continuous deformation (flow), not equilibrium.

As a result, the internal force distribution in a static fluid becomes purely normal and identical in all directions. This isotropic stress state is what we call pressure.

In contrast, solids can sustain both normal and shear stresses, leading to anisotropic stress states where different components act along different directions (We only have scalar stress in syllabus).

Pressure = isotropic normal stress (same in all directions).
General stress = directional distribution of both normal and shear components.

Why divide by Area?

From the spring–ball model, the stiffness of a single interaction (between two neighboring particles) depends only on the material — it reflects the intermolecular forces.

In a real body, many such interactions exist across any cross-section. A larger cross-sectional area contains more parallel chains of these interactions.

These chains behave like springs in parallel. The more parallel paths available, the greater the total resistance to deformation. Hence, the overall force required depends on the area.

Therefore, force alone does not characterize the internal state of the material. To isolate the material response, we divide the force by the cross-sectional area.

This gives a quantity that depends only on the material and not on how much of it is present in cross-section.

Force depends on both material and geometry.
Dividing by area removes the geometric factor (number of parallel interactions), leaving a material-dependent quantity — stress.
Imagine two rods made of the same steel — one thin like a needle and the other thick like a tree trunk.
If both are pulled with the same force, the thin rod experiences a much greater concentration of force and fails, while the thick rod easily withstands it.
Stress captures this concentration: smaller area → higher stress.
