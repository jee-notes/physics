Elastic Potential Energy

The energy stored within the internal molecular structure of a deformed material.

Work Done in Stretching a Wire

When a wire is stretched by an external force, work is done against the internal restoring forces. This work is not lost; it is stored in the material as Elastic Potential Energy.

Assume a wire of length \(L\) and area \(A\) is stretched by a force \(F\), producing an extension \(x\). According to Hooke's Law: \[ F = \frac{YA}{L} x \] The work done to increase the extension by a small amount \(dx\) is \(dW = F \cdot dx\).

Total work done to achieve an extension \(\Delta L\): \[ W = \int_0^{\Delta L} \frac{YA}{L} x \, dx = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{YA}{L} (\Delta L)^2 \]

Elastic Potential Energy Formula (\(U\))

Substituting \(F = \frac{YA}{L} \Delta L\) into the work formula, we get many useful forms:

\[ U = \frac{1}{2} \cdot F \cdot \Delta L \] \[ U = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \text{Load} \cdot \text{Extension} \]
The factor of "1/2" arises because the restoring force is zero initially and increases linearly with extension. Thus, we are effectively using the average force \((0 + F) / 2\).

Energy per Unit Volume (Energy Density)

To isolate the material's energy storage capacity from its geometry, we calculate the energy stored per unit volume of the material.

\[ \text{Energy Density } (u) = \frac{U}{\text{Volume}} = \frac{\frac{1}{2} F \Delta L}{A L} \] \[ u = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{F}{A} \right) \left( \frac{\Delta L}{L} \right) \] \[ u = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \text{Stress} \cdot \text{Strain} = \frac{1}{2} \sigma \varepsilon \]

Using Hooke's Law (\(\sigma = Y\varepsilon\)), we can express this as:

  • \( u = \frac{1}{2} Y (\text{Strain})^2 \)
  • \( u = \frac{1}{2} \frac{(\text{Stress})^2}{Y} \)
Energy density is a powerful concept in material science. It represents how much energy a material can absorb before it reaches its elastic limit or breaks.
