Nature and Origin of Friction

The fundamental science behind resistive contact forces.

1. What is Friction?

Friction is a force that opposes the relative motion (or the tendency of such motion) between two surfaces in contact. It is a non-conservative force, meaning it dissipates mechanical energy into heat.

Friction does not always oppose motion; it opposes relative motion. For example, when you walk, friction between your shoes and the ground actually helps you move forward by opposing the backward push of your feet.

2. Microscopic Origin

Even highly polished surfaces have microscopic irregularities called asperities. When two surfaces touch, they only make contact at these "high points."

  • Interlocking: The asperities of one surface get caught in the valleys of the other.
  • Cold Welding: At the points of contact, atoms from the two surfaces are so close that they form temporary chemical bonds (welds).

To slide one surface over the other, these welds must be sheared and the interlocking peaks must be lifted or broken.

3. Dependency on Normal Force

The number of contact points and the strength of the cold welds depend on how hard the surfaces are pressed together. This is quantified by the Normal Force (\(N\)).

Higher \(N\) \(\implies\) Higher real area of contact \(\implies\) Higher Friction.
