Friction

The tangential contact force opposing relative motion.

Conceptual Overview

Up until this point in mechanics, we have frequently idealized contacting surfaces as perfectly smooth (frictionless) to simplify the analysis of motion. In reality, however, perfectly frictionless surfaces do not exist. Whenever two solid bodies come into contact, friction is generally present.

Whenever two solid surfaces are in contact and there is relative motion (or a tendency for relative motion) between them, a tangential contact force arises at the interface. This resistive force is called Friction.

Friction is arguably the most important force in everyday life. Without friction, we could neither walk nor grip objects, vehicles could neither accelerate nor brake, and even simple knots would immediately unravel.

From a fundamental perspective, friction is not a separate fundamental force of nature. Rather, it is a macroscopic manifestation of countless microscopic electromagnetic interactions between the atoms, molecules, and surface irregularities of the two contacting bodies. Since these interactions are extremely complex, friction is described in mechanics using simple empirical laws rather than being derived directly from electromagnetic theory.

Unlike most forces studied earlier, the magnitude of friction is generally not known beforehand. Static friction automatically adjusts its magnitude to prevent slipping until it reaches a limiting value, making friction one of the first forces whose value must often be determined while solving the equations of motion.

In this chapter, we classify friction into static and kinetic friction, study the empirical laws governing their behavior, and learn to incorporate friction into Newton's Laws of Motion to analyze realistic mechanical systems ranging from a single sliding block to complex multi-body assemblies.

Friction does not simply oppose the motion of an object.
It opposes relative motion (or the tendency for relative motion) between two surfaces in contact.
Consequently, friction may act in the same direction as the motion of an object relative to the ground. For example, the friction exerted by the road on the tires of an accelerating car is precisely the force that propels the car forward.

Self-Adjusting Nature of Friction

The normal force and tension are self-adjusting contact forces, so their magnitudes are determined by the equations of motion. However, their directions are fixed by the geometry of the system. The normal force acts perpendicular to the contact surface, while tension acts along the string.

Friction is unique because both its magnitude and its direction are determined by the motion (or the tendency of motion) of the contacting surfaces.

For kinetic friction, the direction is always opposite to the direction of relative motion between the surfaces, while the magnitude remains fixed at \[ f_k=\mu_kN \]

For static friction, both the direction and the magnitude are self-adjusting. The frictional force automatically adjusts itself to prevent slipping until it reaches its limiting value, \[ f_s\le \mu_sN \]

The analysis of frictional systems begins by identifying the type of friction present.
If slipping occurs, kinetic friction acts with a fixed magnitude \(f_k=\mu_kN\).
If slipping does not occur, static friction acts and its magnitude must be determined from the equations of motion, subject to \(f_s\le\mu_sN\).
Determining whether a system slips is often straightforward for a single particle body but requires a more careful analysis for systems of multiple particle bodies.
