Friction

The contact force universally opposing relative macroscopic motion.

Conceptual Overview

Up until this point in the study of mechanics, many problems involved imaginary "smooth" or "frictionless" surfaces. In reality, absolutely frictionless surfaces do not exist. Whenever two bodies are in physical contact and there is relative motion (or a tendency for relative motion) between them, a resistive force arises tangentially at the interface. This resistive force is Friction.

Friction is arguably the most profoundly important macroscopic force for our daily existence. Without friction, we could not walk, cars could not brake, and knots would instantly unravel. From a fundamental perspective, friction is not a new fundamental force of nature; it is a macroscopic, statistical manifestation of complex electromagnetic interactions between the microscopic irregularities and molecular bonds of two touching surfaces.

In this chapter, we mathematically categorize this resistive force into distinct regimes (static, limiting, kinetic, and rolling). We study the empirical laws governing its magnitude and direction, and finally, we seamlessly integrate frictional forces into Newton's Equations of Motion to solve realistic dynamical systems—ranging from simple sliding blocks to complex multi-block assemblies.

Remember: Friction does not universally "oppose motion". Friction strictly opposes relative motion between two surfaces in contact. In many cases, such as the tires of a smoothly accelerating car or a block sitting on a moving truck, friction is precisely the force causing the forward motion of the object relative to the ground.
