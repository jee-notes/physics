Kinetic Friction

The resistive force mediating objects in macroscopic relative sliding motion.

Kinetic Friction

Once the applied external force irrevocably exceeds the limiting friction threshold, the object snaps into bulk motion. The frictional force that instantly comes into play to oppose this continued macroscopic sliding is termed Kinetic Friction (or dynamic sliding friction), denoted by \(f_k\).

Because the microscopic peaks and valleys (asperities) of the two rapidly sliding surfaces no longer have sufficient time to effectively settle down and strongly "cold weld" to one another, the kinetic friction is almost always physically less than the limiting static friction. It is empirically easier to keep an object sliding than it is to start its macroscopic slide.

Coefficient of Kinetic Friction

Like limiting friction, kinetic friction is strictly proportional to the normal contact force and fundamentally independent of the apparent macroscopic area of contact. Furthermore, for moderate sliding speeds typically encountered in mechanics problems, kinetic friction is modeled as completely independent of the velocity of sliding.

Kinetic Friction Formulation: \[ f_k = \mu_k N \]

Where:

  • \(f_k\) is the kinetic frictional force.
  • \(\mu_k\) is the Coefficient of Kinetic Friction (dimensionless).
  • \(N\) is the normal reaction force.

Universal Relation: For essentially all dry surface combinations: \[ \mu_k < \mu_s \] Consequently: \[ f_k < (f_s)_{max} \]

This mathematical inequality explains the sudden "jerk" a stationary object exhibits when pushed. The applied force builds until slipping occurs, at which exact instant the opposing resistive force drops from \(\mu_s N\) down to \(\mu_k N\), resulting in a sudden burst of unbalanced net force and rapid forward mechanical acceleration.
