Types of Friction

Categorizing the behavioral regimes of the frictional force.

Overview of Frictional Regimes

The behavior of friction is governed entirely by the relative motion between the two contacting surfaces. As the state of relative motion changes, the behavior of friction also changes. Accordingly, friction is classified into four distinct regimes.

Friction (like Normal) acts at the point of contact between the two surfaces. For a particle body, its the same, but for a multi-particle body, point of application has impact and will be studied later in rotation. All types of friction act at the point of contact and so they are contact forces. This is true even for viscous fluids.

  • Static Friction: Acts when the points of contact of respective surfaces are at rest relative to each other.
  • Limiting Friction: The maximum possible value of static friction, occurs when the points of contact are just about to slip relative to each other.
  • Kinetic Friction: Acts when the points of contact of respective surfaces are slipping relative to each other.
  • Rolling Friction: The resistive force associated with rolling motion of multi-particle bodies. A particle body cannot experience rolling friction.

To understand these classifications correctly, it is essential to distinguish between sliding and slipping. Although these words are often used interchangeably in everyday language, they represent different physical ideas.

Sliding describes the translational motion of the entire multi-particle body along a surface. Imagine a slide in a park and kids playing on the slide.

Slipping, on the other hand, describes the relative motion between the two contacting surfaces at the point of contact.

Thus, sliding is an event of the entire multi-particle body and slipping is an event of the contacting points only rather than that of the entire body.

For a particle body, sliding and slipping are identical concepts. Since the body is represented by a single point, translational motion of the body automatically implies relative motion at the point of contact.
For a multi-particle body, however, these two concepts become distinct.
A multi-particle body can simultaneously translate and rotate. As a result, the body may continue moving while the point of contact remains momentarily at rest relative to the surface.
This distinction between sliding and slipping is one of the most important conceptual differences between particle dynamics and rigid body dynamics. It is precisely this distinction that makes rolling motion possible.

Rolling is therefore not another type of relative motion between two surfaces in contact; it is a special mode of motion made possible by the geometry and rotation of a multi-particle body.

During pure rolling, the body continuously translates while the point of contact does not slip relative to the surface. Since slipping is eliminated, the large energy losses associated with kinetic friction also vanish.

The wheel is one of humanity's greatest mechanical inventions because it controls friction through design rather than by changing the materials in contact.
Dragging an object requires the two surfaces to slide over one another, producing kinetic friction throughout the motion.
A wheel, however, converts sliding into rolling.
By ensuring that the point of contact does not slip, it dramatically reduces frictional losses and makes transportation of people and goods remarkably efficient.
Rolling is sliding without slipping.

Friction vs. Applied Force Graph

The self-adjusting nature of friction is best understood by studying how the frictional force varies as the applied force is gradually increased.

Consider a particle body of mass \(10\text{ kg}\) resting on a rough horizontal surface. The normal reaction is \[ N=mg=10\times10=100\text{ N} \] The coefficients of friction are \[ \mu_s=0.5 \qquad \mu_k=0.4 \] A horizontal force \(F_{\text{ext}}\) is now applied and increased gradually while the frictional force is observed.

The Friction Curve

  • Phase 1 : Static Friction

    Initially, the block remains at rest. Since there is no slipping, the friction is static.

    Static friction automatically adjusts itself to balance the applied force. For example, if \[ F_{\text{ext}}=10\text{ N} \] then \[ f_s=10\text{ N} \] If the applied force is increased to \(20\text{ N}\), the friction also becomes \(20\text{ N}\). Thus, as long as the block remains at rest, \[ f_s=F_{\text{ext}} \]

    Consequently, the graph of friction versus applied force is a straight line passing through the origin with slope \(m = 1\) or \(\theta = 45^\circ\) .

  • Phase 2 : Limiting Friction

    As the applied force continues to increase, the microscopic junctions between the two surfaces resist the motion by developing a larger and larger static frictional force.

    Eventually, static friction reaches its maximum possible value, \[ f_{s\,max}=\mu_sN=0.5\times100=50\text{ N} \] This maximum value is called the limiting friction. At this stage, the block is just about to slip.

  • Phase 3 : Kinetic Friction

    The moment the applied force exceeds the limiting friction, slipping begins. Static friction can no longer maintain equilibrium and immediately gives way to kinetic friction.

    The microscopic junctions are now continuously formed and broken as the surfaces slide over one another. Consequently, the frictional force drops to \[ f_k=\mu_kN=0.4\times100=40\text{ N} \] and remains constant during sliding.

    The graph therefore shows a sudden drop from the limiting friction to the kinetic friction, followed by a horizontal line.

This explains why it is more difficult to start moving a heavy object than to keep it moving. A larger force is required to overcome the limiting friction than to overcome the kinetic friction once slipping has begun.
The friction versus applied force graph is not a property of the applied force. It is a graphical representation of the self-adjusting nature of static friction.
As long as slipping does not occur, static friction adjusts itself to match the applied force exactly.
The graph changes character only when the limiting friction is exceeded. Beyond this point, static friction ceases to exist and kinetic friction takes over.
