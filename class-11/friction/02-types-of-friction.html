Types of Friction

Categorizing the behavioral regimes of the frictional force before and during macroscopic motion.

Overview of Frictional Regimes

Friction demonstrates significantly disparate behaviors depending strictly on whether the two contacting surfaces are physically moving relative to each other or simply experiencing a tendency to move. We classify these regimes into three primary types:

  • Static Friction: The force operating when there is an applied force but zero relative sliding between the surfaces.
  • Limiting Friction: The absolute maximum boundary value of static friction, occurring right at the precipice of slipping.
  • Kinetic Friction: The roughly constant force operating when actual relative sliding (macroscopic motion) is steadily occurring.
  • Rolling Friction: The distinct, much weaker resistive force that opposes the rolling motion of objects like spheres or cylinders on a surface.

Friction vs. Applied Force Graph

To deeply understand these regimes, consider a heavy block resting on a rough horizontal floor. Imagine applying a steadily increasing horizontal pulling force \(F_{ext}\) to the block, and measuring the resulting frictional force \(f\).

The Friction Curve:
  • Phase 1: No Motion (Static). As you gently pull with a force \(F_{ext} = 10\text{ N}\), the block doesn't move. Friction perfectly matches your pull: \(f_s = 10\text{ N}\). The static friction force \(f_s\) exactly equals \(F_{ext}\) to maintain equilibrium. On a graph of \(f\) vs \(F_{ext}\), this forms a perfectly straight line extending from the origin with a slope of 1 (a \(45^\circ\) angle).
  • Phase 2: The Breaking Point (Limiting). You pull harder. At a specific threshold, say \(F_{ext} = 50\text{ N}\), the microscopic cold welds are stretched to their absolute breaking limit. This peak resistive value is the limiting friction, \(f_{max}\).
  • Phase 3: Sliding (Kinetic). Given a slight push past \(50\text{ N}\), the bonds violently break, and the block abruptly snaps into motion. Because it is much harder to break intact cold welds than to keep objects sliding rapidly over broken microscopic peaks, the frictional force immediately drops slightly to a constant lower value, the kinetic friction \(f_k\). The graph becomes a flat horizontal line, regardless of how much harder you continue pulling.
This is exactly why it takes significantly more physical effort to start moving heavy furniture across a carpet than it takes to physically keep it moving once it starts sliding.
