Rolling Friction

The extremely weak dispersive force opposing wheels, cylinders, and spheres.

Concept of Rolling Friction

When a perfectly rigid sphere rolls over a perfectly rigid frictionless horizontal plane, it touches the plane precisely at a single geometric point. Because the instantaneous velocity of the contact point is absolutely zero (assuming pure rolling without slipping), there is zero relative sliding motion at the interface.

If there is no sliding, kinetic friction \(f_k\) must be structurally zero. In an idealized mathematical universe, a rolling wheel should coast absolutely forever without dissipating any energy.

However, in practical reality, a rolling wheel does eventually slow down and stop even without axel resistance. This slight resistance is termed Rolling Friction (or rolling resistance).

Rolling friction is empirically orders of magnitude physically weaker than both kinetic and static sliding frictions. This profound reduction in resistance is arguably the foundational mechanical basis for the invention of the wheel, ball bearings, and all rotary vehicular transport.

Cause of Rolling Resistance

If there is zero sliding, what force causes this energy loss? The answer lies in the microscopic and macroscopic deformation of the interacting surfaces.

When a heavy wheel rests on a floor, the immense point-pressure mathematically implies infinite stress. Thus, neither surface is perfectly rigid. The wheel dynamically flattens slightly at the bottom, and the floor elastically yields, creating a tiny indentation or "rut" rather than a perfectly sharp geometric point of contact.

As the heavy wheel forcefully rolls forward, it continually plows into the microscopic mound of material in front of it and requires thermodynamic energy to perpetually deform the fresh surface ahead. Meanwhile, the elastic material behind the wheel physically rebounds, but strictly due to internal material hysteresis (energy lost as heat during deformation), it does not vigorously push the wheel forward as hard as it resisted the initial compression.

This asymmetrical deformation shifts the net normal force structurally slightly ahead of the wheel's vertical center of mass, producing a net retarding mechanical torque that powerfully opposes the rolling rotation.

Rolling Resistance Approximation:

While highly complex to precisely model, macroscopic rolling friction (\(f_r\)) is often linearly approximated by:

\[ f_r = \frac{\mu_r N}{R} \] Where \(\mu_r\) is the coefficient of rolling friction (which notably carries units of length), and \(R\) is the geometric radius of the rolling wheel.
