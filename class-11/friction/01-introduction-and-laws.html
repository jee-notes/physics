Laws of Friction

Understanding the physical origin and the empirical rules governing friction.

What are the Laws of Friction?

The behavior of friction was studied experimentally by scientists such as Amontons and Coulomb.

Their observations were summarized into a set of empirical rules known as the Laws of Friction.

These laws apply to friction between two dry solid surfaces in contact.

These laws apply purely to dry, solid friction (Coulomb friction).
If a layer of fluid (lubricant) entirely separates the surfaces, the problem transitions into fluid mechanics (viscous drag), which heavily depends on both surface area and velocity.

Although the laws of friction were established experimentally, the microscopic welding (asperity) model provides a physical explanation for their origin.

Together, the empirical laws and their microscopic explanation form the foundation of the study of dry friction.

Microscopic Origin of Friction

The microscopic origin of friction explains why friction exists, why it opposes relative motion between surfaces, and why it exhibits two distinct regimes: static friction and kinetic friction.

At a macroscopic level, a surface may appear perfectly smooth to the naked eye. However, when viewed under a powerful microscope, even highly polished surfaces reveal a jagged landscape of microscopic peaks (asperities) and valleys.

When two solid surfaces are pressed together, they do not touch perfectly across their entire apparent area. Instead, they make actual contact only at the tips of these microscopic asperities.

Since the true area of contact is much smaller than the apparent area, the local pressure \[ P=\frac{F}{A} \] at these junctions becomes extremely large.

This immense pressure causes the asperities to deform plastically and develop strong intermolecular electromagnetic attractions. In many cases, microscopic adhesive junctions, commonly called cold welds, are formed between the contacting surfaces.

To make one surface slide over the other, an external tangential force must continuously deform, shear, and break these microscopic junctions.

The cumulative resistive force resulting from the breaking of these junctions and the interlocking of surface irregularities is what we measure macroscopically as friction. Since friction is fundamentally a resistive force, it develops only when there is a tendency for relative motion between the surfaces.

A rough surface does not imply that friction is always present.
A rough surface is merely capable of exerting friction.
If there is no tendency for relative motion, there is nothing for friction to resist, and the frictional force is zero.
This is why the magnitude of static friction can vary from \[ 0 \le f_s \le \mu_sN \]

When two solid bodies slide relative to each other, the frictional force is called kinetic friction. When there is no relative motion between the surfaces, the frictional force is called static friction.

Static friction is self-adjusting. As the applied tangential force increases, the frictional force automatically increases to oppose it and maintain equilibrium until it reaches its limiting value. Beyond this limit, the microscopic junctions can no longer prevent slipping, and static friction is replaced by kinetic friction.

Laws of Friction

The microscopic interactions between two contacting surfaces are enormously complex. Consequently, the exact frictional force cannot be derived directly from first principles. Instead, the behavior of friction is described by a set of empirical laws established through experiments.

Although these are not fundamental laws like Newton's Laws of Motion, they provide highly accurate approximations for most problems involving dry solid surfaces.

These laws summarize the quantities on which friction depends and those on which it is approximately independent.

  1. Dependence on Normal Reaction: The limiting static friction and the kinetic friction are directly proportional to the normal reaction exerted between the two surfaces.
  2. Dependence on the Nature of the Surfaces: The magnitude of friction depends on the materials of the contacting surfaces and their surface condition, such as roughness, polish, and lubrication.
  3. Independence of Apparent Contact Area: For a given normal reaction, the frictional force is approximately independent of the apparent area of contact between the surfaces. For example, a brick requires approximately the same force to slide whether it rests on its broad face or its narrow face.
  4. Independence of Sliding Speed: For ordinary sliding speeds, kinetic friction is independent of the relative speed of the surfaces. At extremely high speeds, this may no longer remain valid due to effects such as excessive heating or melting.
