Introduction and Laws of Friction

Understanding the physical origin and the empirical rules governing dry surface contact.

Microscopic Origin of Friction

At a macroscopic level, a surface may appear perfectly smooth to the naked eye. However, when viewed under a powerful microscope, even highly polished surfaces reveal a jagged landscape of peaks (asperities) and valleys.

When two solid surfaces are pressed together, they do not touch perfectly across their entire apparent area. Instead, they make actual contact only at the tips of these microscopic asperities.

Because the true area of contact is astronomically small compared to the apparent area, the local pressure (\(P = F/A\)) at these junction points is exceptionally high. This immense pressure causes the asperities to compress plastically and momentarily "cold weld" together due to intense intermolecular electromagnetic attractions.

To make one surface slide over the other, an external tangential force must physically shear or break these thousands of microscopic cold welds. The cumulative resistive force resulting from the breaking of these junctions and the interlocking of surface irregularities is what we measure macroscopically as Friction.

Laws of Solid Friction

Through extensive historical experimentation (notably by da Vinci, Amontons, and Coulomb), the behavior of dry sliding friction has been codified into several empirical laws. While not fundamental physical laws like Newton's laws of motion, they are highly reliable approximations for macroscopic engineering.

The Empirical Laws of Dry Friction:
  1. Dependence on Normal Force: The maximum frictional force (limiting friction) and the kinetic frictional force are directly proportional to the normal contact force (\(N\)) pressing the surfaces together.
  2. Independence of Apparent Contact Area: Once the normal force is fixed, the frictional force remains essentially independent of the macroscopic area of contact between the two bodies. (A brick requires the same force to pull whether resting on its wide face or its narrow edge).
  3. Dependence on Surface Nature: The magnitude of friction heavily depends on the physical nature and material composition of the two contacting surfaces (e.g., rubber on concrete vs. steel on ice), and their state of polish or lubrication.
  4. Independence of Velocity: Kinetic friction is reasonably independent of the relative sliding velocity between the two surfaces, provided the speed is not so extreme as to cause melting or excessive heating.
It is very important to note that these laws apply purely to dry, solid friction (Coulomb friction). If a layer of fluid (lubricant) entirely separates the surfaces, the problem transitions into fluid mechanics (viscous drag), which heavily depends on both surface area and velocity.
