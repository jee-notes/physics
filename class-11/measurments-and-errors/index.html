Measurements and Errors

Managing the inevitable uncertainties of the physical world.

Conceptual Overview

Physics is a quantitative science, in the sense that the physical world is described and studied using a mathematical framework.

Physics = Physical World + Maths

Measurement is the process that connects these two domains. It enables physical quantities to be represented mathematically by assigning numerical values and units to them.

While mathematics derives its certainty from logical deduction, physics relies on measurements to test, validate and refine its theories. In this sense, physical theories are often mathematical models whose validity is established through experimental observations.

An experimental observation or an instrument reading is essentially the outcome of a measurement process.

However, no measurement is ever perfectly exact. Every measuring instrument possesses a finite resolution, and every experimental procedure is subject to practical limitations.

Consequently, every measured quantity carries some degree of uncertainty. Understanding, estimating and communicating these uncertainties is an essential part of scientific practice.

This chapter is devoted to the study of measurement uncertainties, the methods used to quantify them, and the manner in which they propagate through calculations involving measured quantities.

Measurement

In the Units and Dimensions chapter, we studied that measurement is the process of comparing a physical quantity with a predetermined reference quantity called a unit.

To compare means to determine how many times the chosen unit is contained in the quantity being measured. The outcome of this comparison is therefore expressed as a numerical value along with the unit.

In practice, however, this comparison cannot be performed with infinite precision.

Every measuring instrument has a finite resolution, and every measurement process is subject to practical limitations. Both, the process as well as the instrument introduce uncertainity in the measured value.

Consequently, every measured quantity possesses an inherent uncertainty.

It is important to distinguish measurement from counting. Counting discrete objects is exact. For example, stating that there are 50 students in a classroom is a counting process rather than a measurement.

Measurement, on the other hand, involves determining the magnitude of a physical quantity. For instance, measuring the radius of a drawn circle using a ruler is a measurement because the result depends on the instrument used and is subject to uncertainty.

Accuracy and Precision

As discussed earlier, every measurement is associated with some uncertainty. The primary objective of this chapter is to study and quantify these uncertainties, commonly referred to as errors.

However, errors cannot be studied from a single measurement. To investigate the nature and extent of uncertainty in a measurement process, it is necessary to repeat the measurement several times under similar conditions and analyze the resulting set of observations.

Repeated measurements allow us to characterize the quality of the measurement process through two important but distinct concepts: precision and accuracy.

Precision refers to the degree of agreement among repeated measurements, whereas accuracy describes how close the measured value, usually represented by the mean of repeated observations, is to the true value of the quantity being measured.

Thus, a set of measurements may exhibit high precision by yielding nearly identical readings and yet possess poor accuracy if those readings are consistently displaced from the true value.

Scientific measurements must therefore be evaluated not only for their repeatability but also for their closeness to the true value. In other words, scientific rigor requires us to distinguish between being consistently wrong and being close to the truth.

The Philosophy of Uncertainty

In everyday language, an error usually implies a mistake or carelessness. In experimental physics, however, the term has a different meaning.

An error refers to the uncertainty inherently associated with a measurement. It arises because no measuring instrument possesses infinite resolution, and no experimental procedure can be performed under perfectly identical conditions.

Consequently, the objective of an experiment is not to determine the exact value of a physical quantity, but to estimate its value along with an appropriate measure of its uncertainty.

Uncertainty is a broad concept encountered throughout physics. This chapter, is concerned specifically with uncertainties arising from the process of measurement. Such uncertainties are called errors.

A measured result reported without an estimate of its uncertainty is therefore scientifically incomplete.

Precision is about the instrument's resolution.
Accuracy is about the method's reliability.
