Connected Vessels

Connected Vessel problems using mole conservation.

Two-Bulb System with Stopcock

A standard problem in gaseous-state thermodynamics involves two rigid vessels of volumes \(V_1\) and \(V_2\), connected by a narrow tube of negligible volume containing a stopcock.

Initially, the two vessels contain ideal gases in states \[ (P_1,V_1,T_1,n_1) \qquad \text{and} \qquad (P_2,V_2,T_2,n_2) \]

When the stopcock is opened, each gas spreads throughout the combined available volume: \[ V_f=V_1+V_2 \] At the final thermodynamic equilibrium, both gases have a common temperature: \[ T_{1f}=T_{2f}=T_f \]

Since the combined system is closed and the gases do not react, the amount of each gas is conserved:

\[ n_1=\frac{P_1V_1}{RT_1} \qquad n_2=\frac{P_2V_2}{RT_2} \]

At equilibrium, each gas contributes a partial pressure to the mixture:

\[ P_{1f}=\frac{n_1RT_f}{V_1+V_2} \qquad P_{2f}=\frac{n_2RT_f}{V_1+V_2} \]

The mixture has a single total equilibrium pressure equal to the sum of the partial pressures:

\[ P_f=P_{1f}+P_{2f} \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P_f=\frac{T_f}{V_1+V_2}\left(\frac{P_1V_1}{T_1}+\frac{P_2V_2}{T_2}\right)} \]

This gives the general relation between the final equilibrium pressure \(P_f\) and temperature \(T_f\). To determine their individual values, we need an additional condition specifying how the mixing takes place, such as adiabatic or isothermal conditions.

Adiabatic Mixing

Adiabatic mixing is a mixing process in which there is no heat exchange between the gas system and its surroundings.

For the two-bulb system, the outer walls are adiabatic (implies \(Q=0\)) and the walls of the combined container are rigid (implies \(W=0\)). Therefore, By the First Law of Thermodynamics, \[ \Delta U=Q-W=0 \] Hence, the total internal energy of the two gases is conserved: \[ \Delta U_1+\Delta U_2=0 \]

When the stopcock is opened, each gas gains access to the volume of the other bulb. Thus, the mechanical state of both gases changes as they spread through the combined volume.

At the same time, thermal energy can be transferred internally between the gases, and they eventually reach a common final temperature \(T_f\).

Although the actual process involves a change in volume, for an ideal gas the formula for internal energy involves \(C_V\) and it depends only on temperature. Therefore, \[ \Delta U = nC_V\Delta T \] can be used to calculate the change in internal energy even when the volume of the gas changes during the process.

Thus, \[ n_1C_{V1}(T_f-T_1)+n_2C_{V2}(T_f-T_2)=0 \]

Therefore, \[ \boxed{T_f=\frac{n_1C_{V1}T_1+n_2C_{V2}T_2}{n_1C_{V1}+n_2C_{V2}}} \]

Isothermal Mixing

Isothermal mixing is another type of mixing process.

Isothermal implies that the walls of the system have infinite conductivity and so the initial and final temperatures of individual gases is always same as the surrounding temperature \(T_s\) .

For this setup, an isothermal mixing process is also adiabatic. Since the total external volume is fixed, no work is done by the combined system: \[ W=0 \] Also, for an ideal gas, internal energy depends only on temperature, so under isothermal conditions: \[ \Delta U=0 \] Therefore, from the First Law, \[ \Delta U=Q-W \] we get \[ Q=0 \]

We say that:

\[ T_1 = T_2 = T_f = T_s \]

Putting for the values of \(T_1 = T_2 = T_f = T_s\) in the relation equation, all temperature variables cancel out and we get:

\[ P_f = \frac{P_1 V_1 + P_2 V_2}{V_1 + V_2} \]

Adiabatic Mixing of Same Gas

For adiabatic mixing, the total internal energy of the two gases is conserved: \[ U_{1i}+U_{2i}=U_{1f}+U_{2f}. \] For an ideal gas, \[ \Delta U=nC_V\Delta T, \] so the common final temperature is \[ \boxed{ T_f= \frac{n_1C_{V1}T_1+n_2C_{V2}T_2} {n_1C_{V1}+n_2C_{V2}} }. \]

If both bulbs contain the same ideal gas, then \[ C_{V1}=C_{V2}=C_V, \] and therefore \[ T_f= \frac{n_1T_1+n_2T_2}{n_1+n_2}. \] Using \[ n_1=\frac{P_1V_1}{RT_1}, \qquad n_2=\frac{P_2V_2}{RT_2}, \] this may also be written as \[ T_f= \frac{P_1V_1+P_2V_2} {\dfrac{P_1V_1}{T_1}+\dfrac{P_2V_2}{T_2}}. \]

To determine the final pressure, there is an even more useful way to look at the problem. For the same ideal gas, \[ PV=nRT \] and \[ U=nC_VT. \] Eliminating \(nT\), \[ \boxed{ P=\frac{R}{C_V}\frac{U}{V} }. \] Thus, for the same ideal gas, \[ \boxed{P\propto\frac{U}{V}}. \]

After opening the stopcock, both gases occupy the same final volume \[ V_f=V_1+V_2. \] Therefore their partial pressures are \[ P_{1f}=\frac{R}{C_V}\frac{U_{1f}}{V_f}, \qquad P_{2f}=\frac{R}{C_V}\frac{U_{2f}}{V_f}. \] The final pressure of the mixture is \[ P_f=P_{1f}+P_{2f}. \] Hence, \[ P_f= \frac{R}{C_VV_f} \left(U_{1f}+U_{2f}\right). \]

Since the mixing is adiabatic and the combined container is rigid, \[ U_{1f}+U_{2f}=U_{1i}+U_{2i}. \] Therefore, \[ P_f= \frac{R}{C_V(V_1+V_2)} \left(U_{1i}+U_{2i}\right). \] For the initial states of the same ideal gas, \[ U_{1i}=\frac{C_V}{R}P_1V_1, \qquad U_{2i}=\frac{C_V}{R}P_2V_2. \] Substituting, \[ P_f= \frac{P_1V_1+P_2V_2}{V_1+V_2}. \] Thus, \[ \boxed{ P_f= \frac{P_1V_1+P_2V_2}{V_1+V_2} }. \]

Remarkably, for adiabatic mixing of the same ideal gas, the final equilibrium pressure has exactly the same mathematical form as in isothermal mixing: \[ \boxed{ P_f=\frac{P_1V_1+P_2V_2}{V_1+V_2} }. \] The reason is that, for the same ideal gas, \[ P\propto\frac{U}{V}. \] Adiabatic mixing conserves the total internal energy \(U\), while the final volume is \(V_1+V_2\). The redistribution of that internal energy between the two gases does not matter for the total final pressure.
