State Variables & Pressure Measurement

Thermodynamic state coordinates, hydrostatic manometry, barometric principles, and absolute temperature scales.

Thermodynamic State Variables of Gases

A thermodynamic system composed of a gas is macroscopically specified when its state variables are defined:

1. Pressure (\( P \))

The normal compressive force exerted by gas molecules colliding against unit surface area of the enclosing walls:

\[ P = \frac{F_\perp}{A} \]

SI Unit: Pascal (\(\text{Pa} = \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}\))  |  Dimensional Formula: \([M^1 L^{-1} T^{-2}]\)

Standard Pressure Units & Conversions:

Unit Symbol Equivalent in Pascal (\(\text{Pa}\))
Standard Atmosphere \(\text{atm}\) \( 1\text{ atm} = 1.01325 \times 10^5\text{ Pa} = 101.325\text{ kPa} \)
Bar \(\text{bar}\) \( 1\text{ bar} = 10^5\text{ Pa} = 0.98692\text{ atm} \)
Torr / Millimeter of Mercury \(\text{torr} / \text{mmHg}\) \( 1\text{ atm} = 760\text{ mmHg} = 760\text{ torr} \implies 1\text{ torr} \approx 133.322\text{ Pa} \)
Pounds per Square Inch \(\text{psi}\) \( 1\text{ atm} \approx 14.696\text{ psi} \)

2. Volume (\( V \))

The volume of a gas equals the internal volume of the vessel enclosing it.

  • SI Unit: Cubic meter (\(\text{m}^3\))  |  Dimensional Formula: \([L^3]\)
  • Conversions: \( 1\text{ m}^3 = 10^3\text{ dm}^3 = 10^3\text{ L} = 10^6\text{ cm}^3 = 10^6\text{ mL} = 10^6\text{ cc} \)
  • \( 1\text{ L} = 10^{-3}\text{ m}^3 = 1\text{ dm}^3 = 1000\text{ cm}^3 \)

3. Temperature (\( T \))

An intensive measure of the average kinetic energy of molecular translational motion:

  • SI Unit: Kelvin (\(\text{K}\))  |  Dimensional Formula: \([\Theta]\) or \([K^1]\)
  • Conversion: \( T(\text{K}) = t(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15 \)

4. Amount of Substance (\( n \))

The quantity of matter in moles:

\[ n = \frac{m}{M} = \frac{N}{N_A} \]

where \( m \) is sample mass, \( M \) is molar mass in \(\text{g/mol}\), \( N \) is total number of molecules, and \( N_A \approx 6.022 \times 10^{23}\text{ mol}^{-1} \) is Avogadro's constant.

Macroscopic vs Microscopic Description

Thermal physics analyzes gases from two distinct but mutually consistent viewpoints:

Aspect Macroscopic (Thermodynamic / Gaseous State) Microscopic (Kinetic Theory / Statistical)
Variables \( P, V, T, n, \rho \) Molecular mass \( m \), velocity \( \vec{v} \), momentum \( \vec{p} \), mean free path \( \lambda \)
Measurement Directly measurable using macroscopic instruments (gauges, thermometers). Statistical averages inferred through molecular distributions.
Pressure Origin Continuous normal surface stress (\( F/A \)). Time-averaged rate of momentum transfer during molecular wall collisions (\( P = \frac{1}{3}\rho v_{\text{rms}}^2 \)).
Temperature Origin Equilibrium condition verified by Zeroth Law. Measure of mean translational kinetic energy (\( \frac{1}{2}m\overline{v^2} = \frac{3}{2}k_B T \)).

Hydrostatic Pressure of Gases

Because the mass density of gases at ordinary pressures is small (\( \rho_{\text{air}} \approx 1.2\text{ kg/m}^3 \)), the hydrostatic gravitational pressure variation across standard laboratory containers (\( \Delta P = \rho g \Delta h \approx 1.2 \times 9.8 \times 1 \approx 12\text{ Pa} \)) is negligible compared to atmospheric pressure (\( \approx 10^5\text{ Pa} \)).

Under normal conditions, gas pressure inside a container is strictly homogeneous and isotropic: it exerts identical pressure in all directions and on all interior walls regardless of orientation.

Manometers and Barometers in Gas Systems

1. Open-End U-Tube Manometer

A U-tube containing a liquid of density \( \rho \) (typically mercury, \( \rho_{\text{Hg}} = 13.6 \times 10^3\text{ kg/m}^3 \)) has one limb connected to a gas container and the other open to atmosphere (\( P_0 \)).

  • If the open limb level is higher by \( h \): \[ P = P_0 + \rho g h \]
  • If the connected limb level is higher by \( h \): \[ P = P_0 - \rho g h \]

2. Closed-End U-Tube Manometer

The outer limb is evacuated and sealed (Torricellian vacuum, \( P_{\text{vacuum}} \approx 0 \)).

\[ P_{\text{gas}} = \rho g h \]

3. Simple Mercury Barometer

An inverted glass tube filled with mercury submerged in a mercury trough establishes atmospheric pressure:

\[ P_{\text{atm}} = \rho_{\text{Hg}} g h = 13600 \times 9.8 \times 0.760 \approx 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa} = 760\text{ mmHg} \]

Thermal Scales and Absolute Zero

The relationship between standard temperature measurement scales is given by:

\[ \frac{C - 0}{100} = \frac{F - 32}{180} = \frac{K - 273.15}{100} = \frac{R - 491.67}{180} \]

Concept of Absolute Zero (\( T = 0\text{ K} = -273.15^\circ\text{C} \)):

Extrapolating Charles' and Gay-Lussac's empirical lines to zero volume or zero pressure reveals an absolute lower temperature limit:

Absolute Zero: The theoretical baseline temperature at which the volume and pressure of an ideal gas extrapolate to zero. Microscopically, it is the state of minimum possible kinetic energy of matter.
