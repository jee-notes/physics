State Variables

Thermodynamic state coordinates and their measurements.

Concept

The thermal state of bulk matter is characterised by its temperature \(T\). However, the temperature of bulk matter can be changed through more than one kind of energy exchange.

  • Supplying Heat: For example, heating a body over a hot flame can increase its temperature.
  • Mechanical Work: For example, continuous rubbing or mechanical vibration can also increase the temperature of the body.

In both cases, the thermal state of the bulk matter changes: \[ T_1 \rightarrow T_2 \] However, the mode of energy exchange is different. In the first case, energy is transferred thermally as heat, whereas in the second case, energy is transferred mechanically as work.

A change in temperature does not tell us how the energy was transferred.
The same change in thermal state can be produced either by heat transfer or by mechanical work.

Such changes in thermal state of bulk matter - through energy exchange with its surroundings, are studied in thermodynamics.

For ordinary solids and liquids, the thermal state is described by their temperature \(T\), while the mechanical state is treated independently.

For gases, the thermal and mechanical states are coupled and a change in its mechanical condition can affect its thermal condition and vice versa.

When a gas undergoes rapid expansion, its mechanical state changes and its temperature decreases.

This cooling on expansion is an important practical principle used in refrigerators and air conditioners, in which the gas is first compressed by a compressor and then allowed to expand through an expansion device, causing its pressure and temperature to decrease.

Therefore, temperature alone is not sufficient to describe the thermodynamic state of a gas. We use the state variables \[ P,\;V,\;T,\;n \] to describe its thermodynamic state.

For a fixed sample of gas, \(n\) remains fixed and the remaining state variables are related. For an ideal gas, \[ PV=nRT \] Thus, any two independent variables among \(P\), \(V\), and \(T\) can be used to specify the equilibrium state of the sample.

State Variable vs Process
\(P\), \(V\), \(T\), and \(n\) describe the state of the gas.
Heat \(Q\) and work \(W\) describe energy exchange during a process.
A thermodynamic process changes the state variables of the gas as the gas interacts with its surroundings.

We say that a sample of gas (of \(n\) moles) has undergone a thermodynamic process, when one or more of its state variables \(P\), \(V\), and \(T\) change with time.

In a cyclic process, these variables may return to their initial values after the process is completed, just as a moving body may return to its initial position after undergoing a finite displacement during its motion.

Thermodynamic State Variables and their Measurements

The mechanical condition of a gas is described by two state variables:

  • Pressure (\(P\))
  • Volume (\(V\))

Volume (\(V\))

We start with volume because the volume of a gas is not inherent to the material in the same way as the volume of a solid or liquid.

A gas has no definite volume of its own; its volume is determined by the boundary enclosing it.

We know that a state variable has to be a property of the state. So, if the volume of a gas is determined by its enclosing boundary, how can it be a state variable?
The answer is that the volume is inherent to the gas system in its particular state, even though it is not an inherent geometrical property of the gas material itself. The state of the gas includes the spatial region available to it.

The volume of a gas is therefore defined as the region of space available for the gas molecules to perform translational motion.

For a gas completely enclosed within a vessel, this is equal to the internal volume of the vessel.

We need to be careful in measuring volume in different units. The SI unit of volume is \[ 1\,\mathrm{m^3} \] A commonly used unit is the litre: \[ 1\,\mathrm{L}=1\,\mathrm{dm^3}=10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3} \] Also, \[ 1\,\mathrm{mL}=1\,\mathrm{cm^3}=1\,\mathrm{cc} \] and \[ 1000\,\mathrm{mL}=1\,\mathrm{L} \]

Pressure (\( P \))

Pressure of a gas is defined as the normal force exerted by the gas on unit surface area of the enclosing walls:

\[ P = \frac{F_\perp}{A} \]

SI Unit: Pascal (\(\text{Pa} = \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}\))

Dimensional Formula: \([M^1 L^{-1} T^{-2}]\)

Like temperature, pressure is an intensive property, whereas volume is an extensive property. Therefore, pressure is conventionally preferred over volume as the mechanical variable for describing the thermodynamic state of a gas.

Standard Pressure Units & Conversions:

Unit Symbol Equivalent in Pascal (\(\text{Pa}\))
Standard Atmosphere \(\text{atm}\) \(1\text{ atm} = 1.01325 \times 10^5\text{ Pa} = 101.325\text{ kPa}\)
Bar \(\text{bar}\) \(1\text{ bar} = 10^5\text{ Pa} = 0.98692\text{ atm}\)
Torr \(\text{torr} / \text{mmHg}\) \(1\text{ atm} = 760\text{ mmHg} = 760\text{ torr} \implies 1\text{ torr} \approx 133.322\text{ Pa}\)

Both pressure and volume are state variables that describe the mechanical state of a gas. However, the mechanical condition of the gas is established by its interaction with the external surroundings.

In a rigid container, the volume of the gas is constrained by the container: \[ V_{\text{gas}}=V_{\text{container}} \]

In a container with a freely movable piston, the pressure of the gas is constrained by the external mechanical condition. At equilibrium, \[ P_{\text{gas}}=P_{\text{ext}} \]

Thus, the same state variables \(P\) and \(V\) may be constrained in different ways depending on the physical arrangement.

What is important is that the gas is in thermodynamic equilibrium, so that its state variables have definite values.

Temperature (\( T \))

The thermal condition of a gas is represented by its Temperature (\(T\)).

At the macroscopic level, temperature is the measure of the thermal condition of the gas.

It is an intensive state variable and is used to describe its thermal state.

At the microscopic level, temperature has a physical interpretation in terms of the average kinetic energy of molecular translational motion.

For an ideal gas, \[ \langle K_{\mathrm{trans}} \rangle = \frac{3}{2}k_B T \] The microscopic interpretation of temperature is developed in the sister chapter on Kinetic Theory of Gases (KTG).

SI Unit: Kelvin (\(\text{K}\))

Dimensional Formula: \([K^1]\)

Amount of Substance (\( n \))

The amount of substance tells us how much gas is present, measured in moles:

\[ n=\frac{m}{M}=\frac{N}{N_A} \]

Here, the units are important. The unit used for the molar mass \(M\) must be consistent with the unit used for the mass of the gas sample \(m\).

In chemistry, we often write quantities such as the molecular weight of nitrogen gas as \[ M(\mathrm{N}_2)=28 \] These numbers are dimensionless relative masses. They are not exactly same as the molar mass \(M\) used in \[ n=\frac{m}{M} \]

The corresponding molar mass of nitrogen gas is \[ M(\mathrm{N}_2)=28\,\mathrm{g/mol} \] Thus, if the sample mass is expressed in grams, \(M\) must be used in \(\mathrm{g/mol}\).

In SI units, the same molar mass is \[ M(\mathrm{N}_2)=28\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg/mol} \] Therefore, if \(m\) is expressed in kilograms, \(M\) must be expressed in \(\mathrm{kg/mol}\).

Unit Consistency: \[ n=\frac{m}{M} \] the units of \(m\) and \(M\) must be consistent. For example, \[ \frac{\mathrm g}{\mathrm{g/mol}}=\mathrm{mol} \qquad \frac{\mathrm{kg}}{\mathrm{kg/mol}}=\mathrm{mol} \]

Other Variables as State Variables

We say that in mechanics, the mechanical state of a body is represented by its position and velocity.

However, position and velocity are only considered to be the fundamental state variables in our usual description. We can use other variables to represent the same state. For example, velocity can be replaced by momentum: \[ \vec p=m\vec v \] Similarly, position can be represented through its potential energy when the potential-energy function is known: \[ PE=U(\vec r) \] Therefore, the mechanical state can also be represented using quantities such as kinetic energy and potential energy.

The point is that different variables can provide the same information about the state.

Similarly, for gases, the thermodynamic state can be represented using other state quantities such as internal energy or, at the microscopic level, the average speed of the molecules.

So, as a convention, we consider \[ \boxed{P,\;V,\;T,\;n} \] as the fundamental state variables of a gas.

These are fundamental state variables, but they are not all independent because they are related by an equation of state. For an ideal gas: \[ PV=nRT \]

As a convention, for a fixed sample of gas, we take \(P\) and \(T\) as the two independent state variables, while \(V\) is then determined from the equation of state.
