Critical Phenomena & Constants

Andrews isotherms, cubic equation derivations of critical constants, critical compressibility, and Boyle temperature.

Andrews Isotherms of Carbon Dioxide

Thomas Andrews (1869) conducted pioneering experiments measuring the pressure-volume isotherms of \( \text{CO}_2 \) at various constant temperatures, providing the first clear thermodynamic map of vapor-liquid phase transitions.

  1. For \( T > T_c \): The isotherms are smooth, hyperbolic-like curves. No amount of applied pressure can liquefy the gas. The substance exists exclusively as a supercritical fluid/gas.
  2. At \( T = T_c \): The horizontal coexistence plateau shrinks to a single Point of Inflection (Critical Point \( C \)) where: \[ \left( \frac{\partial P}{\partial V_m} \right)_{T_c} = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \left( \frac{\partial^2 P}{\partial V_m^2} \right)_{T_c} = 0 \]
  3. For \( T < T_c \): The isotherm exhibits three regions:
    • Right branch: Unsaturated vapor.
    • Horizontal flat line: Coexistence region where vapor condenses into liquid at constant saturation vapor pressure.
    • Steep left branch: Pure liquid phase (highly incompressible).

Critical Temperature, Pressure, and Volume

1. Critical Temperature (\( T_c \))

The maximum temperature at which a gas can be liquefied by application of pressure alone. Above \( T_c \), the kinetic energy of molecules exceeds the maximum attractive potential energy, making liquefaction impossible.

2. Critical Pressure (\( P_c \))

The minimum pressure required to liquefy a gas at its critical temperature \( T_c \).

3. Critical Molar Volume (\( V_c \))

The molar volume occupied by one mole of the gas at critical temperature \( T_c \) and critical pressure \( P_c \).

Derivation of Critical Constants from van der Waals Equation

Expanding the van der Waals equation for 1 mole of gas:

\[ \left( P + \frac{a}{V_m^2} \right)(V_m - b) = RT \] \[ P V_m - P b + \frac{a}{V_m} - \frac{ab}{V_m^2} = RT \]

Multiplying the entire equation by \( \frac{V_m^2}{P} \) to form a standard cubic polynomial in \( V_m \):

\[ V_m^3 - \left( b + \frac{RT}{P} \right)V_m^2 + \left( \frac{a}{P} \right)V_m - \frac{ab}{P} = 0 \]

A cubic equation possesses three mathematical roots for \( V_m \). At the critical state (\( T = T_c, P = P_c \)), all three roots become real, identical, and coalesce at \( V_m = V_c \):

\[ (V_m - V_c)^3 = 0 \]
\[ V_m^3 - 3 V_c V_m^2 + 3 V_c^2 V_m - V_c^3 = 0 \]

Comparing coefficients of like powers of \( V_m \) between the two polynomials:

  1. Coefficient of \( V_m^2 \): \[ 3 V_c = b + \frac{R T_c}{P_c} \quad \text{--- (1)} \]
  2. Coefficient of \( V_m \): \[ 3 V_c^2 = \frac{a}{P_c} \quad \text{--- (2)} \]
  3. Constant term: \[ V_c^3 = \frac{ab}{P_c} \quad \text{--- (3)} \]

Solving for the Critical Constants:

Dividing Eq. (3) by Eq. (2):

\[ \frac{V_c^3}{3 V_c^2} = \frac{\left(\frac{ab}{P_c}\right)}{\left(\frac{a}{P_c}\right)} \implies \frac{V_c}{3} = b \implies \boxed{V_c = 3b} \]

Substituting \( V_c = 3b \) into Eq. (2):

\[ 3(3b)^2 = \frac{a}{P_c} \implies 27 b^2 = \frac{a}{P_c} \implies \boxed{P_c = \frac{a}{27 b^2}} \]

Substituting \( V_c \) and \( P_c \) into Eq. (1):

\[ 3(3b) = b + \frac{R T_c}{\left(\frac{a}{27 b^2}\right)} \implies 9b - b = \frac{27 b^2 R T_c}{a} \] \[ 8b = \frac{27 b^2 R T_c}{a} \implies \boxed{T_c = \frac{8a}{27 R b}} \]

Critical Compressibility Factor

The value of the compressibility factor evaluated precisely at the critical point:

\[ Z_c = \frac{P_c V_c}{R T_c} = \frac{\left( \frac{a}{27 b^2} \right) (3b)}{R \left( \frac{8a}{27 R b} \right)} = \frac{\frac{3a}{27 b}}{\frac{8a}{27 b}} = \frac{3}{8} = 0.375 \]
According to the van der Waals model, the critical compressibility factor \( Z_c = 0.375 \) is a universal constant for all gases, independent of their chemical identity or specific values of \( a \) and \( b \). (Experimental values for real gases typically lie between \( 0.28 \) and \( 0.30 \)).

Boyle Temperature and Inversion Temperature

1. Boyle Temperature (\( T_B \))

The Boyle Temperature is the specific temperature at which a real gas obeys the ideal gas law (\( PV = nRT \)) over an appreciably wide range of pressures in the low-pressure limit (\( Z \approx 1 \)).

At \( T_B \), the second virial coefficient vanishes: \( B(T_B) = b - \frac{a}{RT_B} = 0 \):

\[ T_B = \frac{a}{R b} \]

2. Inversion Temperature (\( T_i \))

The temperature below which a gas undergoes cooling upon adiabatic throttling (Joule-Thomson expansion):

\[ T_i = \frac{2a}{R b} = 2 T_B \]
  • If \( T < T_i \): Joule-Thomson expansion causes cooling.
  • If \( T > T_i \): Joule-Thomson expansion causes heating. (For \( \text{H}_2 \) and \( \text{He} \), \( T_i \) is far below room temperature, so they warm up when expanded at room temperature unless pre-cooled).
  • If \( T = T_i \): Neither cooling nor heating occurs.
