Gaseous State

Macroscopic thermodynamics of gases, empirical laws of state, mechanical boundary constraints, and non-ideal behavior.

Conceptual Overview

Matter exists in distinct thermodynamic states of aggregation. In the gaseous state, thermal kinetic energy of molecules vastly overwhelms intermolecular attractive potential energy. Consequently, gas molecules exhibit chaotic, high-velocity translational motion, filling any enclosing boundary uniformly and exerting isotropic pressure upon the confining walls.

The Gaseous State chapter establishes the macroscopic, classical description of gases. While the companion chapter on the Kinetic Theory of Gases (KTG) focuses on microscopic statistical mechanics, this chapter focuses on measurable macroscopic state variables (\( P, V, T, n \)), empirical gas laws, the ideal equation of state, gas mixtures, mechanical constraints (manometers, pistons, capillary tubes, barometers), and the non-ideal corrections of real gases described by the van der Waals equation.

In gases, volume is not an intrinsic material constant—it is an extrinsic boundary constraint imposed entirely by the enclosing vessel. Pressure and temperature serve as the primary conjugate thermodynamic coordinates.

Thermodynamic State Variables of Gases

The thermodynamic equilibrium state of a pure gas is uniquely characterized by four fundamental state parameters:

  • Pressure (\( P \)): The perpendicular compressive force exerted per unit area by colliding gas molecules on container boundaries (\( P = F_\perp / A \)).
  • Volume (\( V \)): The total three-dimensional geometrical space accessible for molecular motion (equal to vessel capacity).
  • Absolute Temperature (\( T \)): The intensive thermal parameter dictating the average translational kinetic energy of molecules, measured on the absolute Kelvin scale.
  • Amount of Substance (\( n \)): The number of moles of gas present (\( n = m/M = N/N_A \)).

The Ideal Gas Model and Empirical Laws

The Ideal Gas Model is a theoretical construct wherein:

  1. The physical volume occupied by the gas molecules themselves is strictly negligible compared to the total container volume.
  2. Intermolecular attractive and repulsive forces between molecules are zero (except during instantaneous elastic collisions).

Historically, this model emerged by synthesizing individual empirical gas laws:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Boyle's Law (Constant } T, n\text{):} & \quad P \propto \frac{1}{V} \implies PV = \text{constant} \\ \text{Charles' Law (Constant } P, n\text{):} & \quad V \propto T \implies \frac{V}{T} = \text{constant} \\ \text{Gay-Lussac's Law (Constant } V, n\text{):} & \quad P \propto T \implies \frac{P}{T} = \text{constant} \\ \text{Avogadro's Law (Constant } P, T\text{):} & \quad V \propto n \implies \frac{V}{n} = \text{constant} \end{aligned} \]

Unifying these proportionalities yields the universal Ideal Gas Equation of State:

\[ PV = nRT = N k_B T = \frac{m}{M}RT \]

Gas Mixtures and Mechanical Constraints

For mixtures of non-reacting gases, Dalton's Law of Partial Pressures states that the total pressure equals the sum of partial pressures exerted by each constituent:

\[ P_{\text{total}} = \sum P_i = \sum \frac{n_i R T}{V} \]

Furthermore, analyzing gases under physical and mechanical constraints—such as connected bulbs with stopcocks, capillary tubes with trapped mercury pellets, open and closed manometers, and faulty barometers—forms a central pillar of JEE problem solving.

Real Gas Deviations and Critical Phenomena

At high pressures and low temperatures, real gases deviate significantly from ideal gas behavior due to finite molecular volume (excluded volume \( b \)) and long-range intermolecular van der Waals attractions (pressure correction \( a/V_m^2 \)).

\[ \left( P + \frac{a n^2}{V^2} \right)(V - nb) = nRT \]

The van der Waals equation quantitatively predicts continuous vapor-liquid phase transitions, critical constants (\( T_c, P_c, V_c \)), and the conditions required for gas liquefaction.
