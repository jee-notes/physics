Empirical Gas Laws

Boyle's, Charles', Gay-Lussac's, and Avogadro's laws.

Historical Development

The study of gases did not directly begin with gas laws. We need some discussion about the natural phenomena and experimental developments that led scientists to study gases systematically and eventually contributed to the development of thermodynamics.

People initially were familiar with many natural phenomena involving gases. Air is all around us and we breathe it. Wind can exert mechanical effects, and windmills were built to make use of those effects. Combustion depends on air, while the conversion of water into vapour during boiling and evaporation shows that a substance such as water can exist in a gaseous state.

The major difficulty was that gases are invisible and do not have a definite shape or volume of their own. To study gases experimentally, scientists had to first learn how to contain, isolate and manipulate them physically.

The development of the air pump in the seventeenth century made it possible to remove air from vessels and to study the mechanical effects of air under controlled conditions. Robert Boyle and Robert Hooke used an air pump and glass apparatus to investigate the physical behaviour of air and showed that air could be compressed and expanded and that its mechanical effects could be measured.

A further major development was the pneumatic trough, which provided a practical method for collecting gases produced in chemical reactions. A vessel filled with water could be inverted in a trough of water, and the gas could then be introduced into the vessel. As the gas entered, it displaced the water and accumulated inside the vessel.

The development of the air pump, suitable glassware and the pneumatic trough transformed gases from invisible natural phenomena into experimentally accessible substances that could be contained, isolated, manipulated and measured.

Once gases could be collected and manipulated separately, another important discovery followed: air itself was not a single chemical substance but a mixture.

Chemists developed methods to isolate different kinds of "air" from ordinary atmospheric air and from chemical reactions. Joseph Black isolated carbon dioxide, in the 1750s. Joseph Priestley subsequently developed experimental methods for isolating several different gases, including the gas now known as oxygen. His work helped establish what became known as pneumatic chemistry.

Lavoisier's work further established the chemical distinction between different gases and showed, through quantitative experiments, that gases could participate in chemical reactions and also be produced from other substances. His experiments with hydrogen and oxygen demonstrated that water itself could be formed from two gases, showing that the gaseous state was not merely a property of atmospheric air but could occur for chemically distinct substances.

Once different gases could be isolated and measured, scientists could move from qualitative observations to quantitative questions: How are the measurable physical properties (such as pressure, volume and temperature) of chemically different gas related? Do different gases obey the same physical relationships?

This led to a sequence of controlled experiments in which scientists varied one state variable while keeping others fixed. Boyle studied the relation between pressure and volume; Charles and later Gay-Lussac studied the relation between volume and temperature; and Avogadro proposed the relation between volume and amount of gas.

The study of gases was historically pursued by both physicists and chemists.
Physicists were interested in the physical behaviour of gases, while chemists were interested in their composition and chemical transformations.
Thermodynamics developed through the combined study of such physical processes, together with the broader study of heat and mechanical energy exchange.
In Chemistry, the study of gases is also connected with the Laws of Chemical Combination and chemical changes, which are essential for understanding matter.

The important point is that these gas laws were initially empirical. Scientists first discovered the regularities experimentally; the microscopic explanation of why different gases exhibit such behaviour came later through the development of the kinetic theory of gases.

Boyle's Law (Isothermal Processes)

Formulated by Robert Boyle in 1662:

Boyle's Law: For a fixed mass (\( n = \text{constant} \)) of an ideal gas maintained at constant temperature (\( T = \text{constant} \)), the pressure exerted by the gas is inversely proportional to its volume: \[ P \propto \frac{1}{V} \Leftrightarrow P V = \text{constant} = k_1 \] \[ P_1 V_1 = P_2 V_2 \]

Physical Mechanism:
When the volume of a container is halved at constant temperature, the number density of gas molecules (\( N/V \)) doubles.
Consequently, the frequency of molecular collisions per unit wall area doubles, leading to twice the macroscopic pressure.

Real-World Examples:

  • The Mechanics of Breathing: When your diaphragm contracts, chest volume increases. This causes the internal pressure to drop below atmospheric pressure (\(P \propto 1/V\)), drawing air into your lungs.
  • Scuba Diver's Bubbles: Bubbles exhaled at depth expand drastically as they rise to the surface because the surrounding water pressure decreases.
  • The Syringe Trick: If you plug an empty syringe with your finger and push the plunger, the volume decreases and the pressure immediately increases, pushing back against your thumb.

Charles' Law (Isobaric Processes)

Formulated by Jacques Charles (1787) and Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac (1802):

Charles' Law: For a fixed mass (\( n = \text{constant} \)) of an ideal gas maintained at constant pressure (\( P = \text{constant} \)), the volume occupied by the gas is directly proportional to its absolute thermodynamic temperature: \[ V \propto T \Leftrightarrow \frac{V}{T} = \text{constant} = k_2 \] \[ \frac{V_1}{T_1} = \frac{V_2}{T_2} \]

Celsius scale:

Starting from volume \( V_0 \) at \( 0^\circ\text{C} \), the volume at temperature \( t^\circ\text{C} \) is:

\[ V_t = V_0 \left( 1 + \alpha_v t \right) = V_0 \left( 1 + \frac{t}{273.15} \right) \]

where \( \alpha_v = \frac{1}{273.15} \, ^\circ\text{C}^{-1} \) is the volume expansion coefficient of all ideal gases.

Real-World Examples:

  • The Dented Ping-Pong Ball: Dropping a dented (but uncracked) ping-pong ball into hot water heats the trapped air. The volume expands (\(V \propto T\)) and pushes the plastic back into a perfect sphere.
  • Hot Air Balloons: Heating the air inside an open-bottom balloon causes the gas to expand. This displaces some air, making the remaining air inside less dense than the outside air, creating lift.
  • The Freezer Balloon: A balloon placed in a freezer will noticeably shrink as the temperature drops, re-expanding when brought back to room temperature.

Gay-Lussac's Law (Isochoric Processes)

Also known as Amontons' Law of Pressure-Temperature:

Gay-Lussac's Law: For a fixed mass (\( n = \text{constant} \)) of an ideal gas maintained in a rigid container of constant volume (\( V = \text{constant} \)), the pressure of the gas is directly proportional to its absolute thermodynamic temperature: \[ P \propto T \Leftrightarrow \frac{P}{T} = \text{constant} = k_3 \] \[ \frac{P_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2}{T_2} \]

Celsius scale:

\[ P_t = P_0 \left( 1 + \beta_p t \right) = P_0 \left( 1 + \frac{t}{273.15} \right) \]

where \( \beta_p = \frac{1}{273.15} \, ^\circ\text{C}^{-1} \) is the pressure coefficient of an ideal gas.

Real-World Examples:

  • Aerosol Can Explosions: Heating a rigid aerosol can spikes the kinetic energy of the gas molecules. Because the volume is fixed, the internal pressure increases rapidly (\(P \propto T\)) until the can ruptures.
  • Winter Tire Deflation: On a freezing morning, a sharp drop in temperature causes a proportional drop in the air pressure inside a car tire, even though the physical volume of the tire remains largely unchanged.

Avogadro's Hypothesis and Molar Volume

Amedeo Avogadro (1811) proposed:

Avogadro's Law: Equal volumes of all gases under identical conditions of temperature and pressure contain an equal number of molecules, regardless of their chemical identity: \[ V \propto n \quad (\text{at constant } P, T) \]

Standard Molar Volumes:

  • Traditional STP (\( T = 273.15\text{ K} = 0^\circ\text{C}, P = 1\text{ atm} = 101.325\text{ kPa} \)): \[ V_m = \frac{RT}{P} = \frac{0.0821 \times 273.15}{1} \approx 22.4\text{ L/mol} \]
  • IUPAC Standard STP (\( T = 273.15\text{ K}, P = 1\text{ bar} = 100\text{ kPa} \)): \[ V_m = \frac{8.314 \times 273.15}{10^5} \approx 22.7\text{ L/mol} \]
  • NTP (Normal Temperature and Pressure: \( 20^\circ\text{C} = 293.15\text{ K}, 1\text{ atm} \)): \( V_m \approx 24.05\text{ L/mol} \)

Real-World Examples:

  • Blowing Up a Balloon: Every time you exhale into a balloon, you add more moles of gas (\(n\)) to the system. Assuming the pressure of the stretched rubber and the temperature remain relatively constant, the volume of the balloon must increase to accommodate the new particles (\(V \propto n\)).
  • Airbags in Car Crashes: When a crash is detected, a chemical reaction (usually the decomposition of sodium azide) rapidly produces a massive amount of nitrogen gas (\(n\)). This sudden influx of gas molecules instantly expands the volume of the nylon airbag before the occupant hits the steering wheel.

Summary of Empirical Gas Laws

Gas Law Variables Held Constant Variables That Change Relation
Boyle's Law \(T,\;n\) \(P,\;V\) \[ PV=\text{constant} \]
Charles' Law \(P,\;n\) \(V,\;T\) \[ \frac{V}{T}=\text{constant} \]
Gay-Lussac's Law \(V,\;n\) \(P,\;T\) \[ \frac{P}{T}=\text{constant} \]
Avogadro's Law \(P,\;T\) \(V,\;n\) \[ \frac{V}{n}=\text{constant} \]
Each empirical gas law is obtained by holding particular state variables constant and studying how the remaining variables respond.
