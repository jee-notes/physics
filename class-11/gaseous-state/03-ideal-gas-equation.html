Ideal Gas Equation

Equation of state and its application.

Derivation of the Ideal Gas Equation

The behaviour of an ideal gas over different thermodynamic states can be unified by combining the three fundamental empirical laws:

  1. According to Boyle's Law: \( V \propto \frac{1}{P} \) (at constant \( T, n \))
  2. According to Charles' Law: \( V \propto T \) (at constant \( P, n \))
  3. According to Avogadro's Law: \( V \propto n \) (at constant \( P, T \))

Combining these three simultaneous proportionalities:

\[ V \propto \frac{n T}{P} \implies V = R \frac{n T}{P} \]
\[ PV = nRT \]

where \( R \) is the Universal Gas Constant.

Alternative Formulations:

In terms of no. of particles (\( N \)) and Avogadro's Number (\( N_A \)) and Boltzmann's constant (\( k_B = \frac{R}{N_A} \)):

\[ PV = \left(\frac{N}{N_A}\right) R T = N \left(\frac{R}{N_A}\right) T = N k_B T \]

In terms of Total Mass (\( m \)) and Molar Mass (\( M \)):

\[ PV = \left(\frac{m}{M}\right) R T \]

Universal Gas Constants

Consider the ideal gas equation: \[ PV=nRT \] It could have been written in any mathematically equivalent form, such as \[ P=\frac{nRT}{V} \] However, the conventional form has a physical significance of its own.

The quantity \(PV\) represents the mechanical side of the gas state. Since \[ [P][V]=[\text{Force}\times\text{Length}]=[\text{Work}]=[\text{Energy}] \] \(PV\) has the dimensions of mechanical work or energy.

On the other side, \(nRT\) represents the corresponding thermal-energy scale of the gas. Thus, the ideal gas equation can be viewed as relating the mechanical and thermal conditions of an ideal gas: \[ \boxed{\text{Mechanical energy scale }PV=\text{Thermal energy scale }nRT} \]

The ideal gas equation is therefore not merely a relation among \(P\), \(V\), and \(T\). In the form \[ PV=nRT \] it directly connects the mechanical condition of the gas with its thermal condition by equating their corresponding energy scales.

This immediately tells us what the universal gas constant \(R\) represents: \[ R=\frac{PV}{nT} \] It is therefore the temperature-related energy scale per mole: \[ [R]=\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}} \]

The Avogadro number (\(N_A\)) also has the dimensions of per mole (\(mol^{-1}\)) as \(N_A = 6.023 \times 10^{23} mol^{-1}\).

Based on this, we have 2 universal gas constants for macroscopic (\(R\)) and microscopic universal gas constant is also called as Boltzmann's constant and is considered as a fundamental constant of nature to indicate temperature related form of energy (heat energy).

1. Universal Gas Constant (\( R \))

\( R \) is a universal physical constant representing the molar work done by a gas during isobaric thermal expansion per Kelvin:

\[ R = \frac{PV}{nT} = \frac{\text{Work}}{\text{Mole} \cdot \text{Temperature}} \]

Dimensional Formula: \([M^1 L^2 T^{-2} K^{-1} \text{mol}^{-1}]\)

System of Units Value of \( R \) Practical Application Context
SI Units \( 8.31 \, \text{J}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}\cdot\text{K}^{-1} \) (\(\approx \frac{25}{3}\)) Thermodynamic work, internal energy, speed of sound, SI units.
Litre-Atmosphere \( 0.08206 \, \text{L}\cdot\text{atm}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}\cdot\text{K}^{-1} \) (\(\approx \frac{1}{12}\)) Direct volume-pressure calculations in laboratory chemistry.
Calorie \( \approx 2 \, \text{cal}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}\cdot\text{K}^{-1} \) Calorimetry and thermal capacity problems.

2. Boltzmann Constant (\( k_B \))

The gas constant per individual molecule:

\[ k_B = \frac{R}{N_A} \approx \frac{8.314}{6.022 \times 10^{23}} \approx 1.3806 \times 10^{-23} \, \text{J}\cdot\text{K}^{-1} \]

Dimensional Formula: \([M^1 L^2 T^{-2} K^{-1}]\)

Avogadro Constant: The Macro–Micro Bridge

The ideal gas equation is initially written in terms of the amount of gas in moles: \[ PV=nRT \] Here, \(n\) represents the macroscopic amount of gas.

However, the gas is ultimately made up of individual molecules. The bridge between the molar description and the molecular description is the Avogadro constant \(N_A\): \[ N=nN_A \] where \(N\) is the total number of molecules.

Using \[ n=\frac{N}{N_A} \] in the ideal gas equation: \[ PV=\frac{N}{N_A}RT \] gives \[ \boxed{PV=Nk_BT} \] where \[ \boxed{k_B=\frac{R}{N_A}} \]

The Avogadro constant connects the macroscopic mole description of a gas with its microscopic particle description: \[ \boxed{n\;\Leftrightarrow{\;N_A\;}\;N} \] Consequently, \[ \boxed{R\;\Leftrightarrow{\;N_A\;}\;k_B} \] \(R\) expresses the temperature-related energy scale per mole, while \(k_B\) expresses the corresponding energy scale per molecule.

Thus, the same ideal-gas relation can be viewed at two levels: \[ \boxed{PV=nRT} \qquad \text{(molar description)} \] and \[ \boxed{PV=Nk_BT} \qquad \text{(molecular description)} \]

Gas Density and Molar Mass Relationships

From the mass form of the ideal gas law:

\[ PV = \frac{m}{M} R T \implies P M = \left(\frac{m}{V}\right) R T \]

Since mass density is \( \rho = \frac{m}{V} \):

\[ \rho = \frac{P M}{R T} \Leftrightarrow \frac{P}{\rho T} = \frac{R}{M} = \text{constant for a given gas} \]

Physical Implications of the Density Formula:

  1. Pressure Dependence: At constant temperature, \( \rho \propto P \). Isothermal compression of a gas causes a linear increase in its density with pressure.
  2. Temperature Dependence: At constant pressure, \( \rho \propto \frac{1}{T} \). Isobaric heating of a gas causes a decrease in density with temperature rise.
  3. Molar Mass Dependence: Under identical \( P \) and \( T \), \( \rho \propto M \). Heavier gases are denser and settle downward.

Open Systems

When a gas container is unsealed (open flask) or develops a leak, the mass/moles of gas inside the vessel change over time.

Heating of an Open Vessel:

Consider a rigid flask of fixed volume \( V \) open to the atmosphere. Because the flask is open, the interior pressure of whatever gas is there in the container, is clamped to the atmospheric pressure (\( P = P_0 = \text{constant} \)).

When heated from initial temperature \( T_1 \) to final temperature \( T_2 \):

\[ P_0 V = n_1 R T_1 = n_2 R T_2 \implies n_1 T_1 = n_2 T_2 \]
\[ \frac{n_2}{n_1} = \frac{T_1}{T_2} \]
  • Fraction of Gas Remaining in Vessel: \( f_{\text{remain}} = \frac{n_2}{n_1} = \frac{T_1}{T_2} \)
  • Fraction of Gas Expelled / Escaped: \[ f_{\text{expelled}} = \frac{n_1 - n_2}{n_1} = 1 - \frac{T_1}{T_2} = \frac{T_2 - T_1}{T_2} \]
Here we consider only heating and expulsion of gas from the vessel and neglect diffusion/mixing effects.
Cooling of an open vessel would involve intrusion of gas from the surroundings.
In that case, the entering gas and its thermodynamic state would need to be specified before the final state inside the vessel could be determined.

Solved Example:

An open glass bulb contains air at \( 27^\circ\text{C} \). To what temperature must it be heated so that one-third (\( 1/3 \)) of the air present initially is expelled into the atmosphere?

Solution:

Initial temperature \( T_1 = 27 + 273 = 300\text{ K} \). Fraction of air expelled:

\[ f_{\text{expelled}} = \frac{1}{3} \implies f_{\text{remain}} = \frac{n_2}{n_1} = 1 - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{3} \]

Applying \( n_1 T_1 = n_2 T_2 \):

\[ T_2 = T_1 \left( \frac{n_1}{n_2} \right) = 300 \left( \frac{3}{2} \right) = 450\text{ K} \]

In Celsius:

\[ t_2 = 450 - 273 = 177^\circ\text{C} \]
