Graham's Law of Effusion

Molecular orifice kinetics, differential rates of escape, pressure-drop effusion, and isotopic gas enrichment.

Mechanisms of Diffusion and Effusion

Although often discussed together, Diffusion and Effusion represent distinct physical processes:

Feature Diffusion Effusion
Physical Process The spontaneous mixing and transport of molecules of one gas into another through continuous inter-molecular collisions. The passage of gas molecules through an extremely small pinhole / orifice (diameter \( d \ll \) mean free path \( \lambda \)) into an evacuated region without molecular collisions.
Role of Collisions Dominates the process; diffusion coefficient \( D \propto \lambda \overline{v} \). Zero collisions during escape through the orifice.
Driving Potential Concentration / partial pressure gradient. Total pressure differential across the orifice.

Graham's Law of Effusion

Formulated empirically by Thomas Graham (1829):

Graham's Law of Effusion: Under identical conditions of temperature and pressure, the rate of effusion (\( r \)) of a gas through a tiny pinhole is inversely proportional to the square root of its mass density (\( \rho \)) or its molar mass (\( M \)): \[ r \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho}} \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{M}} \] \[ \frac{r_1}{r_2} = \sqrt{\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1}} = \sqrt{\frac{M_2}{M_1}} = \sqrt{\frac{\text{V.D.}_2}{\text{V.D.}_1}} \]

where \(\text{V.D.} = M/2\) is the vapor density of the gas.

Dependence on Pressure, Area, Temperature, and Molar Mass

From the kinetic theory of gases, the collision frequency of gas molecules with a unit wall area per second is given by:

\[ Z_{\text{wall}} = \frac{1}{4} n_V \bar{v} = \frac{1}{4} \left(\frac{P}{k_B T}\right) \sqrt{\frac{8 k_B T}{\pi m}} = \frac{P}{\sqrt{2\pi m k_B T}} \]

For an orifice of small cross-sectional area \( A \) (where diameter is much smaller than mean free path \( \lambda \)), every molecule striking the hole escapes. The rate of effusion (molecules escaping per second) is:

\[ \text{Rate of Effusion } r = \frac{dN}{dt} = A \cdot Z_{\text{wall}} = \frac{P A}{\sqrt{2\pi m k_B T}} = \frac{P A N_A}{\sqrt{2\pi M R T}} \]
Comprehensive Proportionality of Effusion Rate: \[ r \propto \frac{P \cdot A}{\sqrt{M \cdot T}} \] For two gases effusing through identical apertures (\( A_1 = A_2 \)): \[ \frac{r_1}{r_2} = \frac{P_1}{P_2} \sqrt{\frac{M_2 T_2}{M_1 T_1}} \]

Time, Volume, and Pressure-Drop Effusion Calculations

The rate of effusion can be expressed in terms of diverse experimentally measurable parameters:

1. In terms of Volume Effused (\( V \)) in Time (\( t \)):

\[ r = \frac{V}{t} \implies \frac{r_1}{r_2} = \frac{V_1 / t_1}{V_2 / t_2} = \sqrt{\frac{M_2}{M_1}} \]
  • For equal volumes effused (\( V_1 = V_2 \)): \( \frac{t_1}{t_2} = \sqrt{\frac{M_1}{M_2}} \) (Heavier gas takes longer).
  • For equal time elapsed (\( t_1 = t_2 \)): \( \frac{V_1}{V_2} = \sqrt{\frac{M_2}{M_1}} \) (Lighter gas effuses larger volume).

2. In terms of Moles Effused (\( n \)):

\[ r = \frac{n}{t} \implies \frac{n_1 / t_1}{n_2 / t_2} = \frac{P_1}{P_2}\sqrt{\frac{M_2}{M_1}} \]

3. In terms of Distance Travelled in a Diffusion Tube:

If two gases (e.g., \( \text{NH}_3 \) and \( \text{HCl} \)) diffuse toward each other from opposite ends of a glass tube of length \( L \):

\[ \frac{d_1}{d_2} = \frac{r_1}{r_2} = \sqrt{\frac{M_{\text{HCl}}}{M_{\text{NH}_3}}} = \sqrt{\frac{36.5}{17}} \approx 1.46 \]

Example: Effusion Time Comparison

\( 50\text{ mL} \) of hydrogen gas effuses through a pinhole in \( 20\text{ seconds} \). Under identical conditions of temperature and pressure, calculate the time required for \( 40\text{ mL} \) of oxygen gas to effuse through the same pinhole.

Solution:

Given: \( V_1 = 50\text{ mL}, t_1 = 20\text{ s}, M_1 = 2\text{ g/mol} (\text{H}_2) \); \( V_2 = 40\text{ mL}, M_2 = 32\text{ g/mol} (\text{O}_2) \).

\[ \frac{V_1 / t_1}{V_2 / t_2} = \sqrt{\frac{M_2}{M_1}} \implies \frac{50 / 20}{40 / t_2} = \sqrt{\frac{32}{2}} = \sqrt{16} = 4 \] \[ \frac{2.5}{40 / t_2} = 4 \implies \frac{2.5 \cdot t_2}{40} = 4 \implies t_2 = \frac{160}{2.5} = 64\text{ seconds} \]

Isotopic Separation by Fractional Effusion

Because isotopic molecules have slightly different molecular weights, their effusion rates differ:

\[ \alpha = \frac{r_1}{r_2} = \sqrt{\frac{M_2}{M_1}} \]

For gaseous uranium hexafluoride isotopes \( ^{235}\text{UF}_6 \) (\( M_1 = 349\text{ g/mol} \)) and \( ^{238}\text{UF}_6 \) (\( M_2 = 352\text{ g/mol} \)):

\[ \alpha = \sqrt{\frac{352}{349}} \approx 1.0043 \]

Although the single-stage separation factor \( \alpha \) is tiny (\( 0.43\% \) enrichment), passing the gas mixture through thousands of successive porous effusion barriers in a cascade separates pure \( ^{235}\text{U} \) for nuclear reactors.
