System of Particles

From individual particles to collective motion — the centre of mass, momentum, and the physics of interactions.

Conceptual Overview

In earlier chapters, we studied individual particles acted upon by forces. Real-world objects, however, are extended — they consist of many particles interacting with one another. The system-of-particles framework allows us to describe the collective motion of such an assembly without tracking every particle individually.

The central construction is the centre of mass (COM), a single point that represents the average position of the entire mass distribution. Newton's second law, when applied to the centre of mass, describes how the system as a whole responds to external forces — regardless of the complexity of internal interactions.

Centre of Mass as the Central Idea

The centre of mass is to a system of particles what a particle is to Newton's laws: the idealised point at which the total mass of the system can be considered concentrated. It decouples the overall translational motion of the system from the relative internal motions.

No matter how complex the internal forces — explosions, collisions, deformations — the centre of mass moves as if a single particle of total mass \( M \) were acted upon by the net external force:

\[ M\,\vec{a}_{\text{CM}} = \vec{F}_{\text{ext}} \]

This is Newton's second law applied to the system. Internal forces, by Newton's third law, always cancel in pairs and cannot affect the COM's motion.

Momentum and Its Conservation

The total linear momentum of a system of particles equals the total mass times the velocity of the centre of mass:

\[ \vec{P} = M\,\vec{v}_{\text{CM}} \]

Taking the time derivative of this equation yields Newton's second law for the system: \( \vec{F}_{\text{ext}} = \dfrac{d\vec{P}}{dt} \). The immediate consequence is:

If the net external force on a system is zero, the total momentum is conserved:

\[ \vec{P}_{\text{initial}} = \vec{P}_{\text{final}} \]

This holds in every direction independently. In a collision, even though individual particles change velocity dramatically, the total momentum remains constant — making momentum conservation the primary tool for analysing interactions.

Impulse and Force

A collision involves very large forces acting over very short times. The quantity that links force and the resulting change in momentum is impulse:

\[ \vec{J} = \int \vec{F}\,dt = \Delta \vec{p} \]

During a collision, the time-averaged impulsive force is typically enormous — far larger than external forces like gravity — which is why we can treat the system as isolated during the collision interval and apply momentum conservation.

Collisions: Elastic and Inelastic

Collisions are classified by whether kinetic energy is conserved in addition to momentum:

Elastic Collisions

Both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved. The objects separate after collision with no permanent deformation or energy loss. The relative speed of separation equals the relative speed of approach.

Inelastic Collisions

Momentum is conserved; kinetic energy is not. Some kinetic energy is converted to heat, sound, or deformation. In the extreme case of a perfectly inelastic collision, the objects stick together and maximum kinetic energy is lost (consistent with momentum conservation).

The Coefficient of Restitution

The coefficient of restitution \( e \) quantifies the nature of a collision:

\[ e = \frac{\text{relative speed of separation}}{\text{relative speed of approach}} \]
  • \( e = 1 \): perfectly elastic
  • \( 0 \lt e \lt 1 \): partially inelastic
  • \( e = 0 \): perfectly inelastic
