Centre of Mass

The mass-weighted average position of a system at a single point.

Definition

The centre of mass (COM) of a system of particles is the point about which the vector sum of the mass moments of all particles is zero.

Consider a system of \(n\) particles having masses \(m_1, m_2, \ldots, m_n\) located at position vectors \(\vec{r}_1, \vec{r}_2, \ldots, \vec{r}_n\) with respect to a chosen origin.

Let the position vector of the centre of mass be \(\vec{r}_c\). If \(\vec{r}_{ic}\) denotes the position vector of the \(i^{\text{th}}\) particle with respect to the centre of mass, then by definition,

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i \vec{r}_{ic}=0. \]

Since

\[ \vec{r}_{ic}=\vec{r}_i-\vec{r}_c \]

substituting into the above equation gives

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n}m_i(\vec{r}_i-\vec{r}_c) = 0 \]

Expanding the summation,

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n}m_i\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_c\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_i = 0 \]

Hence, the position vector of the centre of mass is

\[ \boxed{\vec{r}_c = \vec{r}_{\text{CM}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_i\vec{r}_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_i} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_i\vec{r}_i} \]
The quantity \(\displaystyle \sum m_i\vec{r}_i\) is the total mass moment of the system about the chosen origin.
Thus, the centre of mass is obtained by dividing the total mass moment by the total mass of the system.

Characteristics of the Centre of Mass

The centre of mass possesses several important properties that can be understood most easily by considering simple two-particle and three-particle systems.

  • Towards the heavier particle: The centre of mass always lies closer to the heavier particle or, more generally, towards the heavier region of the mass distribution.
  • Between the particles: For a two-particle system, the centre of mass always lies on the line joining the two particles. More generally, it lies within the overall region occupied by the mass distribution.
  • Independent of the choice of origin: The relative position of the centre of mass within a system depends only on the mass distribution and is completely independent of the origin chosen to describe the position vectors.
  • Collection is replaceable by point mass at centre of mass: While determining the centre of mass of a larger system, an entire subsystem may be replaced by a single point mass equal to its total mass placed at the centre of mass of that subsystem. This greatly simplifies the analysis of composite bodies.
Changing the origin changes the numerical values of the position vectors of every particle as well as that of the centre of mass by exactly the same amount.
Consequently, the relative location of the centre of mass within the system remains unchanged.

Two-Particle System

A two-particle system is the simplest non-trivial system of particles. Consider two point masses \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) separated by a finite distance \(d\).

Although the particles may be located anywhere in three-dimensional space, only the straight line joining them is relevant for determining the centre of mass.

Let \(\vec{r}_1\) and \(\vec{r}_2\) be the position vectors of \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) measured from the centre of mass. Then, by definition,

\[ m_1 \vec{r}_1 + m_2 \vec{r}_2 = 0 \]

Since \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) are positive scalars, the above equation implies that the vector sum of two non-zero vectors is zero.

The sum of two non-zero vectors can be zero only if they are collinear and directed oppositely. Therefore, the two mass-moment vectors \(m_1\vec{r}_1\) and \(m_2\vec{r}_2\) are anti-parallel.

Since multiplication by a positive scalar does not change the direction of a vector, \(\vec{r}_1\) and \(\vec{r}_2\) must also be anti-parallel. Consequently, the centre of mass, \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) all lie on the same straight line.

Furthermore, because \(\vec{r}_1\) and \(\vec{r}_2\) point in opposite directions from the centre of mass, the centre of mass must lie between the two particles.

Let us consider the center of mass is at origin. Particle \(m_1\) is towards its right at \(x_1\) and \(m_2\) is towards its left at \(- x_2\) and \(x_1 + x_2 = d\) . We have:

\[ m_1 x_1 + m_2 (-x_2) = 0 \implies m_1 x_1 = m_2 x_2 \implies \frac{m_1}{m_2} = \frac{x_2}{x_1} \]

Thus, the distance from the center of mass is inversely proportional to the mass.

To find relative distance from \(m_1\) , just do componendo:

\[ \frac{m_1}{m_2} = \frac{x_2}{x_1} \implies \frac{m_1 + m_2}{m_2} = \frac{x_2 + x_1}{x_1} = \frac{d}{x_1} \implies x_1 = \frac{m_2 d}{m_1 + m_2} \]

If we consider origin at \(m_1\):

\[ x_{\text{CM}} = \frac{m_1 (0) + m_2 d}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{m_2 d}{m_1 + m_2} \]

The relative distance of COM from \(m_1\) is same irrespective of the choice of origin.

Three-Particle System

A three-particle system is the simplest non-trivial system of particles forming a two-dimensional planar configuration.

Consider three point masses \(m_1\), \(m_2\) and \(m_3\) located at \((x_1,y_1)\), \((x_2,y_2)\) and \((x_3,y_3)\) respectively.

Using \( \vec{r}_{\text{CM}} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_i\vec{r}_i \) , we have:

\[ x_{\text{CM}} = x_{123} = \frac{m_1 x_1 + m_2 x_2 + m_3 x_3}{m_1 + m_2 + m_3} \] \[ y_{\text{CM}} = y_{123} = \frac{m_1 y_1 + m_2 y_2 + m_3 y_3}{m_1 + m_2 + m_3} \]

Now let us determine the same centre of mass in another way. First, consider only particles \(m_1\) and \(m_2\). Let their centre of mass be \((x_{12},y_{12})\). Replace these two particles by a single particle of mass \[ m_{12}=m_1+m_2 \] placed at \((x_{12},y_{12})\).

The centre of mass of the new two-particle system is

\[ x'_{\text{CM}} = \frac{m_{12} x_{12} + m_3 x_3}{m_{12} + m_3} \]

Since

\[ x_{12} = \frac{m_1 x_1 + m_2 x_2}{m_1 + m_2} \]

we have

\[ m_{12} x_{12} = (m_1 + m_2)\left(\frac{m_1 x_1 + m_2 x_2}{m_1 + m_2} \right) = m_1 x_1 + m_2 x_2 \]

Therefore,

\[ x'_{\text{CM}} = \frac{m_1 x_1 + m_2 x_2 + m_3 x_3}{m_1 + m_2 + m_3} = x_{123} \]

Similarly,

\[ y'_{\text{CM}}=y_{123} \]

Hence, replacing a subsystem by a single particle of equal mass placed at the centre of mass of that subsystem does not change the centre of mass of the entire system.

This result is extremely useful in mechanics.
A complicated collection of particles may be simplified step by step by repeatedly replacing subsystems with equivalent point masses located at their respective centres of mass.

Incremental COM Update

If there is a sub-system of particles of mass \({M'}_{\text{S}}\) at \(({x'}_{s},{y'}_{s})\) .

To this sub-system I want to add another particle of mass \(m_{\text{p}}\) at \(({x}_{p},{y}_{p})\) .

Note that adding to the system here does not mean that I am moving the system or the particle. It implies that I am re-defining the boundaries of the system to include the particle.

Then the new system has mass \({M'}_{\text{S}} + m_p = M_{\text{S}}\) and its center of mass is at:

\[ x_s = \frac{{M'}_{\text{S}} {x'}_{s} + m_{\text{p}} {x}_{p} }{{M'}_{\text{S}} + m_{\text{p}}} \] \[ y_s = \frac{{M'}_{\text{S}} {y'}_{s} + m_{\text{p}} {y}_{p} }{{M'}_{\text{S}} + m_{\text{p}}} \]

Similarly, If there is a sub-system of particles of mass \({M'}_{\text{S}}\) at \(({x'}_{s},{y'}_{s})\) .

From this sub-system I want to remove an existing particle of mass \(m_{\text{p}}\) at \(({x}_{p},{y}_{p})\) .

Note that removing from the system here does not mean that I am moving the system or the particle. It implies that I am re-defining the boundaries of the system to exclude the particle.

Then the new system has mass \({M'}_{\text{S}} - m_p = M_{\text{S}}\) and its center of mass is at:

\[ x_s = \frac{{M'}_{\text{S}} {x'}_{s} - m_{\text{p}} {x}_{p} }{{M'}_{\text{S}} - m_{\text{p}}} \] \[ y_s = \frac{{M'}_{\text{S}} {y'}_{s} - m_{\text{p}} {y}_{p} }{{M'}_{\text{S}} - m_{\text{p}}} \]

Definition: Continuous Bodies

For a continuous mass distribution, the sum becomes an integral. Replacing \(m_i\) with an infinitesimal mass element \(dm\):

\[ \vec{R}_{\text{CM}} = \frac{1}{M} \int \vec{r}\,dm \]

The mass element \(dm\) depends on the type of distribution:

  • Linear (rod, wire): \(dm = \lambda\,dl\), where \(\lambda\) is the linear mass density.
  • Surface (disc, plate): \(dm = \sigma\,dA\), where \(\sigma\) is the surface mass density.
  • Volume (sphere, cube): \(dm = \rho\,dV\), where \(\rho\) is the volume mass density.

Standard Results

Uniform Bodies with Symmetry

For any uniform body with a geometrical centre of symmetry, the COM lies at the geometric centre. This includes:

  • Uniform rod → midpoint
  • Uniform rectangular plate → intersection of diagonals
  • Uniform disc / ring → centre
  • Uniform sphere / spherical shell → centre
  • Uniform cube → body centre

Bodies Without Full Symmetry

  • Semicircular ring of radius \(R\): \(y_{\text{CM}} = \dfrac{2R}{\pi}\) from centre
  • Semicircular disc of radius \(R\): \(y_{\text{CM}} = \dfrac{4R}{3\pi}\) from centre
  • Hemisphere (solid) of radius \(R\): \(y_{\text{CM}} = \dfrac{3R}{8}\) from centre
  • Hemispherical shell of radius \(R\): \(y_{\text{CM}} = \dfrac{R}{2}\) from centre
  • Triangular lamina: at the centroid (intersection of medians), \(\frac{1}{3}\) of the way from each side
  • Solid cone of height \(h\): \(y_{\text{CM}} = \dfrac{h}{4}\) from base
  • Hollow cone of height \(h\): \(y_{\text{CM}} = \dfrac{h}{3}\) from base
These results are derived by integration and should be memorised for rapid use in exam problems.
The pattern is clear: hollow bodies have their COM further from the base than solid bodies.

COM of Bodies with Cavities

To find the COM of a body with a cavity, treat it as the original complete body minus the removed portion. If the complete body has mass \(M\) and COM at \(\vec{R}_1\), and the removed portion has mass \(m\) and COM at \(\vec{R}_2\):

\[ \vec{R}_{\text{CM}} = \frac{M\vec{R}_1 - m\vec{R}_2}{M - m} \]

This "negative mass" trick avoids complex integration with irregular boundaries.
