Linear Momentum of a System

The total momentum of a system equals the total mass times the velocity of the centre of mass.

Definition

The linear momentum of a single particle of mass \(m\) and velocity \(\vec{v}\) is:

\[\vec{p} = m\vec{v}\]

For a system of \(n\) particles, the total linear momentum is the vector sum of the individual momenta:

\[\vec{P} = \sum_{i=1}^n m_i \vec{v}_i\]

Since \(\vec{v}_{\text{CM}} = \frac{1}{M}\sum m_i \vec{v}_i\), this gives the fundamental relation:

\[\vec{P} = M\,\vec{v}_{\text{CM}}\]
The total momentum of any system — no matter how many particles, no matter how complex their motions — is simply the total mass times the velocity of the centre of mass. This remarkable result makes the COM the natural representative point for the system.

Newton's Second Law in Momentum Form

Newton's second law, in its most general form, relates force to the rate of change of momentum:

\[\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt}\]

For a system of particles (where internal forces cancel):

\[\vec{F}_{\text{ext}} = \frac{d\vec{P}}{dt} = M\,\vec{a}_{\text{CM}}\]

This is the same equation we derived from COM kinematics, but now written in terms of momentum. It is the more fundamental form because it also holds for variable-mass systems (like rockets), where \(M\) itself changes with time.

Momentum as a Vector

Momentum is a vector quantity. In two or three dimensions, momentum conservation must be applied component-wise:

\[P_x = \sum m_i v_{ix} = \text{const (if } F_{\text{ext},x} = 0)\] \[P_y = \sum m_i v_{iy} = \text{const (if } F_{\text{ext},y} = 0)\]

One component can be conserved even if the other is not. For example, during a projectile explosion, horizontal momentum is conserved (no horizontal external force) while vertical momentum is not (gravity acts).

Kinetic Energy and Momentum

Kinetic energy and momentum are related by:

\[K = \frac{p^2}{2m} \qquad \text{(single particle)}\]

For a system, the total kinetic energy splits into two parts:

\[K_{\text{total}} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}Mv_{\text{CM}}^2}_{\text{KE of COM}} + \underbrace{K_{\text{rel}}}_{\text{KE relative to COM}}\]

The first term is the kinetic energy of the centre-of-mass motion. The second term is the kinetic energy of particles relative to the COM. Only the second term is available for conversion during interactions; the first is locked by momentum conservation.

This decomposition explains why a perfectly inelastic collision does not lose all kinetic energy — the COM kinetic energy is preserved by momentum conservation. Only the relative kinetic energy is lost.
