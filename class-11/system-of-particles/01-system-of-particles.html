System of Particles

What defines a system, and why the distinction between internal and external forces matters.

What is a System of Particles?

A system of particles is any collection of two or more particles that we choose to analyse together. The "system" is defined by the physicist — we draw an imaginary boundary and classify everything inside as the system and everything outside as the surroundings.

Once the boundary is drawn, every force acting on every particle in the system falls into one of two categories:

  • Internal forces: Forces between particles within the system (e.g., tension in a rope connecting two blocks, gravitational pull between two masses in the system).
  • External forces: Forces exerted on the system by objects outside it (e.g., gravity from the Earth, applied push, friction from the ground).
The classification of a force as internal or external depends entirely on how you define your system. A force that is external for one choice of system may become internal if you expand the system boundary to include its source.

Why This Distinction Matters

By Newton's third law, internal forces always come in equal and opposite pairs. When you sum all forces on all particles, the internal forces cancel exactly:

\[\sum \vec{F}_{\text{internal}} = 0\]

Therefore, the net force on the system is due entirely to external forces:

\[\vec{F}_{\text{net on system}} = \sum \vec{F}_{\text{external}}\]

This is an extraordinarily powerful result. It means we can predict the overall motion of the system (through its centre of mass) without knowing anything about the internal forces — no matter how complex.

Examples of Systems

  • Two blocks connected by a spring: The spring force is internal; gravity and normal force from the surface are external.
  • A bullet and a gun: The explosion forces are internal; gravity is external. Total momentum is conserved if we neglect gravity during the short firing time.
  • Fragments of an exploding bomb: Explosion forces are internal. If the bomb was in free fall (no support), the centre of mass continues its parabolic trajectory undisturbed.
  • Two colliding balls: Contact forces during collision are internal. If the collision is brief, external forces have negligible impulse → momentum conserved.

Rigid Bodies as Systems

A rigid body is a special system in which the distance between any two particles remains constant. No matter what forces act, the body does not deform. This idealisation allows us to describe the motion of a rigid body purely in terms of:

  • Translation of the centre of mass, and
  • Rotation about the centre of mass.

The system-of-particles framework is the foundation for both translational dynamics (this chapter) and rotational dynamics (rotational motion chapter).

Every extended object is, at its core, a system of particles. The methods developed here — centre of mass, momentum conservation, impulse — do not require the object to be rigid and work just as well for deformable systems such as colliding bodies, explosions, and fluid flows.
