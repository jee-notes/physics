Motion of the Centre of Mass

The centre of mass obeys Newton's second law — regardless of internal complexity.

Why Study the Centre of Mass?

So far, we have studied how to locate the centre of mass of a stationary system of particles having either a discrete or a continuous mass distribution.

But why go through all this effort? The answer lies in dynamics. The true importance of the centre of mass is that it provides a remarkably simple way of describing the motion of an entire system of particles, regardless of its internal complexity.

Recall the expression for the position of the centre of mass:

\[ \vec{r}_{\text{CM}} = \frac{1}{M} \sum m_i\vec{r}_i \]

Velocity of the Centre of Mass

Differentiating the above equation with respect to time gives the velocity of the centre of mass:

\[ \vec{v}_{\text{CM}} = \frac{d\vec{r}_{\text{CM}}}{dt} = \frac{1}{M} \sum m_i\vec{v}_i \]

Now consider the momentum of a system of particles. If the \(i^{\text{th}}\) particle has momentum \(\vec{p}_i=m_i\vec{v}_i\), then the total momentum of the system is

\[ \vec{P} = \sum \vec{p}_i = \sum m_i\vec{v}_i \]

Comparing the above two equations, we obtain

\[ \boxed{\vec{P} = M\vec{v}_{\text{CM}}} \]
The total momentum of a system equals the total mass multiplied by the velocity of its centre of mass.
Thus, the velocity of the centre of mass completely represents the translational motion of the entire system.

Acceleration of the Centre of Mass

Differentiating the centre-of-mass velocity once more gives the acceleration of the centre of mass:

\[ \vec{a}_{\text{CM}} = \frac{d\vec{v}_{\text{CM}}}{dt} = \frac{1}{M}\sum m_i\vec{a}_i \]

Applying Newton's second law to every particle, \[ m_i\vec{a}_i=\vec{F}_i \] gives

\[ M\vec{a}_{\text{CM}} = \sum \vec{F}_i \]

Since the internal forces cancel pairwise by Newton's third law, only the external forces remain. Hence,

\[ \boxed{ M\vec{a}_{\text{CM}} = \sum \vec{F}_{\text{external}}} \]
This is Newton's second law for the system as a whole.
The centre of mass moves exactly as if the entire mass of the system were concentrated at the centre of mass and all external forces acted on that single particle.
No matter how large or complicated the internal forces are, they cannot alter the motion of the centre of mass.

Consequences

1. No External Force → COM Stays Put (or Moves Uniformly)

If \(\vec{F}_{\text{ext}} = 0\), then \(\vec{a}_{\text{CM}} = 0\), so \(\vec{v}_{\text{CM}} = \text{const}\).

If the system was initially at rest, the COM remains stationary even if individual particles move wildly.

Example: A man in a boat walks in the boat that is floating on a frictionless lake. As he walks one way, the boat drifts the other way such that their common COM does not move.

2. Projectile Explosion

A projectile in flight explodes into fragments. The internal explosion forces do not affect the COM.

Therefore, the COM continues along the original parabolic trajectory — even as fragments fly in all directions.

3. Binary Star System

Two stars orbiting each other in space experience no external forces. Both orbit their common COM, which either remains stationary or moves at constant velocity through space.

COM Frame of Reference

The centre of mass frame is a reference frame in which the centre of mass is at rest (\(\vec{v}_{\text{CM}} = 0\)). In this frame:

  • Total momentum is zero: \(\vec{P}_{\text{total}} = M\vec{v}_{\text{CM}} = 0\).
  • Collision problems simplify enormously, because the two objects approach each other with equal and opposite momenta.
  • The minimum kinetic energy of the system is zero (in the COM frame), and the kinetic energy in any other frame exceeds this by \(\frac{1}{2}Mv_{\text{CM}}^2\).
The COM frame is the "natural" frame for analysing interactions. The kinetic energy in the COM frame is the energy available for creating deformations, heat, or new particles — the rest is just the energy of bulk translation, which momentum conservation prevents from being used.
