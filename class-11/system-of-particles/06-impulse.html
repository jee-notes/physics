Impulse

Force integrated over time — the quantity that connects a collision's force profile to the resulting momentum change.

Definition

Impulse is defined as the integral of force over the time interval during which it acts:

\[\vec{J} = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \vec{F}\,dt\]

By Newton's second law (\(\vec{F} = \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt}\)), this integral evaluates to:

\[\vec{J} = \Delta \vec{p} = \vec{p}_f - \vec{p}_i\]

This is the impulse–momentum theorem: the impulse delivered to an object equals the change in its momentum.

Impulse is the "bridge" between force (which is instantaneous) and momentum change (which is a before-vs-after comparison). It tells us the total effect of a force over time, regardless of how complex the force's time profile may be.

Average Force and Impulse

The average force during a time interval \(\Delta t = t_2 - t_1\) is defined such that:

\[\vec{J} = \vec{F}_{\text{avg}} \cdot \Delta t\]

so:

\[\vec{F}_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\Delta \vec{p}}{\Delta t}\]

For the same momentum change, a shorter collision time implies a larger average force. This is why:

  • A cricketer pulls their hands back while catching a ball — increases \(\Delta t\), reduces \(F_{\text{avg}}\).
  • Car crumple zones extend the collision time, reducing the force on passengers.
  • A karate chop with a stiff hand maximises force by minimising contact time.

Impulsive Forces

During a collision, the contact forces are typically very large and act for a very short time (\(\sim 10^{-3}\) to \(10^{-2}\) s). These are called impulsive forces. Their impulse — the product \(F \cdot \Delta t\) — is finite and significant, even though the time is tiny.

During this short interval, non-impulsive forces (gravity, friction) deliver negligible impulse because their magnitude is small compared to the impulsive force:

\[J_{\text{gravity}} = mg\Delta t \approx 0 \qquad (\Delta t \to 0)\]

This is why momentum is approximately conserved during a collision.

Be careful: there are situations where "non-impulsive" forces can be large enough to not neglect during a collision — for example, a normal force from a rigid wall that suddenly appears at impact. Always check the physical setup.

Graphical Interpretation

On a force–time (\(F\text{–}t\)) graph, the impulse is the area under the curve between \(t_1\) and \(t_2\). This is analogous to the work–displacement relationship, where area under the \(F\text{–}x\) curve gives work.

  • Area above the time axis → positive impulse (momentum increases in the chosen direction).
  • Area below the time axis → negative impulse (momentum decreases).
  • Total impulse = net area = \(\Delta p\).

Impulse in a Bouncing Ball

A ball of mass \(m\) hits a wall with speed \(v\) and bounces back with speed \(ev\) (where \(e\) is the coefficient of restitution). Taking the direction toward the wall as positive:

\[p_i = +mv, \qquad p_f = -mev\] \[J = \Delta p = -mev - mv = -m(1+e)v\]

The magnitude of the impulse is \(m(1+e)v\). For a perfectly elastic bounce (\(e = 1\)), the impulse is \(2mv\) — exactly twice the incoming momentum.
