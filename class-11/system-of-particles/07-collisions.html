Collisions

Short-duration interactions analysed through momentum conservation, energy bookkeeping, and the coefficient of restitution.

What is a Collision?

A collision is a brief, intense interaction during which two or more objects exert large impulsive forces on each other. The defining characteristic is that the collision duration \(\Delta t\) is so small that external forces have negligible impulse during that interval.

Consequently, the total momentum of the colliding system is conserved across the collision. Whether kinetic energy is also conserved determines the type of collision.

Types of Collisions

1. Elastic Collision

Both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved:

\[\vec{P}_i = \vec{P}_f \qquad K_i = K_f\]

Objects bounce off each other without any permanent deformation. The relative speed of separation equals the relative speed of approach.

2. Inelastic Collision

Momentum is conserved; kinetic energy is not. Some kinetic energy is converted to internal energy (heat, deformation, sound):

\[\vec{P}_i = \vec{P}_f \qquad K_i > K_f\]

3. Perfectly Inelastic Collision

The extreme case: objects stick together and move with a common velocity. This produces the maximum kinetic energy loss consistent with momentum conservation:

\[v_f = \frac{m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2}{m_1 + m_2}\]
"Perfectly inelastic" does not mean "all kinetic energy is lost." The COM kinetic energy \(\frac{1}{2}(m_1+m_2)v_f^2\) is always preserved. Only the kinetic energy relative to the COM is fully lost.

Head-On Elastic Collision: Formulae

For a 1D elastic collision between \(m_1\) (speed \(u_1\)) and \(m_2\) (speed \(u_2\)), solving momentum and energy conservation simultaneously:

\[v_1 = \frac{(m_1 - m_2)\,u_1 + 2m_2\,u_2}{m_1 + m_2}\] \[v_2 = \frac{(m_2 - m_1)\,u_2 + 2m_1\,u_1}{m_1 + m_2}\]

Special Cases

  • Equal masses (\(m_1 = m_2\)): Velocities exchange. \(v_1 = u_2,\; v_2 = u_1\).
  • Heavy projectile (\(m_1 \gg m_2\)): \(v_1 \approx u_1\); \(v_2 \approx 2u_1 - u_2\). Target shoots off at nearly twice the projectile speed.
  • Light projectile (\(m_1 \ll m_2\)): \(v_1 \approx -u_1 + 2u_2\); \(v_2 \approx u_2\). Projectile bounces back, target barely moves.

Coefficient of Restitution

The coefficient of restitution \(e\) is the ratio of the relative speed of separation to the relative speed of approach along the line of impact:

\[e = \frac{v_2 - v_1}{u_1 - u_2}\]
  • \(e = 1\) → elastic
  • \(0 < e < 1\) → partially inelastic
  • \(e = 0\) → perfectly inelastic

Using \(e\) together with momentum conservation gives two equations for two unknowns (\(v_1, v_2\)), making it a complete and efficient method for solving collision problems even when energy conservation cannot be applied directly.

The coefficient of restitution is an empirical property of the material and surface pair. It encapsulates all the microscopic physics of deformation and recovery in a single dimensionless number.

Energy Lost in a Perfectly Inelastic Collision

For \(m_2\) initially at rest:

\[\Delta K = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2} \cdot u_1^2\]

This is the kinetic energy relative to the COM — exactly the fraction that can be converted to non-mechanical forms.

In general (with coefficient of restitution \(e\)):

\[\Delta K = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2} \cdot (1 - e^2)(u_1 - u_2)^2\]

Oblique (2D) Collisions

When two objects collide at an angle, the analysis is performed along two axes:

  • Along the line of impact (normal): Apply momentum conservation and the \(e\)-relation.
  • Perpendicular to the line of impact (tangent): If surfaces are smooth, velocity components along this direction remain unchanged.

This reduces the 2D problem to a 1D collision along the normal, plus unchanged tangential velocities.
