Conservation of Energy

Energy Dynamics for system of particles.

Energy Dynamics

Conservation of linear momentum and conservation of mechanical energy are the two principal tools for solving dynamics problems involving systems of particles.

Although both are derived from Newton's laws and are therefore applicable only in inertial frames, they differ fundamentally in how forces are treated.

  • Momentum conservation: Internal forces cancel because they occur in equal and opposite pairs. Consequently, only the net external impulse changes the total momentum of the system.
  • Energy conservation: No such cancellation occurs. Every force capable of doing work must be included because both internal and external forces can transfer energy between different forms.

Solved Example

The following example clearly illustrates the difference between the two approaches. While applying conservation of momentum, we deliberately exclude internal forces. In contrast, while applying the work-energy principle, we begin by identifying every force acting on the system. These forces are then classified as conservative or non-conservative to determine whether total mechanical energy is conserved.

By definition, gravitational, electrostatic and spring forces are conservative. Unless explicitly stated otherwise, all other forces should be treated as non-conservative.

Consider a block attached to a vertical spring fixed to the ceiling. The block is released from the unstretched position. Using conservation of mechanical energy, it can be shown that the block descends through a distance \[ \frac{2mg}{k} \] Notice that this analysis never uses the concepts of internal and external forces. Instead, every force acting on the system is considered.

Choose the system as spring + block. The forces acting are:

  • On the block: gravitational force and spring force.
  • On the spring: spring force and the force exerted by the ceiling clamp.

Since the spring is assumed massless, its weight is neglected and its kinetic energy remains zero. Applying the work-energy principle to the complete system gives

Work (on block by gravity) + Work (on block by spring force) + Work (on spring by spring force) + Work (on spring by ceiling clamp) = \(\Delta\)KE (block) + \(\Delta\)KE (spring)

Now simplify each term.

  • \(\Delta\)KE of the spring is zero because the spring is massless.
  • Work done by the ceiling clamp is zero because its point of application undergoes no displacement.
  • The work done by the two spring forces combines to give the change in elastic potential energy of the spring.
  • The work done by gravity gives the change in the gravitational potential energy of the block.

Hence,

\[ \Delta U_{\text{gravity}} + \Delta U_{\text{spring}} = \Delta K_{\text{block}} \]

Thus, the total mechanical energy of the system is conserved because no non-conservative force performs work.

Do not classify forces as internal or external while applying conservation of energy.
That classification belongs to momentum conservation.
For energy methods, begin by identifying every force acting on the system. Then classify each force as conservative or non-conservative, because every force capable of doing work contributes to the energy balance.

Work Done by Internal Forces

Unlike momentum conservation, internal forces generally cannot be ignored while applying the work-energy principle because they are capable of transferring energy between different forms within the system.

However, three important internal forces require special attention:

  • Internal normal force.
  • Internal tension.
  • Internal static friction.

For ideal constraints, the net work done by these internal forces on the system is zero. Consequently, they do not appear in the final energy equation.

Do not memorise that "internal forces do no work." That statement is incorrect.
Only the net work done by internal normal forces, internal tensions and internal static friction is zero for ideal constraints.
Internal conservative forces such as spring, electrostatic and gravitational forces generally perform non-zero work and therefore contribute through potential energy.
