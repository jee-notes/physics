Variable Mass Systems

When the mass of the system changes during the motion — rockets, conveyor belts, and rain on moving carts.

Why Newton's Second Law Needs Modification

Newton's second law in the form \(\vec{F} = m\vec{a}\) assumes that the mass \(m\) of the object is constant. For a rocket burning fuel, a conveyor belt collecting material, or a raindrop growing as it falls, the mass changes with time.

The correct form of Newton's second law — valid for all cases — is the momentum form:

\[\vec{F}_{\text{ext}} = \frac{d\vec{P}}{dt} = \frac{d(m\vec{v})}{dt}\]

When \(m\) is not constant, expanding this gives:

\[\vec{F}_{\text{ext}} = m\frac{d\vec{v}}{dt} + \vec{v}\frac{dm}{dt}\]
This expanded form is not the standard way to handle variable-mass problems. The correct method uses the concept of thrust, derived by applying momentum conservation to the complete system (object + ejected/acquired mass).

The Rocket Equation

A rocket of instantaneous mass \(m\) ejects fuel at a rate \(\frac{dm}{dt}\) (negative, since mass decreases) with an exhaust velocity \(\vec{v}_e\) relative to the rocket. Using momentum conservation for the system (rocket + exhaust), we derive the thrust force:

\[\vec{F}_{\text{thrust}} = -\vec{v}_e \frac{dm}{dt} = \vec{v}_e \left|\frac{dm}{dt}\right|\]

The equation of motion for the rocket (in the absence of gravity and other external forces) is:

\[m\frac{dv}{dt} = v_e \left|\frac{dm}{dt}\right|\]

Integrating:

\[v - v_0 = v_e \ln\frac{m_0}{m}\]

This is the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation. It gives the velocity gain in terms of the exhaust speed and the ratio of initial to final mass.

The logarithmic dependence on mass ratio means that to achieve large velocity changes, a rocket must carry a very large proportion of its initial mass as fuel. This is the fundamental limitation of chemical rockets and the reason multi-stage rockets are needed for space missions.

Rocket with Gravity

When a rocket ascends vertically against gravity, the equation of motion becomes:

\[m\frac{dv}{dt} = v_e \left|\frac{dm}{dt}\right| - mg\]

The condition for liftoff is:

\[v_e \left|\frac{dm}{dt}\right| > mg \qquad \text{(thrust exceeds weight)}\]

The velocity after time \(t\) (if fuel burn rate is constant) is:

\[v = v_e \ln\frac{m_0}{m_0 - \mu t} - gt\]

where \(\mu = \left|\frac{dm}{dt}\right|\) is the rate of fuel ejection.

Conveyor Belt Problems

A conveyor belt moves at constant speed \(v\). Material is dropped onto it at a rate \(\frac{dm}{dt}\). The force required to keep the belt moving at constant speed is:

\[F = v\frac{dm}{dt}\]

The power delivered by the motor:

\[P = Fv = v^2 \frac{dm}{dt}\]

The rate of increase of kinetic energy of the material:

\[\frac{dK}{dt} = \frac{1}{2}v^2 \frac{dm}{dt}\]

Half the input power goes into kinetic energy; the other half is lost to friction (heat) as the material accelerates to belt speed.

This 50% efficiency is a fundamental result, independent of belt speed or material flow rate. It arises because the material must be accelerated from rest to belt speed, and during that process, the relative sliding between material and belt dissipates energy.

Rain Falling on a Moving Cart

A cart of mass \(M\) moves on a frictionless surface with initial speed \(v_0\). Rain falls vertically (zero horizontal momentum) at a rate \(\frac{dm}{dt}\). Since no horizontal external force acts:

\[P_x = (M + m)v = Mv_0 = \text{const}\] \[v(t) = \frac{M v_0}{M + m(t)}\]

The cart slows down as it accumulates mass (horizontal momentum is shared over increasing mass).
