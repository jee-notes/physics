Newton's Law of Gravitation

The fundamental inverse-square law describing the mutual attraction between point masses.

Universal Law of Gravitation Formulation

In 1687, Sir Isaac Newton published his universal law of gravitation in the Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica. The law asserts that every point mass in the universe attracts every other point mass along the straight line joining them.

The magnitude of this gravitational force \( F \) is directly proportional to the product of their inertial masses \( m_1 \) and \( m_2 \), and inversely proportional to the square of the distance \( r \) between their centers:

Scalar Statement of Newton's Law

\[ F = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2} \]

Where:

  • \( F \) = Magnitude of the mutual attractive gravitational force (\(\text{N}\)).
  • \( m_1, m_2 \) = Inertial/gravitational masses of the interacting point particles (\(\text{kg}\)).
  • \( r \) = Center-to-center distance separating the two masses (\(\text{m}\)).
  • \( G \) = Universal gravitational constant.
Newton's law strictly applies to point particles. For extended spherical bodies with spherically symmetric mass distributions, Newton's Shell Theorem proves that they attract external objects as if all their mass were concentrated strictly at their geometric center.

Vector Form of Newton's Gravitational Law

Because force is a vector quantity, directional precision is critical for multi-particle problem solving. Let position vectors of particles with masses \( m_1 \) and \( m_2 \) be \( \vec{r}_1 \) and \( \vec{r}_2 \) relative to an arbitrary origin \( O \).

The displacement vector pointing from mass \( 1 \) to mass \( 2 \) is:

\[ \vec{r}_{12} = \vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1, \quad r = |\vec{r}_{12}|, \quad \hat{r}_{12} = \frac{\vec{r}_{12}}{r} \]

The gravitational force \( \vec{F}_{21} \) exerted on mass 2 by mass 1 is directed inward toward mass 1 (opposite to \( \hat{r}_{12} \)):

\[ \vec{F}_{21} = -G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2} \hat{r}_{12} = -G \frac{m_1 m_2}{|\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1|^3} (\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1) \]

Similarly, the force \( \vec{F}_{12} \) exerted on mass 1 by mass 2 is:

\[ \vec{F}_{12} = -G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2} \hat{r}_{21} = +G \frac{m_1 m_2}{|\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1|^3} (\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1) \]
Newton's Third Law Verification: \[ \vec{F}_{12} = -\vec{F}_{21} \] Gravitational forces always form an action-reaction pair: equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, act along the line connecting the particles, and act on different bodies simultaneously.

The Universal Gravitational Constant (G)

The constant \( G \) is a fundamental physical constant of nature. It was first accurately measured in the laboratory by Henry Cavendish in 1798 using a highly sensitive torsion balance (often referred to as "weighing the Earth").

Value, Units and Dimensions of \( G \)

\[ G \approx 6.67430 \times 10^{-11} \, \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2 \quad (\text{or } \text{m}^3\cdot\text{kg}^{-1}\cdot\text{s}^{-2}) \]

From \( F = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2} \), we have \( G = \frac{F r^2}{m_1 m_2} \):

\[ [G] = \frac{[F][L]^2}{[M]^2} = \frac{[M L T^{-2}][L^2]}{[M^2]} = [M^{-1} L^3 T^{-2}] \]
The numerical value of \( G \) is extremely small (\(\sim 10^{-11}\)), which explains why we do not notice gravitational attraction between ordinary everyday objects (e.g., two 1 kg masses separated by 1 m exert a mutual force of only \( \approx 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \, \text{N} \)). Noticeable gravitational forces require at least one astronomical mass (such as Earth, \( M \approx 6 \times 10^{24} \, \text{kg} \)).

Principle of Superposition for Gravitational Forces

The gravitational interaction between any pair of particles is completely unaffected by the presence of other surrounding particles. Therefore, the net gravitational force \( \vec{F}_{\text{net}} \) acting on a test mass \( m_0 \) due to a system of \( N \) discrete point masses \( m_1, m_2, \ldots, m_N \) is the vector sum of the individual forces:

\[ \vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \vec{F}_{i} = -G m_0 \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{m_i}{|\vec{r}_0 - \vec{r}_i|^3} (\vec{r}_0 - \vec{r}_i) \]

Example: Three Identical Masses at the Vertices of an Equilateral Triangle

Three equal point masses \( m \) are placed at the vertices of an equilateral triangle of side length \( a \). Find the magnitude of the net gravitational force on any one mass.

Solution:

Let the target mass be at vertex \( A \). The other two masses at vertices \( B \) and \( C \) exert forces \( \vec{F}_1 \) and \( \vec{F}_2 \) directed along \( AB \) and \( AC \) respectively.

The magnitudes of both forces are equal:

\[ F_1 = F_2 = F_0 = \frac{G m^2}{a^2} \]

The angle between \( \vec{F}_1 \) and \( \vec{F}_2 \) is \( \theta = 60^\circ \). Using the vector resultant formula:

\[ F_{\text{net}} = \sqrt{F_1^2 + F_2^2 + 2 F_1 F_2 \cos 60^\circ} = \sqrt{F_0^2 + F_0^2 + 2 F_0^2 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)} = \sqrt{3} F_0 \] \[ F_{\text{net}} = \sqrt{3} \frac{G m^2}{a^2} \]

The net force is directed along the angle bisector toward the centroid of the triangle.

Gravitational Force for Continuous Bodies & Cavity Problems

For a continuous mass distribution with volume mass density \( \rho(\vec{r}') \), the total gravitational force exerted on an external point mass \( m \) is obtained by integrating infinitesimal contributions \( dm = \rho(\vec{r}') dV' \):

\[ \vec{F} = -G m \int \frac{\rho(\vec{r}')}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}'|^3} (\vec{r} - \vec{r}') dV' \]

The Cavity Method (Superposition Technique)

When a spherical cavity is carved out of a uniform solid sphere of mass \( M \) and radius \( R \), the gravitational force on an external mass \( m \) can be evaluated without difficult integration by treating the cavity as a superposition of the original intact sphere and a negative mass sphere occupying the cavity region:

\[ \vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \vec{F}_{\text{complete sphere}} - \vec{F}_{\text{removed mass sphere}} \]

Example: Gravitational Force on a Point Mass near a Sphere with a Spherical Cavity

A solid uniform sphere of radius \( R \) and mass \( M \) has a spherical cavity of radius \( R/2 \) carved out such that the cavity's surface touches the outer surface and the center of the sphere. Find the gravitational force exerted on a point mass \( m \) placed at a distance \( d = 2R \) from the center of the original sphere along the line joining both centers (on the side of the cavity).

Solution:

1. Mass of the original complete sphere = \( M \).

2. Volume of cavity of radius \( R/2 \) is \( V_{\text{cavity}} = \frac{4}{3}\pi (R/2)^3 = \frac{1}{8} V_{\text{total}} \). Thus, the mass of the removed sphere is:

\[ M_{\text{cav}} = \frac{M}{8} \]

3. The center of the removed sphere is located at distance \( R/2 \) from the main center toward the test mass. Therefore, the distance from the center of the cavity to the test mass \( m \) is:

\[ d_{\text{cav}} = 2R - \frac{R}{2} = \frac{3R}{2} \]

4. Applying superposition of forces:

\[ F_{\text{net}} = F_{\text{full}} - F_{\text{cav}} = \frac{G M m}{(2R)^2} - \frac{G (M/8) m}{\left(\frac{3R}{2}\right)^2} \] \[ F_{\text{net}} = \frac{G M m}{4R^2} - \frac{G M m}{8 \cdot \frac{9}{4} R^2} = \frac{G M m}{R^2} \left[ \frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{18} \right] = \frac{7}{36} \frac{G M m}{R^2} \]

The net attractive force is \( \frac{7}{36} \frac{G M m}{R^2} \).
