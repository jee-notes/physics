Newton's Law of Gravitation

The fundamental inverse-square law describing the mutual attraction between point masses.

Concept

In 1687, Isaac Newton published his universal law of gravitation, a formulation that altered humanity's fundamental understanding of the cosmos itself.

The law is titled Universal because it asserts the profound truth: the exact same invisible force that pulls a falling apple toward the Earth is also responsible for the complex orbital mechanics of the Moon, the planets, and all other astronomical bodies.

This was a groundbreaking paradigm shift. Prior to Newton, the prevailing Aristotelian worldview dictated that terrestrial (earthly) and celestial (heavenly) bodies belonged to entirely different physical realms.

The celestial heaven was widely regarded as a domain of pure perfection and ideal motions, composed of entirely different substances than the heavy matter found on Earth.

Consequently, natural philosophers firmly believed that entirely different laws of nature governed these two separate domains.

Newton's law of gravitation shattered this cosmic divide. By mathematically proving that celestial bodies and terrestrial objects respond to the exact same attractive forces, he unified the heavens and the Earth.

Through this unification, Newton laid the crucial foundation for the modern concept of the universe itself — a single, cohesive physical system governed everywhere by the same fundamental and unchanging mathematical laws.

Law of Gravitation

The law of gravitation asserts that every point mass in the universe attracts every other point mass with a force directed along the straight line intersecting their positions.

In Kinematics and Mechanics, we often referred to idealized objects as "particle bodies".
In Gravitation, we refer to them as "point masses", but the underlying idealization is identical.
This law applies to anything that possesses mass and occupies space, stripping away the complexities of volume and shape to focus purely on the interaction of matter itself.

The magnitude of this gravitational force \( F \) is directly proportional to the product of their masses \( m_1 \) and \( m_2 \), and inversely proportional to the square of the separation distance \( r \) between them:

\[ F = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2} \]
  • \( F \) = Magnitude of the mutual attractive gravitational force (\(\text{N}\)).
  • \( m_1, m_2 \) = Gravitational masses of the interacting point particles (\(\text{kg}\)). (While conceptually distinct from inertial mass, the two are physically observed to be numerically equivalent).
  • \( r \) = The straight-line spatial distance separating the localized coordinates of the two point masses (\(\text{m}\)).
  • \( G \) = Universal gravitational constant.
The Center of Mass Fallacy: Newton's law strictly applies to point particles.
A common, fatal misconception is assuming that we can calculate the gravitational force between any extended bodies simply by concentrating their mass at their respective Centers of Mass (COM).
This mathematical shortcut is valid only for uniform spherical distributions (Newton's Shell Theorem).
For arbitrary extended bodies, we cannot use center-to-center distance; we must use calculus to integrate the forces based on their specific spatial mass distribution.

The Universal Gravitational Constant (G)

The constant \(G\) is a fundamental physical constant of nature that dictates the strength of the gravitational interaction across the universe.

It was first accurately measured in a laboratory setting by Henry Cavendish in 1798. Using a highly sensitive macroscopic torsion balance, he successfully measured the microscopic gravitational attraction between heavy lead spheres — an experiment popularly referred to as "weighing the Earth."

The universally accepted standard value of \(G\) is:

\[ G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2 \, \approx \frac{20}{3} \times 10^{-11} \]

Unlike a dimensionless ratio, \(G\) is a dimensional constant; its numerical value is entirely dependent on the chosen system of units.

  • Units: \(\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2\) in standard SI, or \(\text{m}^3\cdot\text{kg}^{-1}\cdot\text{s}^{-2}\).
  • Dimensions: \([G] = \frac{[F][L]^2}{[M]^2} = \frac{[M L T^{-2}][L^2]}{[M^2]} = [M^{-1} L^3 T^{-2}]\)
The extraordinarily small numerical value of \( G \) (\(\sim 10^{-11}\)) explains why we do not perceive gravitational attraction between ordinary everyday objects.
For instance, two 1 kg masses separated by 1 m exert a mutual force of only \( \approx 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \, \text{N} \).
For gravitational forces to become macroscopic and noticeable, at least one of the interacting bodies must possess an astronomical mass (such as the Earth, where \( M \approx 6 \times 10^{24} \, \text{kg} \)).

Principle of Superposition for Gravitational Forces

The gravitational interaction between any pair of particles is completely unaffected by the presence of other surrounding particles.

Therefore, the net gravitational force \( \vec{F}_{\text{net}} \) acting on a test mass \( m_0 \) due to a system of \( N \) discrete point masses \( m_1, m_2, \ldots, m_N \) is the vector sum of the individual forces:

\[ \vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \vec{F}_{0/i} \]

Here \(\vec{F}_{1/2}\) denotes the force on point mass 1 exerted by point mass 2.

Equilateral Triangle

Three equal point masses \(m\) are placed at the vertices of an equilateral triangle ABC of side length \(a\). Find the magnitude of the net gravitational force on any one mass.

Solution:

Let the target mass be at vertex \(A\). The other two masses at vertices \(B\) and \(C\) exert forces \(\vec{F}_{A/B}\) and \(\vec{F}_{A/C}\) directed along \(AB\) and \(AC\) respectively.

The magnitudes of both forces are equal:

\[ F_{A/B} = F_{A/C} = \frac{G m^2}{a^2} \qquad \text{Lets call it} F \]

The angle between \(\vec{F}_{A/B}\) and \(\vec{F}_{A/C}\) is same as angle between \(AB\) and \(AC\) (\(\theta = 60^\circ\)). Using the vector resultant formula:

\[ F_{\text{net}} = \sqrt{F_{A/B}^2 + F_{A/C}^2 + 2 F_{A/B} F_{A/C} \cos 60^\circ} = \sqrt{F^2 + F^2 + 2 F^2 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)} = \sqrt{3} F \] \[ F_{\text{net}} = \sqrt{3} \frac{G m^2}{a^2} \]

The net force is directed along the angle bisector toward the centroid of the triangle.

Vector Form of Gravitational Law

We use vector form when there are multiple particles and their position vectors are given in 3 dimensional space.

Let position vectors of particles with masses \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) be \(\vec{r}_1\) and \(\vec{r}_2\) relative to an origin \(O\) .

The displacement vector pointing from mass \(m_1\) to mass \(m_2\) is:

\[ \vec{r}_{2/1} = \vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1, \quad r = |\vec{r}_{2/1}|, \quad \hat{r}_{2/1} = \frac{\vec{r}_{2/1}}{r} \]

The gravitational force \( \vec{F}_{2/1} \) exerted on mass \(m_2\) by mass \(m_1\) is directed inward toward mass \(m_1\) (opposite to \( \hat{r}_{2/1} \)):

\[ \vec{F}_{2/1} = -G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2} \hat{r}_{2/1} = -G \frac{m_1 m_2}{|\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1|^3} (\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1) \]

Thus we see that the gravitational force between interacting masses depends on the relative positions and not on the choice of origin.

Similarly, the force \( \vec{F}_{1/2} \) exerted on mass 1 by mass 2 is:

\[ \vec{F}_{1/2} = -G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2} \hat{r}_{1/2} = -G \frac{m_1 m_2}{|\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2|^3} (\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2) \]
Gravitational Force follows Newton's Third Law \[ \vec{F}_{1/2} = -\vec{F}_{2/1} \] Gravitational forces always form an action-reaction pair: equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, act along the line connecting the particles, and act on different bodies simultaneously.

Vector Form and Superposition

The net gravitational force \( \vec{F}_{\text{net}} \) acting on a test mass \( m_0 \) due to a system of \( N \) discrete point masses \( m_1, m_2, \ldots, m_N \) is the vector sum of the individual forces:

\[ \vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \vec{F}_{i} = -G m_0 \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{m_i}{|\vec{r}_0 - \vec{r}_i|^3} (\vec{r}_0 - \vec{r}_i) \]
