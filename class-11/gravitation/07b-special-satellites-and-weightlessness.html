Special Satellites & Weightlessness

Geostationary communication orbits, polar remote sensing orbits, and the physics of zero normal reaction.

Geostationary and Geosynchronous Satellites

A geostationary satellite (also known as a geosynchronous equatorial satellite) is a satellite that appears completely stationary relative to an observer at a fixed point on the Earth's surface.

Essential Conditions for a Geostationary Orbit

  1. Orbital Period: Must equal the Earth's sidereal rotation period: \( T = 24 \, \text{hours} = 86,400 \, \text{s} \).
  2. Direction of Rotation: Must orbit from West to East, matching Earth's rotation.
  3. Orbital Plane: Must lie strictly in the equatorial plane of the Earth (inclination \( i = 0^\circ \)).
  4. Orbit Shape: Must be a concentric circular orbit.

Calculation of Geostationary Orbital Radius and Altitude

From Kepler's Third Law \( T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{r^3}{GM}} \):

\[ r = \left( \frac{G M T^2}{4\pi^2} \right)^{1/3} \]

Substituting \( G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \), \( M = 6 \times 10^{24} \, \text{kg} \), and \( T = 86,400 \, \text{s} \):

\[ r_{\text{geo}} \approx 42,400 \, \text{km} \approx 6.6 \, R \]

Altitude \( h \) above Earth's surface (\( R \approx 6400 \, \text{km} \)):

\[ h_{\text{geo}} = r - R \approx 42,400 - 6400 \approx 36,000 \, \text{km} \]

Orbital velocity in geostationary orbit:

\[ v_{\text{geo}} = \frac{2\pi r}{T} \approx 3.1 \, \text{km/s} \]
Geostationary vs Geosynchronous: Every geostationary satellite is geosynchronous, but not every geosynchronous satellite is geostationary. If an orbit has \( T = 24 \, \text{hours} \) but is tilted at an angle to the equator (inclination \( i > 0 \)), it is geosynchronous and traces an "analemma" (figure-8 pattern) in the sky rather than staying fixed at a single point.

Polar Satellites and Sun-Synchronous Orbits

A polar satellite revolves in a low-altitude orbit that passes over or near the geographic North and South poles (inclination \( i \approx 90^\circ \)).

Characteristics and Applications

  • Altitude: Typically low Earth orbits at \( h \approx 500 \text{ to } 800 \, \text{km} \).
  • Orbital Period: Very short, approximately \( T \approx 100 \, \text{minutes} \).
  • Global Coverage: As the satellite orbits North-South and the Earth rotates West-East beneath it, the satellite passes over different longitudinal strips of Earth on every successive orbit, mapping the entire globe within a few days.
  • Applications: Remote sensing, meteorological forecasting, environmental monitoring, spy/reconnaissance, and oceanography.

Physical Concept of Weightlessness in Orbit

A widespread misconception is that astronauts in an orbiting spacecraft experience "weightlessness" because "there is no gravity in space."

At the altitude of the International Space Station (\( h \approx 400 \, \text{km} \)), the acceleration due to gravity is:

\[ g_h = g \left(\frac{R}{R+h}\right)^2 = 9.8 \left(\frac{6400}{6800}\right)^2 \approx 8.7 \, \text{m/s}^2 \approx 89\% \text{ of surface gravity} \]

Earth's gravity is very strong in orbit!

True Origin of Weightlessness: Zero Normal Reaction

What a human observer perceives as "weight" is not the gravitational pull itself, but the normal contact force (\( N \)) exerted by the floor or scale supporting them.

Inside an orbiting satellite, both the satellite and the astronaut have the exact same centripetal acceleration toward Earth's center:

\[ a = g_h = \frac{GM}{r^2} \]

Writing Newton's second law for the astronaut inside the spacecraft:

\[ m g_h - N = m a = m g_h \implies N = 0 \]

Because both the spacecraft and astronaut are in a state of continuous, perpetual free fall toward Earth at the exact same rate, the contact force \( N = 0 \). This sensation of zero normal reaction is known as apparent weightlessness.
