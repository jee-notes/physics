Kepler's Laws of Planetary Motion

Empirical kinematic laws of planetary orbits and their foundational dynamical explanations from Newton's laws.

Kepler's First Law: Law of Orbits and Ellipse Geometry

Statement of Kepler's First Law (Law of Orbits)

All planets move in elliptical orbits around the Sun, with the Sun situated at one of the two foci of the ellipse.

An ellipse is defined mathematically by its semi-major axis \( a \), semi-minor axis \( b \), and eccentricity \( e \) (\( 0 \le e < 1 \)):

\[ b = a\sqrt{1 - e^2} \]
  • Focal Distance: The Sun is at distance \( c = ae \) from the center.
  • Perihelion (Closest Approach): \( r_p = a - c = a(1 - e) \)
  • Aphelion (Farthest Distance): \( r_a = a + c = a(1 + e) \)
  • Major Axis Relation: \( r_p + r_a = 2a \implies a = \frac{r_p + r_a}{2} \)

Kepler's Second Law: Law of Areas & Conservation of Angular Momentum

Statement of Kepler's Second Law (Law of Areas)

The radius vector drawn from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal intervals of time. That is, the areal velocity of the planet is strictly constant.

\[ \frac{dA}{dt} = \text{Constant} \]

Proof from Conservation of Angular Momentum

Let a planet of mass \( m \) be at position \( \vec{r} \) relative to the Sun with velocity \( \vec{v} \). In a small time \( dt \), the displacement is \( d\vec{r} = \vec{v} dt \).

The elemental area swept by the radius vector is the area of the infinitesimal triangle:

\[ d\vec{A} = \frac{1}{2} (\vec{r} \times d\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{2} (\vec{r} \times \vec{v}) dt \] \[ \frac{d\vec{A}}{dt} = \frac{1}{2} (\vec{r} \times \vec{v}) = \frac{\vec{L}}{2m} \]

Because gravitation is a strictly central force, the torque acting on the planet about the Sun is identically zero:

\[ \vec{\tau} = \vec{r} \times \vec{F}_g = \vec{r} \times \left(-\frac{GMm}{r^3}\vec{r}\right) = \vec{0} \] \[ \vec{\tau} = \frac{d\vec{L}}{dt} = \vec{0} \implies \vec{L} = \text{Constant vector} \]

Since \( \vec{L} \) and \( m \) are constant:

\[ \frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{L}{2m} = \text{Constant} \]
Kepler's Second Law is a direct mathematical consequence of the central nature of the gravitational force (torque is zero), regardless of whether the force follows an inverse-square law or any other radial force law \( F(r) \).

Kepler's Third Law: Law of Periods and Newton's Derivation

Statement of Kepler's Third Law (Law of Periods)

The square of the orbital period \( T \) of revolution of a planet is directly proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis \( a \) of its elliptical orbit:

\[ T^2 \propto a^3 \implies \frac{T^2}{a^3} = \text{Constant} \]

Derivation for a Circular Orbit of Radius \( r \)

For a circular orbit (\( a = r \)), the gravitational force supplies the necessary centripetal force:

\[ \frac{GMm}{r^2} = \frac{m v^2}{r} = m \omega^2 r = m \left(\frac{2\pi}{T}\right)^2 r \] \[ \frac{GM}{r^2} = \frac{4\pi^2 r}{T^2} \implies T^2 = \left(\frac{4\pi^2}{GM}\right) r^3 \]

For general elliptical orbits, advanced dynamics shows that replacing \( r \) with semi-major axis \( a \) preserves the exact proportionality constant:

\[ T^2 = \left(\frac{4\pi^2}{GM}\right) a^3 \]

Properties of Elliptical Orbits (Perihelion, Aphelion, Energy, Areal Velocity)

By conservation of angular momentum at perihelion (\( r_p \)) and aphelion (\( r_a \)):

\[ L = m r_p v_p = m r_a v_a \implies r_p v_p = r_a v_a \] \[ \frac{v_p}{v_a} = \frac{r_a}{r_p} = \frac{1+e}{1-e} \]

Since \( r_p < r_a \), the planet moves fastest at perihelion (\( v_p = v_{\text{max}} \)) and slowest at aphelion (\( v_a = v_{\text{min}} \)).

Total Mechanical Energy of an Elliptical Orbit

\[ E = K + U = -\frac{GMm}{2a} \]
  • Total energy depends solely on the semi-major axis \( a \), completely independent of the eccentricity \( e \)!
  • All orbits with the same major axis length \( 2a \) have identical total mechanical energy and identical orbital periods \( T \).

Example: Velocity at Perihelion and Aphelion

A planet of mass \( m \) orbits a star of mass \( M \) in an elliptical orbit of semi-major axis \( a \) and eccentricity \( e \). Express the speeds \( v_p \) and \( v_a \) in terms of \( G, M, a, e \).

Solution:

Using conservation of total mechanical energy \( E = -\frac{GMm}{2a} \) at perihelion \( r_p = a(1-e) \):

\[ \frac{1}{2}m v_p^2 - \frac{GMm}{a(1-e)} = -\frac{GMm}{2a} \] \[ v_p^2 = \frac{2GM}{a(1-e)} - \frac{GM}{a} = \frac{GM}{a} \left[ \frac{2 - (1-e)}{1-e} \right] = \frac{GM}{a} \left( \frac{1+e}{1-e} \right) \] \[ v_p = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{a} \left(\frac{1+e}{1-e}\right)} \]

Similarly, at aphelion \( r_a = a(1+e) \):

\[ v_a = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{a} \left(\frac{1-e}{1+e}\right)} \]
