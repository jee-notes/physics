Variation of \( g \) due to Rotation and Shape of Earth

Understanding centrifugal fictitious forces on a rotating planet and the geometric influence of oblateness.

Effect of Earth's Axial Rotation (Centrifugal Effect & Latitude)

The Earth rotates about its polar axis from West to East with a steady angular velocity \( \omega \):

\[ \omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = \frac{2\pi}{24 \times 3600 \, \text{s}} \approx 7.27 \times 10^{-5} \, \text{rad/s} \]

Consider a particle of mass \( m \) situated on the Earth's surface at latitude \( \lambda \) (where \( \lambda = 0^\circ \) at the equator and \( \lambda = 90^\circ \) at the poles). As the Earth rotates, the particle moves in a circular path of radius \( r = R \cos\lambda \) centered on the rotation axis.

In the rotating (non-inertial) reference frame of the Earth, the particle experiences two distinct forces:

  1. True gravitational force: \( F_g = m g \) directed radially toward the center of the Earth.
  2. Outward centrifugal pseudo-force: \( F_c = m \omega^2 r = m \omega^2 R \cos\lambda \) directed perpendicularly away from the rotation axis.

The angle between the true gravitational force vector (pointing to center) and the centrifugal force vector is \( 180^\circ - \lambda \). The effective weight \( m g' \) is the vector sum:

\[ (m g')^2 = (m g)^2 + (m \omega^2 R \cos\lambda)^2 + 2(m g)(m \omega^2 R \cos\lambda)\cos(180^\circ - \lambda) \]

Since \( \omega^2 R \approx 0.034 \, \text{m/s}^2 \ll g \), higher-order terms in \( \omega^4 \) can be neglected. Expanding to first order in \( \omega^2 \):

Effective Acceleration Due to Gravity at Latitude \( \lambda \)

\[ g' = g - \omega^2 R \cos^2\lambda \]
  • \( g \) = True acceleration due to gravity without rotation.
  • \( \omega \) = Angular speed of Earth's rotation (\(\text{rad/s}\)).
  • \( R \) = Radius of Earth (\(\approx 6.4 \times 10^6 \, \text{m}\)).
  • \( \lambda \) = Geographic latitude angle.

Effective Gravity at Equator and Poles

Applying the rotation formula to specific geographic locations:

1. At the Equator (\( \lambda = 0^\circ \), \( \cos 0^\circ = 1 \))

\[ g_{\text{equator}} = g - \omega^2 R \]

Centrifugal force is maximum and acts directly opposite to gravity. The reduction in \( g \) due to rotation is:

\[ \Delta g_{\text{rot}} = \omega^2 R \approx (7.27 \times 10^{-5})^2 \times (6.4 \times 10^6) \approx 0.0338 \, \text{m/s}^2 \]

2. At the Poles (\( \lambda = 90^\circ \), \( \cos 90^\circ = 0 \))

\[ g_{\text{pole}} = g \]

At the poles, the radius of the circular path of rotation is zero (\( r = 0 \)). Thus, there is zero centrifugal effect.

Special Case: Condition for Weightlessness at Equator

What would be the angular speed of Earth's rotation so that bodies at the equator feel completely weightless (\( g_{\text{equator}} = 0 \))? What would be the duration of a day in that scenario?

Solution:

Setting \( g' = 0 \) at the equator (\( \lambda = 0^\circ \)):

\[ g - \omega^2 R = 0 \implies \omega = \sqrt{\frac{g}{R}} \] \[ \omega = \sqrt{\frac{9.8}{6.4 \times 10^6}} \approx 1.237 \times 10^{-3} \, \text{rad/s} \]

This is approximately 17 times faster than Earth's current angular velocity (\( \omega \approx 17 \omega_0 \)).

The duration of a day (time period \( T \)) would then become:

\[ T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{R}{g}} \approx 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{6.4 \times 10^6}{9.8}} \approx 5077 \, \text{s} \approx 84.6 \, \text{minutes} \approx 1.41 \, \text{hours} \]

Effect of Non-Spherical Shape (Oblateness) of Earth

The Earth is not a perfect sphere; it is an oblate spheroid due to billions of years of centrifugal bulging during its molten formation.

  • Equatorial radius: \( R_e \approx 6378 \, \text{km} \)
  • Polar radius: \( R_p \approx 6357 \, \text{km} \)
  • Difference: \( R_e - R_p \approx 21 \, \text{km} \)

From the fundamental surface gravity relation \( g = \frac{GM}{R^2} \), since \( g \propto \frac{1}{R^2} \):

\[ R_e > R_p \implies g_{\text{equator}} < g_{\text{pole}} \]
Combined Difference Between Pole and Equator:

The total observed difference \( g_{\text{pole}} - g_{\text{equator}} \approx 0.052 \, \text{m/s}^2 \) arises from two factors:

  1. Due to rotation: \( \approx 0.034 \, \text{m/s}^2 \) (\(\approx 65\%\) of total difference).
  2. Due to shape (oblateness): \( \approx 0.018 \, \text{m/s}^2 \) (\(\approx 35\%\) of total difference).

Therefore, a body weighs more at the poles than at the equator on both counts.
