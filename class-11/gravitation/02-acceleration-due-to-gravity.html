Acceleration Due to Gravity

The acceleration experienced by a freely falling body under the influence of Earth's gravitational field.

Definition of Acceleration Due to Gravity

When a body of mass \( m \) is released near the surface of a celestial body (such as Earth), it experiences a downward gravitational force \( \vec{F}_g \). According to Newton's second law of motion (\( \vec{F} = m \vec{a} \)), this force imparts an acceleration to the body known as the acceleration due to gravity, denoted by \( \vec{g} \).

\[ \vec{g} = \frac{\vec{F}_g}{m} \]
  • Nature: Vector quantity pointing toward the center of the planet.
  • SI Unit: \(\text{m/s}^2\) (or equivalently \(\text{N/kg}\)).
  • Dimensions: \([g] = [M^0 L^1 T^{-2}]\).
  • Standard Standard Sea-Level Value (Earth): \( g_0 \approx 9.80665 \, \text{m/s}^2 \approx 9.81 \, \text{m/s}^2 \).
The acceleration due to gravity is independent of the mass, shape, size, or material composition of the falling object in vacuum. A feather and a heavy lead sphere fall with precisely the same acceleration under gravity alone, as demonstrated by Galileo's classic experiments.

Relation Between Universal Constant (G) and Surface Gravity (g)

Consider Earth as a uniform solid sphere of mass \( M \) and radius \( R \). By Newton's Shell Theorem, the gravitational force exerted on a test particle of mass \( m \) situated on the surface is:

\[ F_g = G \frac{M m}{R^2} \]

Equating this gravitational force to the body's weight \( F_g = m g \):

\[ m g = G \frac{M m}{R^2} \implies g = \frac{GM}{R^2} \]

Surface Acceleration Formula

\[ g = \frac{GM}{R^2} \]

In terms of mean mass density \( \rho \) of the planet (\( M = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \rho \)):

\[ g = \frac{G \left(\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \rho\right)}{R^2} = \frac{4}{3}\pi G \rho R \]
Key Scaling Relations:
  • If density \( \rho \) is constant: \( g \propto R \).
  • If mass \( M \) is constant: \( g \propto \frac{1}{R^2} \).

Factors Affecting Acceleration Due to Gravity

The value of \( g \) is not strictly constant across the surface or surroundings of Earth. It varies significantly due to four distinct physical mechanisms:

  1. Altitude (\( h \)): As one ascends above the surface, the distance from the Earth's center increases, causing \( g \) to decrease as \( 1/(R+h)^2 \).
  2. Depth (\( d \)): As one descends into a mine shaft, only the mass of the inner sphere of radius \( (R-d) \) contributes to gravity, causing \( g \) to decrease linearly to zero at the center.
  3. Rotation of the Earth (Latitude \( \lambda \)): Centrifugal effects in the rotating frame of reference reduce apparent gravity most at the equator and zero at the poles.
  4. Shape (Oblateness) of the Earth: Earth is an oblate spheroid flattened at the poles, making equatorial radius \( R_e \) about \( 21 \, \text{km} \) larger than polar radius \( R_p \), which makes \( g_{\text{pole}} > g_{\text{equator}} \).
