Gravitational Field and Intensity

Vector field representation of gravitational interaction per unit test mass.

Concept of Gravitational Field and Field Intensity

The concept of a "field" avoids the problematic concept of instantaneous action-at-a-distance. Instead of particle 1 directly acting across empty space on particle 2, mass \( M \) creates an alteration in the properties of the surrounding space known as a gravitational field. Any other mass \( m \) placed at a field point simply interacts locally with the field existing at that point.

The gravitational field intensity (or gravitational field strength), denoted by \( \vec{I} \) or \( \vec{E}_g \), at any point in space is defined as the gravitational force experienced per unit test mass placed at that point, in the limit where the test mass \( m_0 \to 0 \) so that it does not perturb the source distribution:

Definition of Gravitational Field Intensity

\[ \vec{I} = \lim_{m_0 \to 0} \frac{\vec{F}}{m_0} \]
  • Vector Nature: Points in the direction of the force experienced by a positive test mass (radially toward the attractive source).
  • SI Units: \(\text{N/kg}\) or \(\text{m/s}^2\).
  • Dimensions: \([I] = [M^0 L^1 T^{-2}]\).

Field Intensity Due to Point Mass

Consider an isolated point mass \( M \) located at the origin \( O \). Let a test mass \( m_0 \) be placed at a position vector \( \vec{r} \). The gravitational force on \( m_0 \) is:

\[ \vec{F} = -G \frac{M m_0}{r^2} \hat{r} = -G \frac{M m_0}{r^3} \vec{r} \]

Dividing by \( m_0 \):

Field of a Point Mass

\[ \vec{I}(\vec{r}) = -\frac{GM}{r^2} \hat{r} = -\frac{GM}{r^3} \vec{r} \]

Magnitude of field intensity at distance \( r \):

\[ I = \frac{GM}{r^2} \]
The negative sign indicates that the field vector \( \vec{I} \) is radially inward (directed toward the source mass \( M \)), opposite to the outward unit vector \( \hat{r} \).

Principle of Superposition for Gravitational Fields

Like forces, gravitational field intensities obey the principle of linear superposition. If multiple point masses \( M_1, M_2, \ldots, M_N \) are located at position vectors \( \vec{r}_1, \vec{r}_2, \ldots, \vec{r}_N \), the net gravitational field at position \( \vec{r} \) is the vector sum:

\[ \vec{I}_{\text{net}}(\vec{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \vec{I}_i(\vec{r}) = -G \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{M_i}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}_i|^3} (\vec{r} - \vec{r}_i) \]

Null Point Calculation

Two bodies of masses \( M_1 \) and \( M_2 \) are fixed at a distance \( d \) apart. Find the location of the point along the line joining them where the net gravitational field intensity is zero (the "Null Point").

Solution:

Let the null point \( P \) be at distance \( x \) from \( M_1 \), so its distance from \( M_2 \) is \( d - x \).

At the null point, the attractive fields due to \( M_1 \) and \( M_2 \) are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction:

\[ \frac{G M_1}{x^2} = \frac{G M_2}{(d - x)^2} \implies \frac{\sqrt{M_1}}{x} = \frac{\sqrt{M_2}}{d - x} \] \[ \sqrt{M_1}(d - x) = \sqrt{M_2} x \implies x(\sqrt{M_1} + \sqrt{M_2}) = \sqrt{M_1} d \] \[ x = \frac{\sqrt{M_1}}{\sqrt{M_1} + \sqrt{M_2}} d \]

Notice that the null point lies closer to the smaller mass. If \( M_1 = M_2 \), \( x = d/2 \) (the exact midpoint).

Gravitational Field Lines and Their Properties

A gravitational field line is an imaginary continuous curve drawn in space such that the tangent to the curve at any point gives the direction of the gravitational field intensity vector \( \vec{I} \) at that point.

Fundamental Properties of Gravitational Field Lines

  1. Always Inward: Field lines originate at infinity and terminate on masses (since negative gravitational charges do not exist).
  2. No Closed Loops: Field lines never form closed loops because the gravitational field is strictly conservative (\( \oint \vec{I} \cdot d\vec{r} = 0 \)).
  3. Never Intersect: Two field lines never cross each other. If they did, there would be two different directions of gravitational field at the intersection point, which is physically impossible.
  4. Field Density Represents Magnitude: The number of field lines crossing perpendicularly through a unit surface area is proportional to the magnitude of the field intensity at that region.
  5. Perpendicular to Equipotential Surfaces: Gravitational field lines are always orthogonal to equipotential surfaces at every point.
