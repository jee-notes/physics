Variation of \( g \) with Altitude and Depth

Mathematical derivations and physical analysis of the radial dependence of gravitational acceleration.

Variation of g with Altitude (Height Above Surface)

Let \( M \) and \( R \) be the mass and radius of the Earth. The acceleration due to gravity at the Earth's surface is:

\[ g = \frac{GM}{R^2} \]

At a point located at height \( h \) above the surface, the distance from the center of Earth is \( r = R + h \). The acceleration due to gravity \( g_h \) at this altitude is:

Exact Formula for Any Altitude \( h \)

\[ g_h = \frac{GM}{(R+h)^2} = \frac{GM}{R^2\left(1 + \frac{h}{R}\right)^2} = \frac{g}{\left(1 + \frac{h}{R}\right)^2} \]

As \( h \to \infty \), \( g_h \to 0 \). The decrease in gravity with height is purely governed by the inverse-square law.

Approximation for Small Altitudes (h << R)

When the height \( h \) is much smaller than the radius of the Earth (\( h \ll R \), typically \( h < 300 \, \text{km} \)), we can use the binomial expansion:

\[ g_h = g \left(1 + \frac{h}{R}\right)^{-2} = g \left(1 - \frac{2h}{R} + \frac{3h^2}{R^2} - \cdots\right) \]

Neglecting higher-order terms in \( h/R \):

Binomial Approximation (\( h \ll R \))

\[ g_h \approx g \left(1 - \frac{2h}{R}\right) \]
  • Absolute decrease in \( g \): \( \Delta g = g - g_h \approx \frac{2gh}{R} \)
  • Fractional decrease in \( g \): \( \frac{\Delta g}{g} \approx \frac{2h}{R} \)
  • Percentage decrease in \( g \): \( \frac{\Delta g}{g} \times 100\% \approx \frac{2h}{R} \times 100\% \)
Critical Exam Pitfall: Use the approximation formula \( g_h = g(1 - 2h/R) \) ONLY when \( h \le 0.05 R \) (\( h \le 320 \, \text{km} \)). If \( h = R \), the exact formula gives \( g_h = g/(1+1)^2 = g/4 \), whereas the approximation gives an impossible result \( g_h = g(1-2) = -g \).

Variation of g with Depth (Below Surface of Earth)

Consider a test mass \( m \) taken to a depth \( d \) below the Earth's surface. Its distance from the center of Earth is \( r = R - d \).

According to Newton's Shell Theorem:

  1. The outer spherical shell of thickness \( d \) exerts zero net gravitational force on the mass inside it.
  2. Only the inner solid sphere of radius \( r = R - d \) and mass \( M' \) attracts the mass toward the center.

Assuming uniform density \( \rho \), the mass of the inner sphere is:

\[ M' = \frac{4}{3}\pi (R-d)^3 \rho = M \frac{(R-d)^3}{R^3} \]

The gravitational acceleration at depth \( d \) is:

\[ g_d = \frac{GM'}{(R-d)^2} = \frac{G}{(R-d)^2} \left[ M \frac{(R-d)^3}{R^3} \right] = \frac{GM}{R^3} (R - d) \]

Acceleration at Depth \( d \)

\[ g_d = g \left(1 - \frac{d}{R}\right) = g \left(\frac{r}{R}\right) \]
  • At the surface (\( d = 0 \)): \( g_d = g \).
  • At the center of Earth (\( d = R \)): \( g_{\text{center}} = 0 \).
  • Fractional decrease in \( g \): \( \frac{\Delta g}{g} = \frac{d}{R} \).
Exact vs Approximate Linear Form: Notice that \( g_d = g(1 - d/R) \) is an exact linear relation valid for all depths from \( d=0 \) to \( d=R \) (under uniform density assumption), unlike the altitude formula which is only an approximation for small \( h \).

Comparison and Variation Graph of g with Distance r

Let \( r \) denote the radial distance of a point from the center of the Earth:

Region Distance Condition Formula for \( g(r) \) Dependence
Inside Earth \( 0 \le r \le R \) \( g(r) = \frac{GM}{R^3} r = g \frac{r}{R} \) \( g \propto r \) (Linear increase)
Surface of Earth \( r = R \) \( g_{\text{max}} = \frac{GM}{R^2} \) Maximum value
Outside Earth \( r \ge R \) \( g(r) = \frac{GM}{r^2} \) \( g \propto \frac{1}{r^2} \) (Inverse-square decay)

Example: Equal Gravity at Altitude and Depth

At what height \( h \) above the surface of Earth does the acceleration due to gravity become equal to the value at a depth \( d = 64 \, \text{km} \)? (Take radius of Earth \( R = 6400 \, \text{km} \)).

Solution:

Since \( d = 64 \, \text{km} \ll R \), \( h \) will also be very small compared to \( R \).

Equating \( g_h \) and \( g_d \):

\[ g\left(1 - \frac{2h}{R}\right) = g\left(1 - \frac{d}{R}\right) \implies \frac{2h}{R} = \frac{d}{R} \implies h = \frac{d}{2} \] \[ h = \frac{64 \, \text{km}}{2} = 32 \, \text{km} \]

The decrease in \( g \) for a small height \( h \) is twice the decrease in \( g \) for the same depth \( d \).
