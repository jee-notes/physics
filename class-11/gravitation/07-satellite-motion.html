Motion of Satellites

Orbital kinematics, velocity conditions, revolution periods, and satellite dynamics around central bodies.

Principle of Satellite Launching and Orbital Speed

A satellite is any natural or artificial body that continuously revolves around a planet in a stable gravitational orbit.

To place a satellite into a stable circular orbit at an altitude \( h \) above Earth's surface (orbital radius \( r = R + h \)), it must first be lifted above the dense atmosphere to minimize aerodynamic drag and then injected with a specific horizontal velocity known as the orbital velocity (\( v_o \)).

In a stable circular orbit, the centripetal force required for circular motion is provided entirely by the gravitational attraction of the planet:

\[ F_{\text{centripetal}} = F_{\text{gravitational}} \implies \frac{m v_o^2}{r} = \frac{GMm}{r^2} \]

Solving for the orbital speed \( v_o \):

Orbital Speed Formula

\[ v_o = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{R+h}} = R \sqrt{\frac{g}{R+h}} \]
  • Independence of Satellite Mass: Orbital velocity depends solely on the central body's mass \( M \) and the orbital radius \( r \), not on the mass \( m \) of the satellite.
  • Radial Fall-Off: Higher orbits require lower orbital speeds: \( v_o \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \).
  • Near-Earth Orbit (\( h \ll R \)): For satellites orbiting just above the atmosphere (\( r \approx R \)): \[ v_{o, \text{near}} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{R}} = \sqrt{gR} \approx \sqrt{9.8 \times 6.4 \times 10^6} \approx 7.92 \, \text{km/s} \approx 8 \, \text{km/s} \]
  • Relation to Escape Velocity: \[ v_e = \sqrt{2} \, v_o \approx 1.414 \, v_o \] If the speed of an orbiting satellite in a circular orbit increases by \( \approx 41.4\% \), it escapes the gravitational field completely along a parabolic path.

Time Period and Angular Velocity of Satellites

The orbital period (\( T \)) is the total time taken by the satellite to complete one full revolution around the planet:

\[ T = \frac{\text{Circumference}}{\text{Orbital Speed}} = \frac{2\pi r}{v_o} = \frac{2\pi r}{\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}} = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{r^3}{GM}} \]

Substituting \( r = R + h \) and \( GM = gR^2 \):

Orbital Time Period Formula

\[ T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{(R+h)^3}{GM}} = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{(R+h)^3}{gR^2}} \]

Near-Earth Satellite Period (\( h \ll R \)):

\[ T_{\text{near}} = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{R}{g}} \approx 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{6.4 \times 10^6}{9.8}} \approx 5077 \, \text{s} \approx 84.6 \, \text{minutes} \]

Angular Velocity (\( \omega \)) and Frequency (\( f \))

\[ \omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r^3}}, \quad f = \frac{1}{T} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r^3}} \]
No satellite orbiting the Earth in a closed orbit can have a period shorter than \( \approx 84.6 \, \text{minutes} \), since the smallest possible orbital radius is the Earth's radius itself (\( r = R \)).
