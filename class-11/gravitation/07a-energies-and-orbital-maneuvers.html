Orbital Energies and Maneuvers

Energy relationships of orbiting bodies, binding mechanics, and orbital transition transfers.

Kinetic, Potential, and Total Mechanical Energy of Satellite

Consider a satellite of mass \( m \) in a stable circular orbit of radius \( r \) around a planet of mass \( M \).

1. Kinetic Energy (\( K \))

Using orbital speed \( v_o = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}} \):

\[ K = \frac{1}{2}m v_o^2 = \frac{1}{2}m \left(\frac{GM}{r}\right) = +\frac{GMm}{2r} \]

2. Potential Energy (\( U \))

\[ U = -\frac{GMm}{r} \]

3. Total Mechanical Energy (\( E \))

\[ E = K + U = \frac{GMm}{2r} - \frac{GMm}{r} = -\frac{GMm}{2r} \]
Fundamental Energy Inter-Relations: \[ E = -K = \frac{U}{2}, \quad |U| = 2K = 2|E| \]
  • Total mechanical energy \( E \) is strictly negative, confirming that the satellite is in a bound closed orbit.
  • As orbital radius \( r \) increases: Potential energy \( U \) becomes less negative (increases), Kinetic energy \( K \) decreases (satellite slows down), and Total energy \( E \) becomes less negative (increases).

Binding Energy of a Satellite

The binding energy \( E_b \) of a satellite is the minimum external energy that must be supplied to liberate the satellite from its orbit and send it to infinity:

Binding Energy Formula

\[ E_b = -E = +\frac{GMm}{2r} \]

If an external energy equal to \( +\frac{GMm}{2r} \) is provided to the orbiting satellite (e.g., via rocket thrusters), its total energy becomes zero (\( E = 0 \)) and it escapes along a parabolic trajectory.

Change of Orbit and Hohmann Transfer Trajectory

To transfer a satellite from a lower circular orbit of radius \( r_1 \) to a higher circular orbit of radius \( r_2 \) (\( r_2 > r_1 \)), the minimum required work is the difference in total mechanical energies:

\[ W = E_2 - E_1 = \left(-\frac{GMm}{2r_2}\right) - \left(-\frac{GMm}{2r_1}\right) = \frac{GMm}{2}\left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2}\right) \]

The Hohmann Transfer Orbit

In practical orbital mechanics, the transition between two coplanar circular orbits is executed via an elliptical transfer orbit known as a Hohmann Transfer Orbit:

  • Semi-major axis of the elliptical transfer orbit: \( a_{\text{trans}} = \frac{r_1 + r_2}{2} \).
  • First velocity boost at periapsis (\( r_1 \)): Increases speed from circular speed \( v_{c1} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r_1}} \) to the elliptical transfer speed \( v_{t1} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r_1}\left(\frac{2r_2}{r_1+r_2}\right)} \).
  • Second velocity boost at apoapsis (\( r_2 \)): Increases speed from elliptical arrival speed \( v_{t2} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r_2}\left(\frac{2r_1}{r_1+r_2}\right)} \) to circular speed \( v_{c2} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r_2}} \).
  • Transfer time (half of the elliptical orbital period): \[ t_{\text{transfer}} = \frac{T_{\text{trans}}}{2} = \pi \sqrt{\frac{a_{\text{trans}}^3}{GM}} = \pi \sqrt{\frac{(r_1 + r_2)^3}{8GM}} \]
Atmospheric Braking Paradox: When a satellite in low Earth orbit experiences atmospheric friction, it loses mechanical energy (\( E \) becomes more negative). Paradoxically, this causes its orbital radius \( r \) to decrease, which in turn causes its kinetic energy and speed to increase (\( v \propto 1/\sqrt{r} \)) as it spirals down!
