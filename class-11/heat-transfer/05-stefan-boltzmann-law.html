Stefan-Boltzmann Law & Radiative Exchange

The fundamental fourth-power temperature law of thermal radiation and net energy exchange in enclosures.

Stefan-Boltzmann Law for a Blackbody

Formulated empirically by Josef Stefan and subsequently derived from thermodynamic principles and Maxwell's electromagnetic theory by Ludwig Boltzmann, the Stefan-Boltzmann Law governs the total radiant power emitted by an ideal blackbody.

Stefan-Boltzmann Law: The total radiant energy emitted per unit surface area per unit time (total emissive power \( E \)) by a perfect blackbody is directly proportional to the fourth power of its absolute thermodynamic temperature (\( T \)): \[ E = \sigma T^4 \]

For a blackbody of total surface area \( A \), the total radiative power emitted (rate of thermal energy loss \( P = \frac{dQ}{dt} \)) is:

\[ P = \sigma A T^4 \]

Stefan's Constant (\( \sigma \)):

  • Numerical Value: \( \sigma \approx 5.67037 \times 10^{-8} \, \text{W}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}\cdot\text{K}^{-4} \) (or \( \text{J}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}\cdot\text{K}^{-4} \))
  • Dimensional Formula: \[ [\sigma] = \frac{[P]}{[A][T^4]} = \frac{M^1 L^2 T^{-3}}{(L^2)(K^4)} = [M^1 L^0 T^{-3} K^{-4}] \]
  • Fundamental Constant Formulation: From Planck's radiation distribution: \[ \sigma = \frac{2\pi^5 k_B^4}{15 c^2 h^3} \] where \( k_B \) is Boltzmann's constant, \( c \) is the speed of light, and \( h \) is Planck's constant.

Stefan-Boltzmann Law for Real Bodies

A real physical body (non-blackbody) emits radiant power at a fraction of an ideal blackbody, scaled by its surface emissivity (\( e \)):

\[ P = e \sigma A T^4 \]

where \( e \) is the emissivity of the surface (\( 0 < e < 1 \)).

If a body has emissivity \( e \) and surface area \( A \), its total radiant power scales extremely sensitively with absolute temperature. Doubling the absolute temperature (\( T \to 2T \)) increases emitted power by a factor of \( 2^4 = 16 \)!

Net Rate of Radiation Heat Exchange with Surroundings

Consider a body of surface area \( A \), emissivity \( e \), and absolute temperature \( T \) placed inside an enclosure whose walls are maintained at absolute temperature \( T_0 \).

According to Prevost's Theory of Heat Exchanges, thermal radiation is a continuous bidirectional process:

  1. Radiative power emitted by the body: \[ P_{\text{emit}} = e \sigma A T^4 \]
  2. Radiative power incident from enclosure walls: The enclosure radiates as a blackbody cavity with intensity \( \sigma T_0^4 \). The body absorbs a fraction equal to its absorptive power \( a \): \[ P_{\text{absorb}} = a \sigma A T_0^4 \]

By Kirchhoff's Law (\( a = e \)), the net rate of heat loss \( P_{\text{net}} \) is:

\[ P_{\text{net}} = \frac{dQ_{\text{net}}}{dt} = P_{\text{emit}} - P_{\text{absorb}} = e \sigma A (T^4 - T_0^4) \]
Prevost's Dynamic Equilibrium:
  • If \( T > T_0 \): \( P_{\text{net}} > 0 \implies \) The body cools down by net radiation emission.
  • If \( T < T_0 \): \( P_{\text{net}} < 0 \implies \) The body warms up by net radiation absorption.
  • If \( T = T_0 \): \( P_{\text{net}} = 0 \implies \) The body is in dynamic radiative equilibrium with the enclosure (rates of emission and absorption are identically equal).

Rate of Cooling Due to Radiation

Let a body of mass \( m \), specific heat capacity \( c \), surface area \( A \), and emissivity \( e \) cool by radiation in an evacuated enclosure at temperature \( T_0 \).

The heat lost in time \( dt \) causes a temperature decrease \( -dT \):

\[ dQ = -m c \, dT \]

Equating to the net rate of radiative loss \( P_{\text{net}} = e \sigma A (T^4 - T_0^4) \):

\[ -m c \frac{dT}{dt} = e \sigma A (T^4 - T_0^4) \]
\[ \text{Rate of Cooling } \left(-\frac{dT}{dt}\right) = \frac{e \sigma A}{m c} (T^4 - T_0^4) \]

If the body has volume \( V \) and density \( \rho \) (\( m = \rho V \)):

\[ -\frac{dT}{dt} = \frac{e \sigma}{\rho c} \left(\frac{A}{V}\right) (T^4 - T_0^4) \]
Geometric Dependence of Cooling Rate: The rate of cooling is directly proportional to the surface-area-to-volume ratio (\( A/V \)):
  • For a sphere of radius \( R \): \( \frac{A}{V} = \frac{4\pi R^2}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3} = \frac{3}{R} \implies \left(-\frac{dT}{dt}\right) \propto \frac{1}{R} \) (Smaller spheres cool faster).
  • For a cube of side \( a \): \( \frac{A}{V} = \frac{6a^2}{a^3} = \frac{6}{a} \).
  • For a thin disc of radius \( R \) and thickness \( t \): \( \frac{A}{V} \approx \frac{2\pi R^2}{\pi R^2 t} = \frac{2}{t} \) (Cools very rapidly).

Radiation Loss in Concentric Enclosures and Radiation Shields

1. Concentric Spheres / Cylinders Radiative Exchange

For two long concentric cylinders or concentric spheres with emissivities \( e_1, e_2 \) and surface areas \( A_1, A_2 \) maintained at temperatures \( T_1 \) and \( T_2 \):

\[ P_{12} = \frac{\sigma A_1 (T_1^4 - T_2^4)}{\frac{1}{e_1} + \frac{A_1}{A_2}\left(\frac{1}{e_2} - 1\right)} \]

2. Radiation Shields

A Radiation Shield is a thin, highly reflective sheet (low emissivity \( e \)) inserted between two radiating surfaces to minimize radiative heat transfer.

If \( N \) identical thin radiation shields of emissivity \( e \) are placed in series between two large parallel blackbody surfaces: \[ P_{\text{with } N \text{ shields}} = \frac{1}{N + 1} P_{\text{without shield}} \] Inserting a single radiation shield cuts radiative heat transfer by 50% (\( N=1 \implies \frac{1}{2} \)).
