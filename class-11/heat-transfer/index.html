Heat Transfer

The fundamental mechanisms that govern the flow of thermal energy.

Conceptual Overview

To understand what this chapter is about, it helps to have some idea of Chemical Thermodynamics and Chemical Kinetics from Chemistry.

Even without that background, we can understand the idea here. We learned that heat transfers spontaneously from a body at higher temperature to a body at lower temperature.

This tells us about the eventuality of the process, but it does not tell us how slow or how fast the transfer will occur.

The Heat Transfer chapter is concerned precisely with this: how slow or how fast thermal energy is transferred.

This information is useful in many practical fields, such as construction. For example, in countries where extreme weather conditions prevail, houses may be built using materials such as wood.

This is because wood is a better thermal insulator than concrete, so it reduces the rate at which thermal energy is transferred between the inside of the house and the surroundings.

A heat-transfer mechanism cannot reverse the eventuality; it can only delay it or accelerate it.

Isn't this how humans control any eventuality?
Eventually, all will die and we fight to prolong our lives.

Thus, Thermodynamics establishes whether a thermal process is energetically permissible and dictates the equilibrium end states of a system.

However, classical thermodynamics is inherently silent regarding the rate at which equilibrium is attained.

In real physical systems, energy transfer is a dynamic, rate-dependent process driven by spatial and temporal temperature gradients.

Heat Transfer is the branch of thermal physics that investigates the rates, mechanisms, and physical laws by which thermal energy propagates across space or matter from higher temperature regions to lower temperature regions.

Whenever a non-zero temperature difference \( \Delta T \) exists between thermally connected spatial regions or bodies, spontaneous net energy transfer occurs from the region of higher temperature to the region of lower temperature.

Thermodynamics tells us about the energetics and equilibrium states of a thermal process.
Heat Transfer tells us how fast thermal energy flows (\( \frac{dQ}{dt} \)) and how the temperature distribution evolves over time.

Three Fundamental Modes of Heat Transfer

Thermal energy is transferred through space and matter by three distinct physical mechanisms:

  • Conduction: The flow of thermal energy through bulk matter without macroscopic motion of the matter as a whole. It is the dominant mode of heat transfer in solids.
  • Convection: The transport of thermal energy through the macroscopic motion of a fluid. It occurs in liquids and gases.
  • Radiation: The transport of thermal energy through electromagnetic radiation. It requires no material medium and can propagate through vacuum at the speed of light \(c\).
We know that heat transfer between two bodies depends on the temperature difference between them.
However, the mechanism by which heat is transferred also depends on other factors, such as the physical nature of the intervening medium.
We therefore study how different materials and physical conditions affect heat transfer—for example, why copper is used as a heat sink in electronic circuits, or why we prefer to wear light-colored clothes in hot weather.

Conduction and Thermal Resistance Analogy

In solid media, one-dimensional steady-state heat conduction obeys Fourier's Law:

\[ \frac{dQ}{dt}=-kA\frac{dT}{dx} \]

where \(k\) is the material's thermal conductivity, \(A\) is the cross-sectional area perpendicular to heat flow, and \(\frac{dT}{dx}\) is the temperature gradient.

For a uniform slab of thickness \(L\), with temperatures \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) maintained at its two faces:

\[ H=\frac{dQ}{dt}=\frac{kA(T_1-T_2)}{L}=\frac{\Delta T}{R_{\rm th}} \]

where

\[ \boxed{R_{\rm th}=\frac{L}{kA}} \]

This has the same mathematical form as Ohm's Law in an electrical circuit:

\[ I=\frac{\Delta V}{R_e} \]

Thus, by treating the rate of heat transfer \(H=\frac{dQ}{dt}\) as thermal current and \(R_{\rm th}\) as thermal resistance, composite slabs, cylindrical pipes, and thermal networks can be analyzed using the corresponding series and parallel resistance methods.

We can use Kirchoff's voltage law in loops as it is the thermal analogue of energy conservation and at the junctions, we can use Kirchoff's current law as it is the thermal analogue of current conservation (steady state), allowing complex thermal networks to be solved systematically.

Convection and Bulk Transport

Convection couples fluid dynamics with thermal energy transport. It is broadly categorized into:

  • Natural (Free) Convection: Fluid motion is generated spontaneously by buoyancy forces arising from temperature-induced density gradients in a gravitational field.
  • Forced Convection: Fluid motion is driven by an external mechanical agency such as a pump, blower, or fan.

Convective heat transfer from a solid surface to an adjacent fluid is modeled by Newton's Law of Cooling:

\[ \frac{dQ}{dt} = h A (T - T_0) \]

where \( h \) is the convective heat transfer coefficient.

Radiation and the Electromagnetic Nature of Thermal Energy

Thermal radiation is emitted across a continuous spectrum of wavelengths as a consequence of the thermal agitation and oscillations of charged subatomic particles within matter. An ideal radiator and absorber—termed a Blackbody—emits radiation at a maximum theoretical rate described by the Stefan-Boltzmann Law:

\[ P = \sigma A T^4 \]

where \( \sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8} \text{ W}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}\cdot\text{K}^{-4} \) is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant. Real bodies emit a fraction \( e \) (emissivity) of blackbody radiation. Kirchhoff's Law establishes that at thermal equilibrium, good emitters are necessarily good absorbers (\( e = a \)).

Because radiative power scales with the fourth power of absolute temperature (\( T^4 \)), radiation dominates over conduction and convection at high temperatures and in rarefied environments.

Key Laws Governing Heat Flow

  • Fourier's Law of Conduction: Relates heat flux directly to the negative spatial temperature gradient.
  • Stefan-Boltzmann Law: Establishes the \( T^4 \) dependence of total emissive power for thermal radiators.
  • Wien's Displacement Law: Governs the spectral shift of peak emission wavelength with temperature (\( \lambda_m T = b \)).
  • Kirchhoff's Law of Radiation: Proves equality between spectral emissivity and spectral absorptivity under thermal equilibrium.
  • Newton's Law of Cooling: Provides a linear differential approximation for rate of cooling at small temperature differences.
