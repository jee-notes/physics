Heat Transfer

The fundamental mechanisms governing the non-equilibrium flow of thermal energy.

Conceptual Overview

Thermodynamics establishes whether a thermal process is energetically permissible and dictates the equilibrium end states of a system. However, classical thermodynamics is inherently silent regarding the rate at which equilibrium is attained. In real physical systems, energy transfer is a dynamic, rate-dependent process driven by spatial and temporal temperature gradients.

Heat Transfer is the branch of thermal physics that investigates the rates, mechanisms, and physical laws by which thermal energy propagates across space or matter from higher temperature regions to lower temperature regions. Whenever a non-zero temperature difference \( \Delta T \) exists between two spatial coordinates or bodies, spontaneous energy transfer occurs in accordance with the Second Law of Thermodynamics.

Thermodynamics tells us how much heat is exchanged between initial and final equilibrium states (\( \Delta Q = \Delta U + W \)), while Heat Transfer tells us how fast that energy flows (\( \frac{dQ}{dt} \)) and what temperature distribution evolves over time.

Three Fundamental Modes of Heat Transfer

Thermal energy traverses space and matter through three distinct physical mechanisms:

  • Conduction: The transfer of thermal energy at the microscopic scale through atomic lattice vibrations (phonons) and the diffusion of free electrons without any macroscopic translational motion of the medium. Conduction is the dominant mode in solids.
  • Convection: The transport of heat mediated by the macroscopic collective movement of fluid particles. It occurs exclusively in liquids and gases and combines microscopic diffusion with bulk macroscopic fluid advection.
  • Radiation: The transmission of thermal energy via electromagnetic waves emitted by all matter having a temperature strictly greater than absolute zero (\( T > 0\text{ K} \)). Radiation requires no material intervening medium and can propagate through a complete vacuum at the speed of light \( c \).
The mechanism governing heat exchange depends on the physical nature of the intervening medium rather than solely the temperature of the participating bodies.

Conduction and Thermal Resistance Analogy

In solid media, one-dimensional steady-state heat conduction obeys Fourier's Law:

\[ \frac{dQ}{dt} = -kA \frac{dT}{dx} \]

where \( k \) is the material's thermal conductivity, \( A \) is the cross-sectional area perpendicular to heat flow, and \( \frac{dT}{dx} \) is the temperature gradient.

This law exhibits an exact mathematical isomorphism with Ohm's Law in electrical circuits (\( I = \frac{\Delta V}{R_e} \)). By defining thermal current \( H = \frac{dQ}{dt} \) and thermal resistance \( R_{th} = \frac{L}{kA} \), composite slabs, cylindrical pipes, and complex networks can be solved using standard series, parallel, and Kirchhoff-type network theorems.

Convection and Bulk Transport

Convection couples fluid dynamics with thermal energy transport. It is broadly categorized into:

  • Natural (Free) Convection: Fluid motion is generated spontaneously by buoyancy forces arising from temperature-induced density gradients in a gravitational field.
  • Forced Convection: Fluid motion is driven by an external mechanical agency such as a pump, blower, or fan.

Convective heat transfer from a solid surface to an adjacent fluid is modeled by Newton's Law of Cooling:

\[ \frac{dQ}{dt} = h A (T - T_0) \]

where \( h \) is the convective heat transfer coefficient.

Radiation and the Electromagnetic Nature of Thermal Energy

Thermal radiation is emitted across a continuous spectrum of wavelengths as a consequence of the thermal agitation and oscillations of charged subatomic particles within matter. An ideal radiator and absorber—termed a Blackbody—emits radiation at a maximum theoretical rate described by the Stefan-Boltzmann Law:

\[ P = \sigma A T^4 \]

where \( \sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8} \text{ W}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}\cdot\text{K}^{-4} \) is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant. Real bodies emit a fraction \( e \) (emissivity) of blackbody radiation. Kirchhoff's Law establishes that at thermal equilibrium, good emitters are necessarily good absorbers (\( e = a \)).

Because radiative power scales with the fourth power of absolute temperature (\( T^4 \)), radiation dominates over conduction and convection at high temperatures and in rarefied environments.

Key Laws Governing Heat Flow

  • Fourier's Law of Conduction: Relates heat flux directly to the negative spatial temperature gradient.
  • Stefan-Boltzmann Law: Establishes the \( T^4 \) dependence of total emissive power for thermal radiators.
  • Wien's Displacement Law: Governs the spectral shift of peak emission wavelength with temperature (\( \lambda_m T = b \)).
  • Kirchhoff's Law of Radiation: Proves equality between spectral emissivity and spectral absorptivity under thermal equilibrium.
  • Newton's Law of Cooling: Provides a linear differential approximation for rate of cooling at small temperature differences.
