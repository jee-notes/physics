Variable Cross-Section & Conductivity

Integral formulation of Fourier's law for non-uniform geometries and non-linear transport coefficients.

Heat Conduction Through a Truncated Cone

Consider a solid rod in the shape of a frustum of a circular cone having length \( L \), base radii \( r_1 \) at \( x = 0 \) and \( r_2 \) at \( x = L \), and thermal conductivity \( k \). Let the lateral surface be perfectly insulated, with the ends maintained at steady temperatures \( T_1 \) and \( T_2 \) (\( T_1 > T_2 \)).

The radius of the circular cross-section increases linearly along the axis:

\[ r(x) = r_1 + \left(\frac{r_2 - r_1}{L}\right)x \]

The cross-sectional area at position \( x \) is:

\[ A(x) = \pi [r(x)]^2 = \pi \left[ r_1 + \left(\frac{r_2 - r_1}{L}\right)x \right]^2 \]

In steady state, the thermal current \( H \) is constant across every section. The thermal resistance of a differential slice of thickness \( dx \) is:

\[ d R_{th} = \frac{dx}{k A(x)} = \frac{dx}{k \pi [r(x)]^2} \]

Integrating over the length of the cone from \( x = 0 \) to \( x = L \):

\[ R_{th} = \int_0^L \frac{dx}{k \pi \left[ r_1 + \left(\frac{r_2 - r_1}{L}\right)x \right]^2} \]

Let \( u = r(x) \implies du = \left(\frac{r_2 - r_1}{L}\right)dx \):

\[ R_{th} = \frac{L}{\pi k (r_2 - r_1)} \int_{r_1}^{r_2} \frac{du}{u^2} = \frac{L}{\pi k (r_2 - r_1)} \left( \frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2} \right) = \frac{L}{\pi k (r_2 - r_1)} \left(\frac{r_2 - r_1}{r_1 r_2}\right) \]
\[ R_{\text{cone}} = \frac{L}{\pi k r_1 r_2} \implies H = \frac{\pi k r_1 r_2 (T_1 - T_2)}{L} \]
Notice that the equivalent area of a truncated cone for heat conduction is the geometric mean of the two end areas: \( A_{eq} = \sqrt{A_1 A_2} = \sqrt{(\pi r_1^2)(\pi r_2^2)} = \pi r_1 r_2 \).

Variable Cross-Section Rods Integration

For any generalized one-dimensional solid with a cross-sectional area \( A(x) \) varying along its axial coordinate \( x \in [0, L] \):

\[ R_{th} = \int_0^L \frac{dx}{k A(x)} \]

The steady-state temperature distribution \( T(x) \) is determined by integrating from \( 0 \) to \( x \):

\[ T(x) = T_1 - H \int_0^x \frac{dx'}{k A(x')} = T_1 - (T_1 - T_2)\frac{\int_0^x \frac{dx'}{A(x')}}{\int_0^L \frac{dx'}{A(x')}} \]
Because \( H = -k A(x) \frac{dT}{dx} = \text{constant} \), the local temperature gradient is inversely proportional to cross-sectional area: \( |\frac{dT}{dx}| \propto \frac{1}{A(x)} \). Temperature drops most steeply where the rod is narrowest.

Temperature Dependent Thermal Conductivity

In many engineering and real physical materials, thermal conductivity is not strictly constant but varies with temperature \( T \). A widely encountered linear model is:

\[ k(T) = k_0 (1 + \alpha T) \]

where \( k_0 \) is the conductivity at reference temperature \( T = 0 \) and \( \alpha \) is the thermal temperature coefficient of conductivity.

Applying Fourier's Law across a uniform rod of length \( L \) and constant area \( A \):

\[ H = -k(T) A \frac{dT}{dx} \implies \frac{H}{A} dx = -k_0(1 + \alpha T) dT \]

Integrating from \( x = 0 \) (\( T = T_1 \)) to \( x = L \) (\( T = T_2 \)):

\[ \frac{H}{A} \int_0^L dx = -k_0 \int_{T_1}^{T_2} (1 + \alpha T) dT = k_0 \int_{T_2}^{T_1} (1 + \alpha T) dT \] \[ \frac{H L}{A} = k_0 \left[ (T_1 - T_2) + \frac{\alpha}{2}(T_1^2 - T_2^2) \right] = k_0(T_1 - T_2)\left[ 1 + \alpha \left(\frac{T_1 + T_2}{2}\right) \right] \]
\[ H = \frac{k_{\text{avg}} A(T_1 - T_2)}{L}, \qquad \text{where } k_{\text{avg}} = k\left(\frac{T_1 + T_2}{2}\right) = k_0 \left[1 + \alpha \left(\frac{T_1 + T_2}{2}\right)\right] \]
For linearly temperature-dependent conductivity, the heat current is computed exactly using standard Fourier's Law evaluated at the mean temperature \( T_{\text{mean}} = \frac{T_1 + T_2}{2} \).

Spatially Varying Thermal Conductivity

Consider a functionally graded material or composite rod where thermal conductivity varies along the length as \( k(x) = k_0(1 + \beta x) \).

The total thermal resistance is:

\[ R_{th} = \int_0^L \frac{dx}{k(x) A} = \frac{1}{A} \int_0^L \frac{dx}{k_0(1 + \beta x)} = \frac{1}{k_0 A \beta} \ln(1 + \beta L) \]

The resulting steady-state heat current is:

\[ H = \frac{k_0 \beta A (T_1 - T_2)}{\ln(1 + \beta L)} \]
