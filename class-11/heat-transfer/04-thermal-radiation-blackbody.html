Thermal Radiation

Electromagnetic origin of thermal energy emission.

Concept

Like conduction, we first need to understand what exactly is radiation qualitatively.

Radiation is also a transport phenomenon (flow, current,flux etc.), but unlike conduction, it does not require a bulk matter medium and occurs in vacuum space.

Therefore, the propagation of radiation through space is not characterized by a material transport property such as thermal conductivity.

Instead, it is described primarily by the geometry of space and the relative orientation of the source and target.

Quantities such as distance, area, angle of incidence, solid angle become important in describing radiative transport.

These are geometrical measures of the spatial arrangement between the source and the receiving surface.

All bulk matter at temperatures above absolute zero (\(T>0\text{ K}\)) continually emits electromagnetic radiation. (As per prevost theory of exchange)

This emission is called thermal radiation only because it originates from the thermal state of matter but it is the same electromagnetic waves.

Thus, the source and the target of thermal radiation are still bulk matter and energy flow is through vacuum space.

At the microscopic level, the constituent particles of matter undergo continual thermal motion. Their interactions produce time-varying charge and current distributions, which give rise to electromagnetic radiation.

Thermal radiation is therefore electromagnetic radiation that is manifested due to thermal state of bulk matter and propagates through vacuum (space itself). Some of its characteristics are:

  • Propagation Velocity: In vacuum, thermal radiation propagates at the speed of light, \(c\approx3\times10^8\text{ m/s}\).
  • Wavelength Spectrum: Thermal radiation has a continuous spectrum whose distribution depends on temperature. At ordinary temperatures it lies predominantly in the infrared region; at sufficiently high temperatures, significant visible and ultraviolet radiation may also be emitted.
  • Wave Properties: Thermal radiation exhibits the usual properties of electromagnetic waves, including reflection, refraction, interference, diffraction, and transverse polarization. It can also exert radiation pressure on surfaces.
Meaning of a Continuous Spectrum:
The frequency of the emitted radiation is related to the frequency of the microscopic oscillations producing it.
A higher-frequency oscillation requires more energy: \(E=\frac{1}{2}m\omega^2A^2\)
Therefore, higher-frequency oscillations become progressively less probable at a given temperature.
However, there is no sharp upper-frequency cut-off. The probability simply becomes progressively smaller as frequency increases.
Hence the thermal spectrum is continuous.

Incandescence is the emission of visible light by a body because of its high temperature. The glowing filament of an incandescent bulb is a familiar example of thermal radiation.

Prevost's Theory of Exchanges

According to Prevost's theory of exchanges (1791), every body continuously emits thermal radiation as long as its temperature is above absolute zero.

At the same time, a body also continuously absorbs radiation incident upon it from surrounding bodies. Thus, emission and absorption occur simultaneously.

  • Emission: A body continuously emits thermal radiation according to its thermal state.
  • Absorption: A body continuously absorbs radiation incident upon it from its surroundings.
Prevost's theory was important because, firstly, it eliminated the idea of "cold rays" analogous to "heat rays".
Prevost says that there are only heat rays that are either emitted or absorbed. "Cold rays" are not emitted separately. It is not like having two separate types of charges, positive and negative.
Prevost's theory also says that emission and absorption are two opposing processes that occur in the same body simultaneously, and both processes affect the temperature of the body in opposite ways.
When thermal equilibrium is achieved by the body, the rates of absorption and emission are equal.

To understand the implication of Prevost's theory of exchanges, consider a sealed box with an ideal vacuum inside and a highly reflecting, silvered inner surface. Inside the box, place a cup of hot coffee at \(80^\circ\mathrm C\) and some ice cubes at \(-10^\circ\mathrm C\).

The vacuum eliminates conduction and convection. The highly reflecting inner surface is assumed to have negligible absorption and emission, so the temperature of the box itself does not affect the radiative exchange.

Inside this box, a continuous crossfire of radiation (photons) begins. Both the coffee and the ice are actively firing radiation into the vacuum, and both are catching the radiation that is incident on them.

Because the coffee is much hotter, the radiation it emits is far more intense than the radiation emitted by the ice. Consequently, the ice cubes absorb more radiation than they emit (gaining thermal energy), while the hot coffee emits more radiation than it absorbs (losing thermal energy).

\[ \boxed{\text{Net radiative loss}=\text{emission}-\text{absorption}} \]

As the two bodies exchange radiation, their temperatures approach one another. When thermal equilibrium is reached, the rates of absorption and emission become equal. There is then no net radiative heat transfer, even though emission and absorption continue.

Rather than two opposing thermal poles — heat and cold — Prevost's theory describes two exactly opposing processes: absorption and emission of thermal radiation.

Stefan-Boltzmann Law

The sequencing of topics taught in radiation is often quite confusing and so we try to understand as per logical sequencing rather than historical.

Stefan-Boltzmann Law came much after Prevost theory of exchange but we study it immediately after Prevost theory as it provides a quantitative description.

Formulated empirically by Josef Stefan and subsequently derived from thermodynamic principles and Maxwell's electromagnetic theory by Ludwig Boltzmann, the Stefan-Boltzmann Law governs the total radiant power emitted by a body.

Stefan-Boltzmann Law:

The total radiant energy emitted per unit time (total radiant power \( P = \frac{dQ}{dt} \)) by a body is directly proportional to the fourth power of its absolute thermodynamic temperature (\( T \)) and to its area (\( A \)) and it also depends on the nature of the body (\( e \)):

\[ P \propto eAT^4 \]

For a body of total surface area \( A \), the total radiative power emitted (rate of thermal energy loss \( P = \frac{dQ}{dt} \)) is:

\[ \frac{dQ}{dt} = \sigma eAT^4 \]

Stefan's Constant (\( \sigma \)):

Stefan's Constant (\( \sigma \)) is a universal constant and was determined experimentally similar to how other universal constants like \(R\) , \(G\) , \(h\) , \(c\) etc were determined experimentally.

  • Numerical Value: \( \sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8} \, \text{W}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}\cdot\text{K}^{-4} \)
  • Dimensional Formula: \[ [\sigma] = \frac{[P]}{[A][T^4]} = \frac{M^1 L^2 T^{-3}}{(L^2)(K^4)} = [M^1 L^0 T^{-3} K^{-4}] \]
  • Fundamental Constant Formulation: From Planck's radiation distribution: \[ \sigma = \frac{2\pi^5 k_B^4}{15 c^2 h^3} \] where \( k_B \) is Boltzmann's constant, \( c \) is the speed of light, and \( h \) is Planck's constant.

Nature of the Body (\(e\))

In quantifying thermal radiation, the nature of the radiating surface is primarily associated with the colour of the surface, which determines how the surface interacts with radiation.

Stefan-Boltzmann law represents this qualitative nature of the surface quantitatively through \(e\), called the emissivity:

\[ \boxed{P=e\sigma AT^4} \qquad (0 \le e \le 1) \]

Thus, \(e\) represents the radiating nature of the surface. A surface with greater emissivity radiates more thermal energy than a surface with lower emissivity at the same temperature and area.

This is also why the two ideal limiting cases are given names based on their colour:

\[ \boxed{e=1\quad\rightarrow\quad\text{perfect black body}} \] \[ \boxed{e=0\quad\rightarrow\quad\text{perfect white body}} \]

A perfect black body is the ideal surface that absorbs and emits maximally, while a perfect white body is the ideal surface that reflects all incident radiation and has no absorption or emission.

Prevost's theory qualitatively states that a body at \(T>0\text{ K}\) continuously emits thermal radiation. However, Prevost's theory does not specify the rate of emission quantitatively.

Stefan-Boltzmann law provides this quantitative description and adds the factor of Nature of the Radiating Surface (\(e\)).

Therefore, in numerical problems, the radiative power emitted by the body is determined using the Stefan-Boltzmann law and the given emissivity \(e\).

For example, for an ideal perfectly reflecting surface, \[ e=0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad P=0 \] Thus, although the qualitative picture says that bodies emit thermal radiation, the quantitative idealization \(e=0\) gives zero radiative power in the Stefan-Boltzmann model.

How, the Nature of the surface is majorly determined by its colour will be studied later in detail as part of theory but that theory is not considered important for solving numerical problems.

Absorption using Stefan-Boltzmann

Stefan-Boltzmann only talks about emission of radiation power - both instantaneous quantities (\(P = \frac{dQ}{dt}\) and \(T\)) being related.

This means that initially lets say, we keep a hot cup of coffee at temperature (Body Temperature = \(T_b\)) in a room with constant temperature (Surrounding Temperature = \(T_s\)) on a wooden table.

We can fairly say that there is very less (negligible) heat transfer through conduction (or convection) as air and wood are bad conductors and most heat is lost through radiation.

Now, ultimately we know that the coffee will attain room temperature \(T_s\) . Its original temperature \(T_b\) kept falling and its rate of heat loss too kept decreasing till finally it attained room temperature.

After attaining room temperature \(T_s\) the temperature of coffee is no longer falling. This implies that it has attained thermal equilibrium.

This thermal equilibrium is dynamic and so we can use Prevost theory of exchanges and dynamic thermal equilibrium to determine the radiation absorption rate of the coffee as well.

Consider a body of surface area \( A \), emissivity \( e \), and absolute temperature \( T_b \) placed inside an enclosure whose temperature is maintained at absolute temperature \( T_s \).

According to Prevost's Theory of Heat Exchanges, thermal radiation is a continuous bidirectional process:

\[ \boxed{P_{\text{net}} = P_{\text{emission}} - P_{\text{absorption}}} \]

At thermal equilibrium,

\[ P_{\text{net}} = P_{\text{emission}} - P_{\text{absorption}} = 0 \] \[ \boxed{P_{\text{absorption}} = P_{\text{emission}}} \]

So, if we know \(P_{\text{emission}}\) as per Stefan's Law, we also know \(P_{\text{absorption}}\) due to Prevost theory of exchanges and thermal equilibrium.

The same body body of surface area \( A \), emissivity \( e \), which was at absolute temperature \( T_b \) is now at absolute temperature \( T_s \) and its temperature is no longer changing. As per Stefan's Law, \(P_{\text{emission}}\) is given by:

\[ P_{\text{emission}} = e \sigma A T_s^4 = P_{\text{absorption}} \]

Thus, Absorption is happening at a constant rate:

\[ \boxed{P_{\text{absorption}} = e \sigma A T_s^4} \]

Thus, we get an in general formula for net heat loss when a body at absolute temperature \(T_b\) is kept in a surrounding of constant temperature \(T_s\) .

\[ P_{\text{net}} = \frac{dQ_{\text{net}}}{dt} = P_{\text{emit}} - P_{\text{absorb}} = e \sigma A (T_b^4 - T_s^4) \]

The emission rate and the rate of net heat loss is changing as temperature of the body is changing.

This provides a way to combine Stefan-Boltzmann law with Prevost's theory of exchanges.
Quantitatively, we can say:
If a body has temperature \(T_b>0\text{ K}\), then it emits radiation power at the rate \[ P_{\text{emit}}=e\sigma A T_b^4 \] If the body is placed in surroundings maintained at temperature \(T_s>0\text{ K}\), then it absorbs radiation power at the rate \[ P_{\text{absorb}}=e\sigma A T_s^4 \] This is a major piece of understanding: steady-state thermal equilibrium allows us to equate the rates of emission and absorption and hence determine the absorption rate from Stefan-Boltzmann law.
The other difficult part is generally to determine the area that is emitting or absorbing radiation.
