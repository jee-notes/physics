Thermal Radiation

Electromagnetic origin of thermal energy emission.

Electromagnetic Nature of Thermal Radiation

All bulk matter at temperatures above absolute zero (\(T>0\text{ K}\)) continually emits electromagnetic radiation.

This emission is called thermal radiation because it originates from the thermal state of matter.

At the microscopic level, the constituent particles of matter undergo continual thermal motion. Their interactions produce time-varying charge and current distributions, which give rise to electromagnetic radiation.

Thermal radiation is therefore electromagnetic in nature and can propagate through empty space without requiring a material medium.

  • Propagation Velocity: In vacuum, thermal radiation propagates at the speed of light, \(c\approx 3\times10^8\text{ m/s}\).
  • Wavelength Spectrum: Thermal radiation has a continuous spectrum whose distribution depends on temperature. At ordinary temperatures it lies predominantly in the infrared region; at sufficiently high temperatures, significant visible and ultraviolet radiation may also be emitted.
  • Wave Properties: Thermal radiation exhibits the usual properties of electromagnetic waves, including reflection, refraction, interference, diffraction, and transverse polarization. It can also exert radiation pressure on surfaces.

Incandescence is the emission of visible light by a body because of its high temperature. The glowing filament of an incandescent bulb is a familiar example of thermal radiation.

Prevost's Theory of Exchanges

According to Prevost's theory of exchanges, every body continuously emits thermal radiation as long as its temperature is above absolute zero.

At the same time, a body also continuously absorbs radiation emitted by the bodies surrounding it. Thus, emission and absorption occur simultaneously.

  • Emission: A body continuously emits thermal radiation according to its thermal state.
  • Absorption: A body continuously absorbs radiation incident upon it from its surroundings.

The observed transfer of thermal energy depends on the net exchange between emission and absorption.

\[ \boxed{\text{Net radiation transfer}=\text{emission} - \text{absorption}} \]

If a body emits more radiation than it absorbs, it loses thermal energy. If it absorbs more than it emits, it gains thermal energy.

When the body and its surroundings are at the same temperature, the rates of emission and absorption become equal. There is then no net radiative heat transfer, even though emission and absorption continue.

Thermal equilibrium does not mean absence of radiation. A body at thermal equilibrium continues to emit and absorb radiation; the two processes simply balance each other.

Absorptive Power, Reflective Power, and Transmissive Power

When a total quantity of radiant energy \( Q \) impinges upon the surface of a body:

  • A fraction \( Q_a \) is absorbed by the body.
  • A fraction \( Q_r \) is reflected from the surface.
  • A fraction \( Q_t \) is transmitted through the body.

By conservation of energy:

\[ Q_a + Q_r + Q_t = Q \]

Dividing both sides by the total incident energy \( Q \):

\[ \frac{Q_a}{Q} + \frac{Q_r}{Q} + \frac{Q_t}{Q} = 1 \implies a + r + t = 1 \]

where:

  • Absorptive Power / Absorptance (\( a \)): \( a = \frac{Q_a}{Q} \). The fraction of incident radiation absorbed. (Dimensionless scalar, \( 0 \le a \le 1 \)).
  • Reflective Power / Reflectance (\( r \)): \( r = \frac{Q_r}{Q} \). The fraction of incident radiation reflected. (Dimensionless scalar, \( 0 \le r \le 1 \)).
  • Transmissive Power / Transmittance (\( t \)): \( t = \frac{Q_t}{Q} \). The fraction of incident radiation transmitted. (Dimensionless scalar, \( 0 \le t \le 1 \)).
Special Classifications of Surfaces:
  • Opaque Body (Athermanous): \( t = 0 \implies a + r = 1 \). (Most solids and liquids).
  • Transparent Body (Diathermanous): \( t = 1, a = 0, r = 0 \). (e.g., dry air, rock salt, quartz).
  • Perfect Reflector: \( r = 1, a = 0, t = 0 \). (e.g., highly polished silver mirror).
  • Perfect Blackbody: \( a = 1, r = 0, t = 0 \).

Emissive Power and Spectral Emissive Power

To quantify the rate of emission of radiant energy from a real surface, two physical definitions are introduced:

Total Emissive Power (\( E \))

Definition: Total emissive power of a body is the total radiative energy emitted per unit surface area per unit time across all wavelengths in all forward directions at absolute temperature \( T \). \[ E = \frac{dQ}{A \cdot dt} = \frac{P}{A} \]

SI Unit: \(\text{W}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}\) (or \(\text{J}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}\))  |  Dimensional Formula: \([M^1 L^0 T^{-3}]\)

Spectral Emissive Power (\( E_\lambda \))

Radiant energy is distributed across a continuum of wavelengths. The emissive power within a differential wavelength interval \( [\lambda, \lambda + d\lambda] \) is \( dE = E_\lambda d\lambda \).

Definition: Spectral emissive power is the radiant energy emitted per unit surface area per unit time per unit wavelength interval centered at wavelength \( \lambda \). \[ E_\lambda = \frac{dE}{d\lambda} = \frac{dQ}{A \cdot dt \cdot d\lambda} \]

SI Unit: \(\text{W}\cdot\text{m}^{-3}\) (or \(\text{W}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}\cdot\text{nm}^{-1}\))  |  Dimensional Formula: \([M^1 L^{-1} T^{-3}]\)

The total emissive power is the integral of spectral emissive power over all wavelengths from zero to infinity:

\[ E = \int_0^\infty E_\lambda \, d\lambda \]

Concept of an Ideal Blackbody

An Ideal Blackbody is defined as an idealized physical object that possesses the following properties:

  1. It completely absorbs all electromagnetic radiation incident upon it, regardless of frequency, wavelength, or angle of incidence (\( a = 1, r = 0, t = 0 \)).
  2. It reflects zero radiation and transmits zero radiation; hence, at room temperature, it appears completely black.
  3. At any given temperature \( T \), a blackbody emits the maximum possible thermal radiation at every wavelength compared to any other body of the same surface area and temperature. It is a perfect emitter.
  4. The radiation emitted by a blackbody depends solely on its absolute temperature \( T \) and is completely independent of the material composition, geometry, or nature of the surface.
A blackbody is not necessarily black in color when hot! When heated to high temperatures, a blackbody glows brightly because it emits maximum thermal radiation. The Sun, molten iron, and the filament of an incandescent lamp behave to a very close approximation as blackbodies.

Fery's Black Body and Cavity Radiation

Because no naturally occurring surface is a perfect blackbody (even lampblack absorbs only \( \approx 96-98\% \)), practical blackbody simulators are constructed using an isothermal enclosed cavity with a tiny aperture.

Construction of Fery's Blackbody

Fery designed a hollow, double-walled spherical copper sphere with a very small opening (pinhole) \( O \). The interior surface is coated with finely divided lampblack or platinum black, and the space between double walls is evacuated to prevent conduction loss. Directly opposite the aperture, a conical projection \( C \) is formed on the inner wall.

Physical Action

Any ray entering through the small pinhole strikes the conical projection and is reflected at an angle. It undergoes dozens of successive internal reflections off the lampblack walls. At each reflection, \( \approx 98\% \) of the energy is absorbed. After only a few reflections:

\[ \text{Fraction Remaining Unabsorbed} = (0.02)^n \approx 0 \]

The probability of the ray finding its way back out through the tiny hole is virtually zero. Thus, the aperture behaves as a 100% perfect blackbody absorber (\( a = 1 \)), regardless of the wall material!

When the entire enclosure is heated uniformly to temperature \( T \), the radiation issuing out from the aperture is pure Blackbody Cavity Radiation.
