Radial Conduction in Cylinders & Spheres

Curvilinear heat conduction, logarithmic and inverse-radial temperature distributions, and critical insulation thickness.

Radial Heat Flow Through a Cylindrical Shell

Consider a hollow cylindrical pipe of inner radius \( r_1 \), outer radius \( r_2 \), length \( L \) (where \( L \gg r_2 \) to neglect end effects), and thermal conductivity \( k \). Let the inner surface be maintained at a uniform temperature \( T_1 \) and the outer surface at \( T_2 \) (\( T_1 > T_2 \)).

Because of axial and angular symmetry, heat flows purely radially outward. Consider an infinitesimal coaxial cylindrical shell of radius \( r \) and thickness \( dr \) within the pipe wall.

  • Surface area normal to radial heat flow at radius \( r \): \( A(r) = 2\pi r L \)
  • Temperature drop across differential thickness: \( dT \)

Applying Fourier's Law across the differential shell in steady state:

\[ H = -k A(r) \frac{dT}{dr} = -k(2\pi r L)\frac{dT}{dr} \]

Separating variables and integrating from the inner boundary (\( r = r_1, T = T_1 \)) to the outer boundary (\( r = r_2, T = T_2 \)):

\[ H \int_{r_1}^{r_2} \frac{dr}{r} = -2\pi k L \int_{T_1}^{T_2} dT \] \[ H \ln\left(\frac{r_2}{r_1}\right) = 2\pi k L (T_1 - T_2) \]
\[ H = \frac{2\pi k L (T_1 - T_2)}{\ln(r_2 / r_1)} \]

Thermal Resistance of a Cylindrical Pipe

Comparing with the general definition \( H = \frac{\Delta T}{R_{th}} \), we obtain:

\[ R_{\text{cyl}} = \frac{\ln(r_2 / r_1)}{2\pi k L} \]

Temperature Profile inside the Cylindrical Wall:
Integrating from \( r_1 \) to an intermediate radius \( r \):

\[ T(r) = T_1 - (T_1 - T_2)\frac{\ln(r/r_1)}{\ln(r_2/r_1)} \]
Unlike planar slabs where the steady-state temperature profile is strictly linear, the radial temperature distribution in a cylindrical conductor is logarithmic. The temperature gradient \( |\frac{dT}{dr}| = \frac{H}{2\pi k L r} \) is steepest at the inner surface and decays inversely with radius \( r \).

Radial Heat Flow Through a Concentric Spherical Shell

Consider a hollow sphere of inner radius \( r_1 \), outer radius \( r_2 \), and thermal conductivity \( k \). Let the inner and outer spherical surfaces be maintained at steady temperatures \( T_1 \) and \( T_2 \) respectively (\( T_1 > T_2 \)).

Consider a differential concentric spherical shell of radius \( r \) and thickness \( dr \):

  • Area normal to heat flow: \( A(r) = 4\pi r^2 \)
  • Fourier's Law in differential form: \[ H = -k (4\pi r^2) \frac{dT}{dr} \]

Separating variables and integrating across the shell:

\[ H \int_{r_1}^{r_2} \frac{dr}{r^2} = -4\pi k \int_{T_1}^{T_2} dT \] \[ H \left[ -\frac{1}{r} \right]_{r_1}^{r_2} = 4\pi k (T_1 - T_2) \] \[ H \left( \frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2} \right) = 4\pi k (T_1 - T_2) \implies H\left(\frac{r_2 - r_1}{r_1 r_2}\right) = 4\pi k (T_1 - T_2) \]
\[ H = \frac{4\pi k r_1 r_2 (T_1 - T_2)}{r_2 - r_1} \]

Thermal Resistance of a Spherical Shell

The thermal resistance of a spherical shell is given by:

\[ R_{\text{sph}} = \frac{r_2 - r_1}{4\pi k r_1 r_2} = \frac{1}{4\pi k}\left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2}\right) \]

Temperature Distribution:
Integrating from \( r_1 \) to an intermediate radius \( r \):

\[ T(r) = T_1 - (T_1 - T_2)\frac{\left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r}\right)}{\left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2}\right)} \]
In a spherical conductor, the steady-state temperature profile varies hyperbolically as \( T(r) \sim \frac{1}{r} \).

Critical Thickness of Insulation

When insulation of thermal conductivity \( k \) is wrapped around a small cylindrical wire or pipe carrying heat, two competing effects occur simultaneously as insulation radius \( r \) increases:

  1. Conduction resistance increases: \( R_{\text{cond}} = \frac{\ln(r/r_1)}{2\pi k L} \) increases logarithmically.
  2. Convection resistance to surroundings decreases: Because the outer surface area \( A = 2\pi r L \) increases, the convective resistance \( R_{\text{conv}} = \frac{1}{h A} = \frac{1}{2\pi r L h} \) decreases.

The total thermal resistance is:

\[ R_{\text{total}} = \frac{\ln(r/r_1)}{2\pi k L} + \frac{1}{2\pi r L h} \]

To find the radius corresponding to minimum total resistance (maximum heat dissipation), we differentiate with respect to \( r \) and set to zero:

\[ \frac{d R_{\text{total}}}{dr} = \frac{1}{2\pi k L r} - \frac{1}{2\pi r^2 L h} = 0 \implies \frac{1}{k} = \frac{1}{r h} \]
\[ r_{\text{cr}} = \frac{k}{h} \quad (\text{For Cylinder}), \qquad r_{\text{cr}} = \frac{2k}{h} \quad (\text{For Sphere}) \]
If the outer radius of a wire is less than \( r_{\text{cr}} \), adding insulation initially increases the rate of heat loss until \( r = r_{\text{cr}} \). Only when \( r > r_{\text{cr}} \) does additional insulation reduce heat loss. For electric cables where heat dissipation is desirable, the outer radius is designed to be close to \( r_{\text{cr}} \).
