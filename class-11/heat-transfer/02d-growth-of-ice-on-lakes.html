Growth of Ice on Lakes

The classical Stefan moving-boundary problem: transient phase-change kinetics and parabolic layer growth.

Physical Setup and Stefan Problem

In sub-zero winter conditions, the atmospheric temperature above a lake drops to \( -\theta^\circ\text{C} \) (where \( \theta > 0 \)). The upper surface of the lake water freezes into ice. Once an initial ice layer forms:

  • The top surface of the ice in contact with air is at atmospheric temperature: \( T_{\text{top}} = -\theta^\circ\text{C} \).
  • The bottom surface of the ice in direct contact with liquid water remains strictly at the freezing point: \( T_{\text{bottom}} = 0^\circ\text{C} \).
  • For additional ice to form at the bottom boundary, latent heat of fusion \( L_f \) must be conducted upward through the already existing ice layer and released into the atmosphere.

Differential Equation for Ice Layer Growth

Let \( y \) be the thickness of the ice layer at time \( t \). In an infinitesimal time interval \( dt \), let the thickness increase by \( dy \).

The volume of new ice formed over surface area \( A \) is \( dV = A\, dy \).
The mass of new ice formed is:

\[ dm = \rho \, dV = \rho A \, dy \]

where \( \rho \) is the mass density of ice (\( \approx 917\text{ kg/m}^3 \)).

The latent heat of fusion released during this freezing is:

\[ dQ = dm \cdot L_f = \rho A L_f \, dy \]

where \( L_f \) is the specific latent heat of fusion of ice (\( \approx 3.36 \times 10^5\text{ J/kg} \)).

This heat \( dQ \) must be conducted upward through the existing ice layer of thickness \( y \) in time \( dt \). Applying Fourier's Law across the ice slab of thickness \( y \):

\[ H = \frac{dQ}{dt} = \frac{k A [0 - (-\theta)]}{y} = \frac{k A \theta}{y} \]

Equating both expressions for \( dQ \):

\[ \rho A L_f \, dy = \frac{k A \theta}{y} dt \]
\[ y \, dy = \left(\frac{k \theta}{\rho L_f}\right) dt \]

Time Required for Thickness Growth

Integrating this differential equation to find the time \( t \) taken for the ice thickness to grow from an initial thickness \( y_1 \) to a final thickness \( y_2 \):

\[ \int_{y_1}^{y_2} y \, dy = \frac{k \theta}{\rho L_f} \int_0^t dt \] \[ \left[ \frac{y^2}{2} \right]_{y_1}^{y_2} = \frac{k \theta}{\rho L_f} t \]
\[ t = \frac{\rho L_f}{2 k \theta} (y_2^2 - y_1^2) \]

If the layer grows from initial thickness \( y_1 = 0 \) to thickness \( y \):

\[ t = \frac{\rho L_f}{2 k \theta} y^2 \iff y = \sqrt{\frac{2 k \theta}{\rho L_f}} \cdot \sqrt{t} \]
The thickness of ice growing on a lake surface is proportional to the square root of elapsed time: \[ y \propto \sqrt{t} \]

Key Takeaways and JEE Problem Types

1. Successive Layer Growth Time Ratios

Let \( t_1, t_2, t_3 \) be the times taken to form successive ice layers of equal thickness increments \( y_0 \) (e.g., \( 0 \to y_0 \), \( y_0 \to 2y_0 \), \( 2y_0 \to 3y_0 \)):

\[ t_1 \propto (y_0^2 - 0^2) = y_0^2 \] \[ t_2 \propto ((2y_0)^2 - y_0^2) = 3y_0^2 \] \[ t_3 \propto ((3y_0)^2 - (2y_0)^2) = 5y_0^2 \]
\[ t_1 : t_2 : t_3 : \dots : t_n = 1 : 3 : 5 : \dots : (2n - 1) \]
Because already-formed ice acts as a thermal insulator (\( R_{th} \propto y \)), each successive centimeter of ice takes progressively longer to freeze.

Example: Time for Ice Layer Doubling

If it takes 2 hours for an ice layer to reach a thickness of 2 cm on a lake surface at atmospheric temperature \( -10^\circ\text{C} \), how much additional time is required for the thickness to increase to 4 cm under the same conditions?

Solution:

Time taken to grow from 0 to \( y_1 = 2\text{ cm} \):

\[ t_1 = C(2^2 - 0^2) = 4C = 2\text{ hours} \implies C = 0.5\text{ hr/cm}^2 \]

Time taken to grow from \( y_1 = 2\text{ cm} \) to \( y_2 = 4\text{ cm} \):

\[ \Delta t = C(4^2 - 2^2) = C(16 - 4) = 12C = 12(0.5) = 6\text{ hours} \]

Therefore, 6 hours of additional time is required (total time from start = 8 hours).
