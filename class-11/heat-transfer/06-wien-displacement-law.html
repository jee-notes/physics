Wien's Displacement Law

Spectral energy distributions, peak wavelength shifts, stellar thermometry, and the origin of quantum theory.

Blackbody Radiation Energy Distribution Spectrum

Lummer and Pringsheim experimentally measured the spectral distribution of energy in blackbody cavity radiation across different temperatures. The resulting curves of spectral emissive power \( E_\lambda \) plotted against wavelength \( \lambda \) reveal fundamental physical properties:

  1. At a given temperature, the radiant energy is non-uniformly distributed across wavelengths. It starts from zero at \( \lambda = 0 \), reaches a distinct maximum at a specific wavelength \( \lambda_m \), and approaches zero again as \( \lambda \to \infty \).
  2. As temperature increases, the radiant energy emitted at every wavelength increases (\( E_{\lambda}(T_3) > E_{\lambda}(T_2) \) for all \( \lambda \)).
  3. The peak wavelength \( \lambda_m \) shifts ("displaces") toward shorter wavelengths (higher frequencies) as temperature rises.
  4. Total Area under the curve: The area bounded by the curve and the \( \lambda \)-axis equals the total emissive power \( E \): \[ \text{Area} = \int_0^\infty E_\lambda \, d\lambda = \sigma T^4 \]
  5. Peak Intensity Scaling: The maximum spectral emissive power \( E_{\lambda,\max} \) at the peak wavelength scales with the fifth power of temperature:
    \[ E_{\lambda,\max} \propto T^5 \]

Wien's Displacement Law and Constant

Wilhelm Wien derived the mathematical law governing the shift of the peak emission wavelength with temperature:

Wien's Displacement Law: The wavelength \( \lambda_m \) corresponding to maximum spectral emissive power of a blackbody is inversely proportional to its absolute thermodynamic temperature \( T \): \[ \lambda_m \propto \frac{1}{T} \implies \lambda_m T = b = \text{constant} \]

Wien's Displacement Constant (\( b \)):

  • Numerical Value: \( b \approx 2.8978 \times 10^{-3} \, \text{m}\cdot\text{K} \approx 2.9 \times 10^{-3} \, \text{m}\cdot\text{K} = 0.2898 \, \text{cm}\cdot\text{K} \)
  • SI Unit: Meter-Kelvin (\(\text{m}\cdot\text{K}\))
  • Dimensional Formula: \([L^1 K^1]\)
In terms of frequency \( \nu_m \), the law states that the peak frequency is directly proportional to temperature: \( \nu_m \propto T \). (Note: \( \nu_m \neq c / \lambda_m \) because \( dE = E_\lambda d\lambda = E_\nu d\nu \), which alters the derivative maximum condition by a numerical factor).

Physical Significance of Peak Wavelength

Wien's Displacement Law explains why the perceived color of an incandescent heating object shifts progressively through the visible spectrum as its temperature increases:

  • At \( T \approx 800\text{ K} \) (\( \approx 500^\circ\text{C} \)): Peak is deep in the infrared; object glows faint dull red.
  • At \( T \approx 1500\text{ K} \): Peak approaches red-orange (\( \lambda_m \approx 1.9\ \mu\text{m} \)); object glows bright cherry red.
  • At \( T \approx 3000\text{ K} \) (Incandescent bulb): Emits significant yellow-orange light.
  • At \( T \approx 6000\text{ K} \) (Solar surface): Peak is at \( \lambda_m \approx 500\text{ nm} \) (green-yellow visible band); Sun appears brilliant yellowish-white.
  • At \( T > 10000\text{ K} \) (Hot stars): Peak shifts into the ultraviolet; star appears intense blue-white.

Temperature Determination of Celestial Bodies

By measuring the continuous emission spectrum of distant astronomical bodies and identifying the peak wavelength \( \lambda_m \), astrophysicists directly calculate surface temperatures using Wien's formula:

\[ T = \frac{b}{\lambda_m} \]

Example: Calculating the Solar Surface Temperature

The solar radiation spectrum exhibits maximum spectral emissive power at a wavelength of \( \lambda_m = 490\text{ nm} \). Calculate the effective surface temperature of the Sun. (Given: Wien's constant \( b = 2.898 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}\cdot\text{K} \)).

Solution:

\[ \lambda_m = 490\text{ nm} = 490 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m} = 4.9 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m} \] \[ T = \frac{b}{\lambda_m} = \frac{2.898 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}\cdot\text{K}}{4.9 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}} \approx 5914\text{ K} \approx 5900\text{ K} \]

Classical Failure and Planck's Quantum Hypothesis

Classical physics attempted to explain the blackbody radiation curve using equipartition of energy:

  • Wien's Distribution Law: Succeeded for short wavelengths (\( \lambda \ll \lambda_m \)) but completely failed for long wavelengths.
  • Rayleigh-Jeans Law: Treated cavity radiation as standing electromagnetic modes with average kinetic energy \( k_B T \). It predicted: \[ E_\lambda d\lambda = \frac{8\pi k_B T}{\lambda^4} d\lambda \] While accurate for long wavelengths (\( \lambda \gg \lambda_m \)), as \( \lambda \to 0 \) (ultraviolet and beyond), the predicted energy diverged to infinity (\( E_\lambda \to \infty \)). This catastrophic breakdown was famously termed the Ultraviolet Catastrophe.
Max Planck's Quantum Revolution (1900): Planck resolved the paradox by introducing the revolutionary hypothesis that atomic oscillators in cavity walls exchange energy with the radiation field only in discrete, quantized packets called quanta (photons): \[ E = n h \nu = n \frac{hc}{\lambda} \quad (n = 1, 2, 3, \dots) \] Using Bose-Einstein type quantum statistics, Planck derived his universal radiation law: \[ E_\lambda d\lambda = \frac{8\pi h c}{\lambda^5 \left(e^{\frac{hc}{\lambda k_B T}} - 1\right)} d\lambda \] Planck's law reproduces both Wien's Displacement Law (\( \frac{dE_\lambda}{d\lambda} = 0 \implies \lambda_m T = b \)) and the Stefan-Boltzmann Law (\( \int_0^\infty E_\lambda d\lambda = \sigma T^4 \)) exactly across all wavelengths!
