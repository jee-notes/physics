Variable Acceleration

Kinematics of variable acceleration.

Variable Acceleration

The standard kinematic equations of motion (\( v=u+at \), etc.) are strictly limited to cases where acceleration is constant.

In reality, acceleration often varies with time (e.g., a rocket launching), position (e.g., a mass on a spring), or velocity (e.g., air drag).

When acceleration is variable, algebraic equations fail, and we must return to the fundamental definitions of kinematics using the tools of calculus.

The Fundamental Differential Relations:

  • Velocity is the derivative of position: \( v = \frac{dx}{dt} \)
  • Acceleration is the derivative of velocity w.r.t time: \( a = \frac{dv}{dt} \)
  • Acceleration in terms of position (Chain Rule): \( a = v \frac{dv}{dx} \)

The Fundamental Integral Relations:

  • Change in position (Displacement): \( \Delta x = x_f - x_i = \int_{t_i}^{t_f} v \, dt \)
  • Change in velocity: \( \Delta v = v_f - v_i = \int_{t_i}^{t_f} a \, dt \)
  • Velocity from position-dependent acceleration: \( \int_{v_i}^{v_f} v \, dv = \int_{x_i}^{x_f} a(x) \, dx \)

Differentiation moves from \[ x(t)\rightarrow v(t)\rightarrow a(t). \] Integration performs the reverse process, \[ a(t)\rightarrow v(t)\rightarrow x(t). \] Thus, differentiation moves forward through the kinematic quantities, whereas integration reconstructs them using suitable boundary conditions.

For constant acceleration, motion can be described completely by algebraic equations.
For variable acceleration, motion is described by differential equations, which must be solved using calculus.

Applications of Differentiation

Differentiation is used when you are given an equation for position (or displacement) \( x(t) \) and need to find velocity or acceleration.

Example

The position of a particle moving along a straight line is given by \[ x=t^3-3t^2-10 \] where \(x\) is in metres and \(t\) is in seconds. Find the total distance travelled by the particle during the first \(4\) seconds \((0\le t\le4)\).

Solution

Differentiate the position function to obtain the velocity.

\[ v=\frac{dx}{dt}=3t^2-6t=3t(t-2) \]

The particle comes to rest whenever \[ v=0 \] Hence,

\[ 3t(t-2)=0 \] \[ t=0,\;2\text{ s} \]

The velocity is negative for \(0 \lt t \lt 2\) and positive for \(t>2\). Therefore, the particle reverses its direction at \(t=2\) s.

To determine the total distance travelled, the position must be evaluated at:

  • The initial instant.
  • The final instant.
  • Every intermediate instant at which \(v=0\), since the particle may reverse its direction there.
\[ x(0)=-10\,\text{m} \] \[ x(2)=2^3-3(2)^2-10=-14\,\text{m} \] \[ x(4)=4^3-3(4)^2-10=6\,\text{m} \]

The total distance travelled is obtained by adding the magnitudes of the displacements over each interval of motion.

\[ |-14-(-10)|+|6-(-14)| \] \[ =4+20 \] \[ \boxed{24\ \text{m}} \]
To calculate the total distance travelled from a position function \(x(t)\):
  1. Differentiate \(x(t)\) to obtain the velocity \(v(t)\).
  2. Find all instants within the given interval for which \(v=0\).
  3. Evaluate the position at the initial instant, the final instant, and every such intermediate instant.
  4. Add the magnitudes of the displacements over the resulting intervals.

Implicit Differentiation

In many problems, position, velocity or acceleration are not given explicitly as functions of time. Instead, they are related through an implicit equation involving two or more variables.

In such cases, the required kinematic quantities are obtained by implicitly differentiating the given relation with respect to time.

Example (JEE Mains PYQ)

The relation between time and position of a particle moving along a straight line is \[ t=\alpha x^2+\beta x \] where \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are constants. If \(v\) denotes the velocity of the particle at any instant, find its acceleration.

Solution

The given relation is not of the form \[ x=f(t) \] therefore we differentiate implicitly with respect to time.

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\left(t\right)=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\alpha x^2+\beta x\right) \] \[ 1=2\alpha x\frac{dx}{dt}+\beta\frac{dx}{dt} \] \[ 1=v(2\alpha x+\beta) \] \[ v=\frac{1}{2\alpha x+\beta} \]

Differentiate this equation once again with respect to time.

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\left[v(2\alpha x+\beta)\right]=0 \] \[ (2\alpha x+\beta)\frac{dv}{dt}+2\alpha v\frac{dx}{dt}=0 \] Since \[ \frac{dx}{dt}=v \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{dv}{dt}=a \] \[ (2\alpha x+\beta)a+2\alpha v^2=0 \] Using \[ 2\alpha x+\beta=\frac{1}{v} \] we obtain \[ \frac{a}{v}+2\alpha v^2=0 \] \[ \boxed{a=-2\alpha v^3} \]
Whenever the position is not given explicitly as \(x=f(t)\), do not try to solve for \(x\) first.
Differentiate the given relation directly with respect to time using the chain rule: \[ \frac{dx}{dt}=v,\qquad \frac{dv}{dt}=a \] This is called implicit differentiation and often leads to a much shorter solution.

Velocity as a Function of Position

Sometimes the velocity of a particle is given as a function of its position rather than time: \[ v=f(x). \] Since acceleration is defined as \[ a=\frac{dv}{dt}, \] we apply the chain rule to obtain \[ a=\frac{dv}{dx}\frac{dx}{dt}. \] Since \[ \frac{dx}{dt}=v, \] we obtain the important relation \[ \boxed{a=v\frac{dv}{dx}} \] which is used whenever velocity is expressed as a function of position.

Example

The velocity of a particle is given by \[ v=x^2+2x \] Find the acceleration when \(x=2\) m.

Solution

Differentiate the velocity with respect to position.

\[ \frac{dv}{dx}=2x+2 \]

At \[ x=2 \]

\[ v=2^2+2(2)=8\,\text{m/s} \] \[ \frac{dv}{dx}=6 \] Hence, \[ a=v\frac{dv}{dx} \] \[ a=8\times6 \] \[ \boxed{a=48\ \text{m/s}^2} \]

Pseudo Variable Acceleration

Some problems appear to involve variable acceleration because the velocity is given as a complicated function of position. However, after evaluating the acceleration using \[ a=v\frac{dv}{dx} \] the acceleration turns out to be constant. Such problems are called pseudo variable acceleration problems.

Typical examples are

\[ v=a\sqrt{b+cx} \] \[ v=c\sqrt{x} \]

For these, \[ a=v\frac{dv}{dx} \] is a constant. Hence, the motion is actually uniformly accelerated motion in disguise. These are simply alternative forms of the equation \[ v^2=u^2+2ax \]

Do not classify a problem by the appearance of the velocity function.
Always evaluate \[ a=v\frac{dv}{dx} \] or \[ a=\frac{dv}{dt} \] If the resulting acceleration is constant, the motion is uniformly accelerated, regardless of how complicated the velocity function appears.

In contrast, functions such as

\[ v=a+b\sqrt{x} \]

lead to an acceleration that depends on position. Such problems represent genuine variable acceleration.
