Calculus in Kinematics

Handling motion with variable acceleration.

Variable Acceleration

The standard kinematic equations (\( v=u+at \), etc.) are strictly limited to cases where acceleration is constant. In reality, acceleration often varies with time (e.g., a rocket launching), position (e.g., a mass on a spring), or velocity (e.g., air drag).

When acceleration is variable, algebraic equations fail, and we must return to the fundamental definitions of kinematics using the tools of calculus.

The Fundamental Differential Relations:
  • Velocity is the derivative of position: \[ v = \frac{dx}{dt} \]
  • Acceleration is the derivative of velocity w.r.t time: \[ a = \frac{dv}{dt} \]
  • Acceleration in terms of position (Chain Rule): \[ a = v \frac{dv}{dx} \]

Applications of Differentiation

Differentiation is used when you are given an equation for position \( x(t) \) and need to find velocity or acceleration.

Example: Finding v and a from x(t)

Suppose the position of a particle is given by \( x(t) = t^3 - 6t^2 + 9t \) meters.

1. Find velocity \( v(t) \): \[ v(t) = \frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}(t^3 - 6t^2 + 9t) = 3t^2 - 12t + 9 \, \text{m/s} \]

2. Find acceleration \( a(t) \): \[ a(t) = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}(3t^2 - 12t + 9) = 6t - 12 \, \text{m/s}^2 \]

Notice that acceleration \( a(t) \) depends on time, so it is variable acceleration. The standard kinematic equations cannot be applied here.

3. When is the particle at rest? Set \( v(t) = 0 \): \[ 3t^2 - 12t + 9 = 0 \implies t^2 - 4t + 3 = 0 \implies (t-1)(t-3) = 0 \] The particle is at rest at \( t = 1 \, \text{s} \) and \( t = 3 \, \text{s} \).

Applications of Integration

Integration is used when you are given acceleration or velocity and need to step "backward" to find velocity or position.

The Fundamental Integral Relations:
  • Change in position (Displacement): \[ \Delta x = x_f - x_i = \int_{t_i}^{t_f} v \, dt \]
  • Change in velocity: \[ \Delta v = v_f - v_i = \int_{t_i}^{t_f} a \, dt \]
  • Velocity from position-dependent acceleration: \[ \int_{u}^{v} v \, dv = \int_{x_i}^{x_f} a(x) \, dx \]
Important: Integration introduces a constant of integration. You must use boundary conditions (e.g., "starts from rest at the origin") to evaluate this constant, or use definite integrals with precise limits.

Example: Finding velocity from time-dependent acceleration

A particle starts from rest at \( t=0 \). Its acceleration is given by \( a(t) = 4t \, \text{m/s}^2 \). Find its velocity at \( t=2 \) seconds.

Since \( a = \frac{dv}{dt} \), we have \( dv = a \, dt \). Integrating both sides: \[ \int_0^v dv = \int_0^2 4t \, dt \] \[ [v]_0^v = 4 \left[ \frac{t^2}{2} \right]_0^2 \] \[ v - 0 = 2(2^2 - 0^2) = 8 \, \text{m/s} \]
