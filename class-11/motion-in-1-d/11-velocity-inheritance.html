Velocity Inheritance

Velocity is inherited. Acceleration is not.

What is Velocity Inheritance?

Many problems involve an object being released from a moving body such as a balloon, rocket, lift or train. This process is called detachment (or release).

During detachment, the contact force between the object and the moving body disappears. Since no impulsive force acts during the release, the object's velocity cannot change instantaneously.

Therefore, at the instant of release, the object simply inherits the velocity of the body from which it is released.

Velocity Inheritance

If an object is released from a moving body, then at the instant of release,

\[ \boxed{\vec v_{\text{object}}=\vec v_{\text{body}}} \]

Consequently, the initial relative velocity of the object with respect to the releasing body is

\[ \boxed{\vec v_{\text{relative}}=0} \]
A body is said to be dropped when no additional velocity is imparted during release. Thus, "dropped" means that the initial relative velocity between the object and the releasing body is zero. It does not necessarily mean that the initial velocity with respect to the ground is zero.

Acceleration is not Inherited

While velocity is inherited, acceleration is not. After detachment, the acceleration of the object is determined only by the external forces acting on it.

The releasing body can no longer exert normal reaction, tension or friction on the object. Hence, the object does not inherit the acceleration of the releasing body.

For example, if the object is released under gravity,

\[ \boxed{\vec a=\vec g} \]

If the releasing body has acceleration \(\vec a_{\text{body}}\), then the acceleration of the object relative to the body is

\[ \boxed{\vec a_{\text{relative}}=\vec g-\vec a_{\text{body}}} \]
Velocity is inherited. Acceleration is not. This single principle explains the motion of objects released from moving balloons, rockets, lifts, trains and other moving bodies.

Misconception Pitfalls

  • The "Dropped implies \(u=0\)" Trap: Students routinely set the initial velocity of a dropped object to zero regardless of context. "Dropped" only guarantees that the initial relative velocity to the releasing entity is zero (\(\vec{v}_{\text{rel}} = 0\)). If the platform is moving relative to the ground, the ground observer sees a non-zero initial velocity.
  • The Acceleration Carryover Illusion: It is a common error to think that if an elevator is accelerating upwards at \(2\text{ m/s}^2\), a coin dropped inside it immediately accelerates downwards with \((9.8 - 2)\text{ m/s}^2\) relative to the ground. Once detached, the elevator's physical engine can no longer communicate forces to the coin; its acceleration relative to the ground is immediately and strictly \(\vec{g}\) downward.

Solved Examples

The principle of velocity inheritance is illustrated through the following applications:

  • Body dropped from a moving balloon: The released body inherits the velocity of the balloon at the instant of release, but does not inherit its acceleration.
  • Multi-stage motion: A journey consisting of successive stages with different constant accelerations. The final velocity of one stage becomes the initial velocity of the next stage. This principle of velocity chaining often simplifies the solution of multi-stage motion problems.

Example 1

A balloon starts rising vertically from the top of a building of height\(15\,\text{m}\) with a constant acceleration \(2\,\text{m/s}^2\) . The balloon starts from rest at \(t=0\). After \(5\,\text{s}\), a stone is dropped from the balloon. Find the height of the balloon above the ground at the instant the stone reaches the ground. Take \(g=10\,\text{m/s}^2\) and neglect air resistance.

Solution

At the instant of release, the balloon has been moving for \(5\,\text{s}\) . Let the velocity of balloon at \(t=5\) be \(v_B\) and displacement of the balloon (or height gained by balloon from ground) in \(t=5\) be \(h_B\) . We are taking upwards direction positive.

\[ v_B=0+(2)(5)=10\,\text{m/s} \] \[ h_B=15+\frac12(2)(5)^2=40\,\text{m} \]

By the principle of velocity inheritance, as the stone is released from a moving balloon, its velocity at the instant of release is same as the velocity as the balloon.

\[ u_{\text{stone}}=10\,\text{m/s upward} \] \[ a_{\text{stone}}=-10\,\text{m/s}^2 \]

The stone reaches the ground after time \(T\).

\[ s=ut+\frac12at^2 \] \[ -40=10T-5T^2 \] \[ T^2-2T-8=0 \] \[ T=4\,\text{s} \]

During these \(4\,\text{s}\), the balloon continues to move upward with the same acceleration.

\[ s=ut+\frac12at^2 \] \[ s=(10)(4)+\frac12(2)(4)^2 \] \[ s=56\,\text{m} \]

Therefore, the height of the balloon above the ground is

\[ 40+56=96\,\text{m} \] \[ \boxed{96\,\text{m}} \]
Although the stone is dropped, its initial velocity is \[ u_{\text{stone}}=u_{\text{balloon}}=10\,\text{m/s}. \] This is the principle of velocity inheritance.
After release, however, the stone no longer remains in contact with the balloon.
Hence, the balloon can no longer communicate contact forces to the stone.
The stone therefore accelerates only under gravity, while the balloon continues to accelerate upward at \(2\,\text{m/s}^2\).

Example 2

A rocket is fired vertically upwards with an initial velocity of \(40\,\text{m/s}\) from the ground level. It is accelerated at \(2\,\text{m/s}^2\) until it reaches an altitude of \(1000\,\text{m}\). At that point, the engines shut off and the rocket goes into free fall. If the velocity just before it collides with the ground is \(40\alpha\,\text{m/s}\), find the value of \(\alpha\).Take \[ g=10\,\text{m/s}^2 \] and neglect air resistance.

Solution

The motion consists of two parts:

  • Powered motion: Ground to engine cut-off.
  • Free fall: Engine cut-off to ground.

Let the velocity of the rocket at the instant the engines shut off be \(v_c\). The exact value of \(v_c\) is not required.

During the powered phase,

\[ v_c^2=u^2+2as \] \[ v_c^2=40^2+2(2)(1000) \]

Immediately after engine cut-off, the rocket enters free fall. Its velocity is inherited from the powered phase. Using the velocity-displacement equation for the free-fall phase,

\[ v^2=v_c^2+2(-g)(-1000) \]

Substituting the expression for \(v_c^2\),

\[ v^2=40^2+2(2)(1000)+2(10)(1000) \] \[ v^2=1600+4000+20000=25600 \] \[ v=160\,\text{m/s} \] \[ 40\alpha=160 \] \[ \boxed{\alpha=4} \]
Notice that the intermediate velocity at engine cut-off was never calculated.
Only its state inheritance was used. The final velocity is obtained by carrying forward the quantity \(v_c^2\), demonstrating that intermediate states need not always be evaluated explicitly.
Successive stages of motion can be chained together without calculating the intermediate velocity explicitly.
If the motion is divided into segments having constant accelerations \(a_1,a_2,\ldots\), then the velocity equation gives \[ v=u+a_1t_1+a_2t_2+\cdots \] Similarly, using the velocity-displacement equation, \[ v^2=u^2+2a_1s_1+2a_2s_2+\cdots \] Thus, each stage simply contributes an additive term \(a_it_i\) or \(2a_is_i\) to the final state.
This avoids the need to calculate intermediate velocities separately.
The final velocity can often be obtained directly by accumulating the contributions from each stage of motion. Example 1 is also a multi-stage journey. The stone first moves together with the balloon and then moves independently after detachment. Thus, the balloon example is also the sample illustration of velocity chaining.
