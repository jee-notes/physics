Distance and Displacement

The physical change that manifest as motion.

Dual Description of Change in Position

In the previous section, we established that motion is fundamentally a change in position with respect to time.

Suppose a particle moves from an initial position to a final position. The physical change in position can now be described mathematically in two distinct ways.

  1. By measuring the actual length of the trajectory followed by the particle during motion.
  2. By measuring the geometric straight-line separation between the initial and final positions, completely independent of the actual trajectory taken.

These two different methods of describing positional change give rise to two different physical quantities: Distance and Displacement.

Although these quantities are often introduced together, they represent fundamentally different mathematical ideas.

Distance measures the total amount of motion that has occurred physically along the path.

Displacement measures only the net positional change between the starting and ending points.

Even though this chapter focuses strictly on one-dimensional motion, these definitions are universal and remain valid in two-dimensional and three-dimensional mechanics as well.

Distance is a path function.
It depends entirely on the history of the motion and the exact trajectory followed.

Displacement is a state function.
It depends only on the initial and final coordinates, completely ignoring the intermediate path taken.

Distance (Path Length)

Distance is defined as the total length of the actual path traversed by a moving particle during a given interval of time.

It measures how much ground the particle has physically covered during motion.

Since distance only concerns the total path length, it is completely independent of direction.

For example, if a particle moves 5 m to the right and then 5 m back to the left, the total distance covered is still:

\[ 5 + 5 = 10\text{ m} \]

This is because distance simply accumulates the total path length traversed during motion.

  • Nature: Distance is a scalar quantity because it has only magnitude and no directional information.

  • Sign: For a moving particle, distance is always positive. It can never become negative.
    If the particle has actually moved during the interval, distance also cannot be zero.

  • Time Dependence: Distance never decreases with time for a moving particle.
    It may increase continuously during motion or remain constant when the particle is at rest, but it can never decrease because already traversed path length cannot be "undone".

Displacement (Change in Position)

Displacement is defined as the shortest straight-line distance between the initial and final positions, directed from the initial point toward the final point.

Unlike distance, displacement does not care about the actual path followed during motion.

It only compares the initial and final states of the particle.

Mathematically, displacement is the change in the position vector.

In strictly one-dimensional motion along the \( x \)-axis, displacement reduces to the algebraic difference between the final and initial coordinates:

\[ \Delta x = x_f - x_i \]

Since displacement depends on orientation, sign becomes extremely important in one-dimensional motion.

  • Nature: Displacement is a vector quantity.
    In one dimension, the entire directional nature of the vector is represented using only a positive (+) or negative (-) sign.

  • Sign: Displacement can be positive, negative or zero depending on the relative positions of the initial and final points.
    A zero displacement does not imply absence of motion (state of rest). It could be due to the particle eventually returning to its initial position: \[ x_f = x_i \]

  • Time Dependence: Unlike distance, displacement can increase, decrease or even become zero with time depending on the direction of motion relative to the chosen origin and positive direction.

Relation Between Distance and Displacement

A fundamental mathematical relation exists between distance and displacement. This relation can be deduced from the geometric definitions of the two quantities.

\[ \text{Distance} \ge |\text{Displacement}| \]

This inequality is universally true in one-dimensional, two-dimensional and three-dimensional motion.

The reason is simple:

The actual path length between two points can never be smaller than the shortest straight-line separation between them.

Only in the special case of unidirectional motion does:

\[ \text{Distance} = |\text{Displacement}| \]

Unidirectional motion means that the particle moves continuously in a single line without reversing its direction of motion at any instant.

The 1D Constraint and Turning Point

A frequent source of conceptual confusion in one-dimensional kinematics is the relationship between total distance covered and the magnitude of displacement.

In one-dimensional motion, a particle cannot reverse its direction of motion without its velocity becoming zero at an intermediate instant.

This is a special geometrical constraint of one-dimensional motion.

In higher dimensions, however, a particle can continuously change direction without necessarily stopping.

For example, a car moving on a two-dimensional road can take a smooth U-turn while still remaining in motion throughout the turn.

In contrast, a train moving on a straight railway track cannot instantly reverse its direction without first coming to rest momentarily.

Thus, in one-dimensional motion, reversal of direction necessarily introduces a turning point where:

\[ v = 0 \]

This idea becomes extremely important while solving kinematics problems involving distance travelled.

The Turnaround Trap:
In kinematics equations, \[ \Delta x = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2 \] gives the displacement, never the total distance travelled.
Whenever acceleration and initial velocity are anti-parallel (opposite signs in 1D motion), the particle may eventually stop and reverse direction.
To correctly calculate total distance in such cases, we must:
  • first find the instant where \( v = 0 \),
  • split the motion into separate intervals before and after reversal,
  • and then add the absolute magnitudes of displacement in each interval.
