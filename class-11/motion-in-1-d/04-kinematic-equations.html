Equations for Uniformly Accelerated Motion

When an object moves with a constant (uniform) acceleration, its velocity changes at a constant rate. In such cases, the complex calculus of motion simplifies into three widely used algebraic equations.

The Three Equations of Motion
Let an object have an initial velocity \( u \) at time \( t = 0 \) and move with a constant acceleration \( a \). Let \( v \) be its final velocity at time \( t \), and let \( s \) be its displacement during this time interval.
  1. Velocity-Time Relation: \[ v = u + at \]
  2. Position-Time Relation: \[ s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2 \]
  3. Position-Velocity Relation: \[ v^2 = u^2 + 2as \]
Crucial Warning: These equations are strictly valid only if the acceleration \( a \) is constant throughout the time interval \( t \). If acceleration is varying with time or position, you must use calculus to solve the problem.

A fourth, often useful equation for displacement in uniformly accelerated motion is the average velocity formula: \[ s = \left( \frac{u + v}{2} \right) t \]

Also, the displacement in the \( n^{\text{th}} \) second of motion is given by: \[ S_n = u + \frac{a}{2}(2n - 1) \]

Derivation using Calculus

Let's derive these fundamental equations using calculus. Assume acceleration \( a = \text{constant} \).

1. Deriving \( v = u + at \)

We know that acceleration is the rate of change of velocity: \( a = \frac{dv}{dt} \). \[ dv = a \, dt \] Integrating both sides, with limits from \( t = 0 \) (where velocity is \( u \)) to \( t \) (where velocity is \( v \)): \[ \int_u^v dv = \int_0^t a \, dt \] Since \( a \) is constant, it comes out of the integral: \[ [v]_u^v = a [t]_0^t \implies v - u = at \implies v = u + at \]

2. Deriving \( s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2 \)

We know velocity is the rate of change of position: \( v = \frac{ds}{dt} \). Substituting the first equation \( v = u + at \): \[ \frac{ds}{dt} = u + at \implies ds = (u + at) \, dt \] Integrating from \( t = 0 \) (where displacement is 0) to \( t \) (where displacement is \( s \)): \[ \int_0^s ds = \int_0^t (u + at) \, dt \] \[ [s]_0^s = [ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2]_0^t \implies s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2 \]

3. Deriving \( v^2 = u^2 + 2as \)

We use the chain rule form of acceleration: \( a = v \frac{dv}{ds} \). \[ a \, ds = v \, dv \] Integrating from initial state (\( s = 0, \text{velocity} = u \)) to final state (\( s, \text{velocity} = v \)): \[ \int_0^s a \, ds = \int_u^v v \, dv \] \[ a [s]_0^s = \left[ \frac{v^2}{2} \right]_u^v \implies as = \frac{v^2 - u^2}{2} \implies v^2 = u^2 + 2as \]

Galileo's Law of Odd Numbers

Galileo made a profound observation about objects moving with constant acceleration starting from rest.

Galileo's Law of Odd Numbers:
The distances traversed, during equal intervals of time, by a body falling from rest, stand to one another in the same ratio as the odd numbers beginning with unity: \[ 1 : 3 : 5 : 7 : 9 \dots \]

Proof: Since initial velocity \( u = 0 \), the displacement in any time \( t \) is \( s = \frac{1}{2}at^2 \). Let the equal time interval be \( t_0 \).

  • Distance covered in \( 0 \) to \( t_0 \): \( s_1 = \frac{1}{2}a(t_0)^2 \)
  • Distance covered in \( 0 \) to \( 2t_0 \): \( S_2 = \frac{1}{2}a(2t_0)^2 = 4 \times \frac{1}{2}at_0^2 = 4s_1 \). Therefore, distance in the 2nd interval is \( S_2 - s_1 = 3s_1 \).
  • Distance covered in \( 0 \) to \( 3t_0 \): \( S_3 = \frac{1}{2}a(3t_0)^2 = 9 \times \frac{1}{2}at_0^2 = 9s_1 \). Distance in the 3rd interval is \( S_3 - S_2 = 5s_1 \).
Hence, the ratio of distances covered in successive equal time intervals is \( s_1 : 3s_1 : 5s_1 = 1 : 3 : 5 \dots \)
