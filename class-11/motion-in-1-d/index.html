Motion in 1D

Kinematics of a particle moving along a straight line.

Conceptual Overview

Mechanics is the branch of physics that studies motion and the causes of motion.

In this first section of mechanics, we not only begin learning mechanics, but also learn how physics itself studies nature mathematically.

In science, we do not describe things casually or emotionally. We describe them quantitatively using measurable physical quantities.

For example, we do not say Virat Kohli is a great batsman because of his tattoos or hairstyle. We describe batting skill mathematically and statistically.

We say that Virat Kohli has scored 10000+ runs, has a batting average above 40, and has scored more than 50 centuries. This is how science-like description works — through measurable quantities and mathematical relationships.

Similarly, in mechanics, we first learn how to describe motion mathematically using physical quantities such as position, displacement, velocity and acceleration.

This mathematical description of motion is called kinematics.

Kinematics — The Mathematics of Mechanics

Kinematics studies motion without worrying about what causes the motion.

In kinematics, we focus only on describing how an object moves. We do not initially care about the forces responsible for producing that motion.

If the problem says that the acceleration of a car is \(2\ m/s^2\), then we directly accept that information.
We do not have to determine the acceleration by analyzing the engine torque, frictional force from the road, air resistance etc.

The branch of mechanics that studies the causes of motion, such as forces and interactions, is called dynamics. That comes later.

Kinematics acts as the mathematical foundation of mechanics. Before studying why motion happens, we must first learn how to describe motion properly.

Different Mathematical Types of Motion

Not all motion is mathematically identical.

Different geometrical constraints produce different mathematical types of motion. Motion along a straight line is mathematically different from motion in a plane. Circular motion is mathematically different from oscillatory motion.

Although all of them belong to mechanics, they are studied differently because their mathematical descriptions are different.

It is somewhat similar to cricket itself having different formats like T20, ODI and Test cricket. All of them are still cricket, but the strategies, pacing and analysis differ.

Similarly, different types of motion require different mathematical tools and descriptions.

For example, oscillatory motion and circular motion are periodic motions. Hence they involve additional physical quantities such as time period, frequency and angular frequency.

Motion in 1D

Motion in 1D (one-dimensional motion) is the simplest mathematically constrained form of motion.

In this chapter, we restrict our study to objects moving only along a straight line.

This restriction greatly simplifies the mathematical treatment of motion.

In higher dimensions, displacement, velocity and acceleration are vector quantities with continuously changing directions. However, in one-dimensional motion, the directional aspect reduces to a simple sign convention.

Quantities can now be represented using positive or negative algebraic values depending on the chosen direction of motion.

Despite its apparent simplicity, Motion in 1D forms the conceptual foundation of all classical mechanics.

Important ideas such as instantaneous velocity, acceleration, relative motion, graphical interpretation of motion and the equations of uniformly accelerated motion are first introduced here.

A deep understanding of concepts such as distance vs displacement or speed vs velocity is extremely important, since confusion between these ideas leads to many conceptual mistakes in physics problem-solving.

Study of Generic Motion

Motion in 1D is not only about straight-line motion itself. It is also our first introduction to many concepts that are true for all types of motion.

For example, the concept of a frame of reference is universal. It applies equally to one-dimensional motion, two-dimensional motion, circular motion and all other forms of motion.

Similarly, the definitions of physical quantities such as displacement, velocity and acceleration remain fundamentally the same in all types of motion. Only their mathematical representation becomes more sophisticated in higher dimensions.

In one-dimensional motion, all vectors are constrained to lie along the same straight line. Hence, their directional nature reduces to a simple sign convention using positive and negative values.

Mathematically, velocity and acceleration in 1D motion are always parallel or anti-parallel to each other geometrically. Their vector nature therefore becomes much simpler compared to motion in two or three dimensions.

In one-dimensional motion, all vectors lie along the same line. Hence,
\(\vec{a} \times \vec{v} = 0\), and \(\vec{v} \neq 0\).
since the vectors are always parallel or anti-parallel.

Thus, Motion in 1D serves two purposes:

  • It teaches the basic universal language of motion.
  • It introduces the special simplifications possible in straight-line motion.

Another extremely important idea introduced here is that motion is a relative phenomenon.

Motion is always relative.
An object can be at rest in one frame of reference while moving in another.
We must always define our origin and positive direction before analyzing any motion.

The observer, coordinate system, origin and chosen positive direction all play an important role in describing motion mathematically.
