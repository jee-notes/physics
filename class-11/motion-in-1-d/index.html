Motion in 1D

Kinematics of a particle moving along a straight line.

Conceptual Overview

Mechanics is the branch of physics that studies the natural phenomenon of motion.

It is usually the first branch of physics that students study. More importantly, it introduces the general scientific approach used throughout physics to describe natural phenomena.

Mechanics itself is a vast subject and is broadly divided into two branches: kinematics and dynamics. We begin with kinematics because, before explaining why objects move, we must first learn how to describe motion mathematically.

Science does not describe nature casually or emotionally. Instead, it describes natural phenomena quantitatively using measurable physical quantities and mathematical relationships.

For example, we do not say Virat Kohli is a great batsman because of his tattoos or hairstyle.
We describe batting skill mathematically and statistically.
We say that Virat Kohli has scored 10000+ runs, has a batting average above 40, and has scored more than 50 centuries.
We describe batting skill using measurable statistics such as the total number of runs scored, batting average, strike rate, centuries etc.
This is how science-like description works — through measurable quantities and mathematical relationships.

Similarly, in mechanics, motion as a natural phenomenon, is described using measurable physical quantities such as position, displacement, velocity and acceleration.

The branch of mechanics that develops this mathematical description of motion, without initially considering its causes, is called kinematics.

Within kinematics, motion in a straight line forms a separate topic because the mathematical framework used to describe one-dimensional motion is considerably simpler than that required for motion in two or three dimensions.

Kinematics — The Mathematics of Mechanics

Motion in One Dimension is a part of Kinematics, which in turn is a branch of Mechanics. Having understood where this chapter fits within mechanics, let us now understand the distinction between Kinematics and Dynamics.

Kinematics is concerned only with the mathematical description of motion. It tells us how an object moves without asking why it moves.

In kinematics, quantities such as position, displacement, velocity and acceleration are treated as given or measured. We do not analyze the physical interactions responsible for producing them.

If a problem states that the acceleration of a car is \(2 \text{ m/s}^2\), we simply accept that value as given.
We do not determine it by analyzing the engine torque, friction from the road, air resistance or other forces acting on the car.

The study of the causes of motion—such as forces and interactions—is the subject of Dynamics. Once we have learned how to describe motion mathematically, we can then study why that motion occurs.

Thus, kinematics forms the mathematical foundation of mechanics. Before explaining the causes of motion, we must first learn the language used to describe it.

Kinematics does not yet introduce the physical laws that govern motion. Instead, it develops the mathematical framework used to describe motion in space and time.

It is more like a continuation of the previous chapter on mathematical tools. Here, mathematical concepts are actually applied to represent the geometry of physical space and the progression of time, allowing us to define physical quantities such as position, displacement, velocity and acceleration.

These are not consequences of physical laws, but mathematical descriptions based on the properties of space and time.

Different Types of Motion based on Mathematical Description

Kinematics is further divided into different topics because not all types of motion can be described using the same mathematical framework.

Different geometrical constraints lead to different mathematical descriptions of motion. As a result, each type of motion requires its own physical quantities, equations and methods of analysis.

For example, the mathematics used to describe motion along a straight line differs from that required to describe motion in a plane. Likewise, circular motion and oscillatory motion each possess their own distinctive mathematical framework.

Although all of these belong to kinematics, they are studied separately because each involves a different mathematical treatment.

Different geometrical constraints → Different mathematical framework → Different chapter

For instance, circular motion and oscillatory motion are periodic motions. Consequently, they introduce additional physical quantities such as time period, frequency and angular frequency that are unnecessary in the study of general straight-line motion.

Motion in 1D

Motion in One Dimension (Motion in 1D) is the simplest mathematical type of motion studied in kinematics.

In one-dimensional motion, the particle is constrained to move only along a straight line. Consequently, it possesses only one degree of freedom, and its position can be completely specified using a single coordinate.

Since motion is restricted to a single dimension, all vector quantities such as displacement, velocity and acceleration are constrained to lie along the same straight line. Their vector nature therefore reduces to a simple sign convention based on the chosen positive direction.

Thus, quantities such as displacement, velocity and acceleration can be represented by positive or negative algebraic values instead of full vector notation.

Throughout this chapter, we shall restrict our study to particles moving only along a straight line and develop the mathematical framework required to describe such motion.

Despite its mathematical simplicity, Motion in One Dimension forms the conceptual foundation of all classical mechanics.

Fundamental concepts such as instantaneous velocity, acceleration, relative motion, graphical interpretation of motion and the equations of uniformly accelerated motion are introduced here before being extended to higher dimensions.

A clear understanding of concepts such as distance and displacement, or speed and velocity, is essential because these ideas recur throughout mechanics and are a common source of conceptual errors.

Study of Generic Motion

Motion in One Dimension is much more than the study of straight-line motion. It is the first chapter of kinematics because it introduces many of the fundamental concepts and physical quantities that are applicable to every type of motion studied later.

For example, the concept of a frame of reference is universal. It applies equally to one-dimensional motion, two-dimensional motion, circular motion, oscillatory motion and all other forms of motion.

Similarly, the definitions of physical quantities such as position, displacement, velocity and acceleration remain fundamentally the same for all types of motion. Only their mathematical representation becomes more sophisticated as we move to higher dimensions.

One-dimensional motion, however, possesses several mathematical simplifications. Since the motion is constrained to a single straight line, all vector quantities lie along that line and their directional nature reduces to a simple sign convention using positive and negative values.

Consequently, velocity and acceleration are always either parallel or anti-parallel to each other. Their vector nature therefore becomes much simpler than in two- or three-dimensional motion.

In one-dimensional motion, all vectors lie along the same straight line. Hence, \[ \vec{a}\times\vec{v}=0 \] since the vectors are always parallel or anti-parallel.

Thus, Motion in One Dimension serves two important purposes:

  • It introduces the universal concepts and physical quantities used to describe every type of motion.
  • It demonstrates the special mathematical simplifications that arise when motion is constrained to a single dimension.

Another fundamental idea introduced in this chapter is that motion is a relative phenomenon.

Motion is always relative.
An object can be at rest in one frame of reference while moving in another.
Therefore, we must always specify the frame of reference, origin and positive direction before describing motion mathematically.

These ideas form the conceptual foundation for all subsequent chapters in mechanics, where the same physical quantities and principles are applied to progressively more complex types of motion.
