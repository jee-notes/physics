Speed and Velocity

Understanding how fast an object is moving and in what direction.

Speed and Velocity

Both speed and velocity are rates of change with respect to time. Speed is the rate of change of distance, whereas velocity is the rate of change of displacement.

These quantities introduce the time factor into the study of motion.

Speed and velocity connect space and time—the two fundamental elements of the physical world.
It is therefore remarkable that one of the fundamental constants of nature, the speed of light \(c\), is itself a speed.

In general, if \(y=f(x)\), then \[ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} \] represents the rate of change of \(y\) with respect to \(x\).

Whenever a physical quantity is defined as a rate, it can be mathematically described in two ways:

  • Average Rate
  • Instantaneous Rate

A familiar example is the run rate in cricket. Suppose a team scores 180 runs in 30 overs. The average run rate is \[ \frac{180}{30}=6 \] runs per over. However, it is possible that the team scored 8 runs in the last over. Thus, the average run rate over the entire innings and the run rate at a particular instant need not be the same.

We shall now study the concepts of average speed, average velocity, instantaneous speed, and instantaneous velocity.

Average Speed and Average Velocity

When an object moves, it takes time to travel a certain distance and undergo a certain displacement. The rates at which these quantities change over a finite time interval are called average speed and average velocity.

Average Speed

Average speed is defined as the total distance traveled divided by the total time interval.

\[ v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\text{Total Distance}}{\text{Total Time Interval}} = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t} \]
  • Nature: Scalar quantity.
  • SI Unit: \( \text{m/s} \) (meters per second).
  • Dimensional Formula: \( [M^0 L^1 T^{-1}] \)

Average speed gives us an overall idea of how fast the object moved during the entire interval, but it tells us nothing about how the speed varied or what direction the object moved in.

Average Velocity

Average velocity is defined as the total displacement divided by the total time interval.

\[ \vec{v}_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\text{Total Displacement}}{\text{Total Time Interval}} = \frac{\Delta \vec{x}}{\Delta t} = \frac{\vec{x}_f - \vec{x}_i}{t_f - t_i} \] Where:

  • \( \Delta \vec{x} \) = Displacement vector
  • \( \Delta t \) = Time interval
  • Nature: Vector quantity.
  • SI Unit: \( \text{m/s} \) (meters per second).
  • Dimensional Formula: \( [M^0 L^1 T^{-1}] \)
Because displacement can be positive, negative, or zero, average velocity can also be positive, negative, or zero.
In contrast, average speed is always positive or zero.

Key Property

The magnitude of the average velocity is always less than or equal to the average speed.

\[ |\vec{v}_{\text{avg}}| \le v_{\text{avg}} \]

Equality holds only when the motion is along a straight line without any reversal of direction (unidirectional motion).

Key Property

Average speed and average velocity are associated with a time interval, not with a single instant of time.

For example, it is perfectly valid to say that the average speed during the first 3 seconds was 50 m/s. However, it is not meaningful to say that the average speed at \(t=3\) s was 50 m/s.

Similarly, it is perfectly valid to say that the average velocity during the interval from \(t=3\) s to \(t=5\) s was 50 m/s. However, it is not meaningful to say that the average velocity at \(t=3\) s was 50 m/s.

Since average quantities are defined over an interval, the notation \(\Delta t\) is used to explicitly represent a finite time interval.

Instantaneous Speed and Velocity

In many physical situations, we need to know the velocity of a particle at a specific instant of time, not just over a large interval.

Instantaneous Velocity

Instantaneous velocity is the limit of average velocity as the time interval \( \Delta t \) approaches zero.

\[ \vec{v} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta \vec{x}}{\Delta t} = \frac{d\vec{x}}{dt} \] Where \( \frac{d\vec{x}}{dt} \) represents the derivative of position with respect to time.

Thus, instantaneous velocity may be regarded as the limiting value of the average velocity as the time interval shrinks to an instant.

In one-dimensional motion, instantaneous velocity is simply \( v = \frac{dx}{dt} \). A positive \( v \) indicates motion in the positive x-direction, while a negative \( v \) indicates motion in the negative x-direction.

Instantaneous Speed

The instantaneous speed is always equal to the magnitude of the instantaneous velocity. \[ \text{Speed} = |\vec{v}| = \left| \frac{dx}{dt} \right| \]

Unlike average speed and average velocity (where magnitude of average velocity can be strictly less than average speed), at any given exact instant, the speed is simply how fast you are going, which is the magnitude of your velocity vector.

Key Property

Thus, to define the instantaneous velocity \[ v=\frac{dx}{dt} \] the position \(x(t)\) must be a continuous and differentiable function of time.

Otherwise, imagine a person teleporting instantaneously from position \(x=x_1\) to position \(x=x_2\).

At one instant, the person is at \(x_1\), and at the very next instant, they are at \(x_2\) without passing through the intermediate positions.

In such a situation, the instantaneous velocity cannot be defined.

Key Property

Instantaneous speed and instantaneous velocity are associated with an single instant of time, and not with a time interval. It is time indexed like position.

For example, it is perfectly valid to say that the instantaneous speed at \(t=3\) s was 50 m/s. However, it is not meaningful to say that the instantaneous speed during the first 3 seconds was 50 m/s.

The concept of instantaneous velocity is fundamental because, to define acceleration, velocity must first be defined as a continuous differentiable function of time, \(v = f(t)\) , just like position.

Average velocity is associated with a finite time interval and provides only an overall description of the motion during that interval.

It compresses the information over the entire interval into a single constant velocity that would produce the same displacement over the same time interval.

Since this single value contains no information about how the velocity changed within the interval, it cannot be used to describe the change in velocity itself.

To study how the velocity changes from one instant to the next, we require the concept of instantaneous velocity. This naturally leads to the definition of acceleration.
