Motion Under Gravity

The kinematics of free fall near the Earth's surface.

Concept of Free Fall

The most common example of motion with nearly constant acceleration is that of an object falling toward the Earth. In the absence of air resistance, all bodies—regardless of their mass or size—fall with the same constant acceleration. This idealized motion is called free fall.

Acceleration due to Gravity (\( g \))
Near the surface of the Earth, the acceleration due to gravity is approximately constant and is denoted by \( g \). \[ g \approx 9.8 \, \text{m/s}^2 \quad \text{or occasionally approximated as} \quad 10 \, \text{m/s}^2 \] This acceleration always acts vertically downward, toward the center of the Earth.

Sign Convention and Equations

Because motion under gravity is a 1D vector problem, setting up a consistent sign convention is critical to avoid errors. The kinematic equations are applied directly, but with \( a \) replaced by \( \pm g \) depending on your chosen coordinate system.

Standard Sign Convention: It is generally easiest to take the upward direction as positive (+y) and the downward direction as negative (-y). Since gravity always pulls downwards, the acceleration is always: \[ a = -g \] Regardless of whether the object is moving up, moving down, or at its highest point, the acceleration is always \( -9.8 \, \text{m/s}^2 \).

Substituting \( a = -g \) into our kinematic equations (and using \( y \) for vertical displacement):

  • \( v = u - gt \)
  • \( y = ut - \frac{1}{2}gt^2 \)
  • \( v^2 = u^2 - 2gy \)

Important Standard Results

Consider a particle projected vertically upwards from the ground with an initial velocity \( u \).

1. Maximum Height (\( H_{\text{max}} \))

At the maximum height, the instantaneous velocity of the particle is zero (\( v = 0 \)). Using \( v^2 = u^2 - 2gH_{\text{max}} \): \[ 0 = u^2 - 2gH_{\text{max}} \implies H_{\text{max}} = \frac{u^2}{2g} \]

2. Time of Ascent (\( t_a \)) and Time of Descent (\( t_d \))

The time taken to reach the maximum height is the time of ascent. Using \( v = u - gt_a \) and setting \( v = 0 \): \[ 0 = u - gt_a \implies t_a = \frac{u}{g} \] By symmetry (ignoring air resistance), the time taken to fall back to the ground equals the time of ascent. Thus, time of descent \( t_d = \frac{u}{g} \).

3. Total Time of Flight (\( T \))

The total time the particle remains in the air is the sum of ascent and descent times: \[ T = t_a + t_d = \frac{2u}{g} \] Alternatively, use \( y = ut - \frac{1}{2}gt^2 \). For the full journey, displacement \( y = 0 \): \[ 0 = uT - \frac{1}{2}gT^2 \implies T = \frac{2u}{g} \]

4. Velocity on Returning to Ground

When the particle returns to the launch point, its displacement is zero. Using \( v^2 = u^2 - 2gy \) with \( y=0 \): \[ v^2 = u^2 \implies v = -u \] (The negative sign indicates the particle is moving downwards with the same speed it was projected upwards).
