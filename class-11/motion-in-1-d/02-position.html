Position

Position is a fundamental physical quantity in mechanics that is relative.

Position is the Foundation of Motion

In mechanics, the entire study of motion ultimately begins with the idea of position.

This is because motion itself is defined as a change in position with time.

Before studying velocity, acceleration or forces, we must first learn how to mathematically define where an object is located.

In physics, motion and rest are treated as mutually exclusive mechanical states in which a body can exist.

This is somewhat similar to how a human being can exist in different emotional states such as happiness or sadness. In mechanics, however, the distinction between states is made purely mathematically using position (space and time).

  • Rest: An object is said to be at rest if it does not change its position with respect to its surroundings with the passage of time.
  • Motion: An object is said to be in motion if it changes its position with respect to its surroundings with the passage of time.

Thus, the entire subject of kinematics is fundamentally about defining position mathematically and studying how it changes with time (position and its time derivatives).

Physics does not just describe these states in words; it builds a mathematical engine to predict them. The process of building this mathematical description is called formulation.

Formulation = Definition + Derivation

Co-ordinate System and Position

In mathematics, position is defined using a co-ordinate system.

Thus, the position of a particle is its location with respect to the origin of a chosen co-ordinate system.

The phrase "with respect to the origin" is extremely important.

The coordinates \( (x,y,z) \) of a point are meaningful only because an origin is first defined as \( (0,0,0) \).

Therefore, before defining position, we must first choose:

  • a co-ordinate system
  • an origin (a spatial reference point)
  • a positive direction

Without these choices, position cannot be mathematically specified.

Position in One-Dimensional Motion

In one-dimensional motion, the object moves only along a single straight line.

That line itself is chosen as the \( x \)-axis.

Examples of one-dimensional motion include:

  • a car moving along a straight road
  • a train moving on a straight track
  • or a ball falling vertically under gravity

In such cases, the position of the object is represented simply by the coordinate \( x \).

The value of \( x \) can be positive, negative or zero depending on the chosen origin and positive direction.

Generally, the starting position is chosen as the origin so that the initial displacement becomes zero.

This idea becomes clearer once we formally study displacement.

Once we learn about displacement, we can conclude that position itself is a special case of displacement from the origin.
It is not that an actual physical object is displaced from the origin.
Rather, position measures how far the point itself is displaced relative to the origin.

Position Needs Definite Time

A well-defined position implies a definite spatial coordinate at a definite instant of time.

A particle can occupy different positions at different instants of time. Even when we discuss position strictly in terms of spatial coordinates, time is always implicitly involved.

If we represent a moving body as a point particle for simplicity, then in one-dimensional motion its position \( x \) must be specified at different time instants.

For example:

At time \( t=t_1 \), the position may be \( x=x_1 \).

At time \( t=t_2 \), the position may become \( x=x_2 \).

Thus, position is always time-indexed. This means that mathematically, for one dimension, position is not simply \( x \), but \( x(t) \) by definition.

To understand this physically, consider the 100 m Olympic race in which the runners move in one dimension.
Suppose we choose the starting line as the origin.
At the instant \( t=0 \), just before the race starts, the runner is at: \[ x=0 \] Now consider tracking the position of Usain Bolt during the race.
At time \( t=1\text{ s} \), his position may be: \[ x=10\text{ m} \] At later times, his position keeps changing continuously.

Since the position of the runner changes with time relative to the starting point, the runner is said to be in a state of motion.

Without information about the time domain, we only know the spatial region in which the particle may exist, but not its exact space-time state.

For example, in the Olympic race discussed above, if we remove the time information entirely, we can only say that the runner exists somewhere on the race track.

This idea loosely resembles the concept of atomic orbitals in quantum mechanics, where we do not specify the exact position of the electron as a single point, but instead describe a spatial region in which the probability of finding the electron is high.

Consider another everyday example of this separation between space and time. Suppose you are studying and receive a phone call. You answer and say, "I am busy in class" (or "Class mein hu, baad mein baat karta hu").

In this conversation, you are defining your state purely in the time domain, not the space domain. Spatially, you might be sitting in an offline class in a school building, or you might be taking an online class from your room at home. The caller does not know your exact spatial coordinate (\( x \)), but they clearly understand your temporal coordinate (\( t \)).

Just as the race track example gave us a spatial region without exact time, the phone call gives us a time interval without exact space. Thus, to pinpoint a complete mechanical event, a fully defined classical position requires both:

  • a definite spatial coordinate
  • and a definite instant of time

Position as a Space-Time Quantity

When we talk about the position of a particle, we are referring to the point in space occupied by the particle at a particular instant of time.

Thus, position is not merely a spatial concept. It is implicitly connected to time as well.

A well-defined position is a definite state of space-time existence in the physical world.

Space and Time Both Need References

A coordinate is meaningful only if the spatial origin is defined.

Similarly, a time measurement is meaningful only if a time reference is defined.

In most practical situations, the reference instant of time is understood implicitly from context.

Time is often implied indirectly through language itself.
For example, if we say: "The book was on the table",
the past tense automatically implies that the position refers to an earlier instant of time from now (present instant).

Thus, position in physics is fundamentally:

  • well-defined in space
  • and well-defined in time

A well-defined space requires a defined origin.

A well-defined time also requires a reference instant, although it is often implicit.

The most common reference used to define position is: Now Here - the current location and current instant of time (present).
