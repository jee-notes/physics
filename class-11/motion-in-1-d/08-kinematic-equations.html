Kinematic Equations

The equations of motion for constant acceleration.

Equations for Uniformly Accelerated Motion

When an object moves with a constant (uniform) acceleration, its velocity changes at a constant rate. Under this condition, the calculus of motion reduces to a set of simple algebraic equations known as the equations of uniformly accelerated motion.

Consider an object with an initial velocity \(u\) at time \(t=0\), moving with a constant acceleration \(a\). Let its velocity become \(v\) after a time interval \(t\), during which it undergoes a displacement \(s\).

\[ v=u+at \] \[ s=ut+\frac{1}{2}at^2 \]

These equations involve five variables: \(u\), \(t\), \(s\), \(a\) and \(v\). A popular mnemonic for remembering them is UTSAV.

The variables \(t\) and \(s\) are interval variables. The time \(t\) represents the interval from \(t=0\) to \(t=t\), while the displacement \(s\) is the displacement accumulated during this interval.

The variables \(u\), \(v\) and \(a\) are instantaneous variables. The velocity \(u\) is the instantaneous velocity at \(t=0\), the velocity \(v\) is the instantaneous velocity at time \(t\), and \(a\) is the constant instantaneous acceleration throughout the interval.

Several other useful equations can be obtained by combining or rearranging these two basic equations:

\[ s=\left(\frac{u+v}{2}\right)t \] \[ v^2=u^2+2as \] \[ s=vt-\frac{1}{2}at^2 \]

Displacement in \(n^{\text{th}}\) second

There is a common formula to determine displacement during the \(n^{\text{th}}\) second of motion. But what is the time duration of \(n^{\text{th}}\) second?

The \(n^{\text{th}}\) second is the time interval between the instants \(t=n-1\) and \(t=n\). For example, the 3rd second is the interval from \(t=2\) s to \(t=3\) s. Students often mistakenly assume that the \(n^{\text{th}}\) second is the interval from \(t=n\) to \(t=n+1\), but this is incorrect because the 1st second begins at \(t=0\) s and ends at \(t=1\) s.

The formula for displacement during the \(n^{\text{th}}\) is:

\[ S_n=u+\frac{a}{2}(2n-1) \]

The derivation relies on the following property: Displacement is additive over time.. The displacement during the \(n^{\text{th}}\) second is obtained by subtracting the displacement covered in the first \((n-1)\) seconds from the displacement covered in the first \(n\) seconds:

\[ S_n=S(n)-S(n-1) \]

This follows from the additive nature of displacement over successive time intervals. Once displacement is defined as the time integral of velocity, the displacement over a longer interval equals the sum of the displacements over its constituent intervals. Mathematically, this simply means that - if \(v(t)\) is an integrable function of \(t\) over the interval [\(t_1, t_3\)], then:

\[ \int_{t_1}^{t_3}v(t)\,dt = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}v(t)\,dt + \int_{t_2}^{t_3}v(t)\,dt \qquad (t_1 \le t_2 \le t_3) \]

Derivation using Calculus

The kinematic equations for uniformly accelerated motion follow directly from the definitions of velocity and acceleration. Throughout this derivation, assume that the acceleration \(a\) is constant.

1. Deriving \(v=u+at\)

Acceleration is defined as the rate of change of velocity:

\[ a=\frac{dv}{dt} \] \[ dv=a\,dt \]

Integrating both sides from \(t=0\) (where the velocity is \(u\)) to time \(t\) (where the velocity is \(v\)),

\[ \int_u^v dv=\int_0^t a\,dt \]

Since \(a\) is constant, it can be taken outside the integral:

\[ [v]_u^v=a[t]_0^t \] \[ v-u=at \] \[ \boxed{v=u+at} \]

2. Deriving \(s=ut+\frac12at^2\)

Velocity is defined as the rate of change of position:

\[ v=\frac{dx}{dt} \]

Substituting the first equation,

\[ \frac{dx}{dt}=u+at \] \[ dx=(u+at)\,dt \]

Integrating from \(t=0\) (where position \(x=x_0\)) to time \(t\) (where position \(x=x\)),

\[ \int_{x_0}^{x} dx=\int_0^t (u+at)\,dt \] \[ [x]_{x_0}^{x}=\left[ut+\frac12at^2\right]_0^t \]

At LHS, the quantity \(x-x_0\) represents change in position, also called as displacement \(s\),

\[ \boxed{s=ut+\frac12at^2} \]
The derivation actually involves solving two first-order differential equations sequentially.
First, \[ \frac{dv}{dt}=a \] whose integration introduces one integration constant, determined by the initial condition \[ v(0)=u \] Next, \[ \frac{dx}{dt}=v \] whose integration introduces a second integration constant, determined by the initial condition \[ x(0)=x_0 \] Thus, the complete solution is \[ x=x_0+ut+\frac12at^2 \] Finally, using \[ s=x-x_0 \] we obtain \[ s=ut+\frac12at^2 \] If the derivation is carried out directly in terms of displacement \(s\), the initial position \(x_0\) is absorbed into the definition of displacement, hiding the second integration constant.
A differential equation describes a family of possible solutions, not a unique solution.
Integrating the differential equation introduces an integration constant, giving the general solution.
The value of the integration constant is determined by an initial condition (or boundary condition), thereby selecting the unique solution corresponding to the physical problem.

3. Deriving \(v^2=u^2+2as\)

Using the chain-rule form of acceleration,

\[ a=v\frac{dv}{ds} \] \[ a\,ds=v\,dv \]

Integrating from the initial state \((s=0,\;v=u)\) to the final state \((s,\;v=v)\),

\[ \int_0^s a\,ds=\int_u^v v\,dv \] \[ a[s]_0^s=\left[\frac{v^2}{2}\right]_u^v \] \[ as=\frac{v^2-u^2}{2} \] \[ \boxed{v^2=u^2+2as} \]
The third equation is not an independent kinematic equation.
Only the two differential relations \[ v=\frac{ds}{dt} \qquad\text{and}\qquad a=\frac{dv}{dt} \] are fundamental. Every other kinematic equation is obtained by algebraic manipulation or integration of these two relations.
Thus, although there are five variables (\(u,\;v,\;a,\;s,\;t\)), there are only two independent equations relating them.
Therefore, three independent quantities must be specified to determine the remaining two.

Galileo's Law of Odd Numbers

Galileo made a profound observation about bodies moving with constant acceleration from rest. This is a special case of uniformly accelerated motion corresponding to \(u=0\).

Galileo's Law of Odd Numbers (GLON)

The distances traversed by a body, during successive equal intervals of time, are in the ratio of the odd numbers beginning with unity:

\[ 1 : 3 : 5 : 7 : 9 : \cdots \]

Proof

For motion starting from rest (\(u=0\)),

\[ s=\frac{1}{2}at^2 \]

Let each time interval have duration \(t_0\). Let \(S_n\) denote the displacement from the start after \(n\) intervals.

  • After the first interval, \[ S_1=\frac{1}{2}at_0^2 \] Hence, the distance covered in the first interval is \[ S_1 \]
  • After the second interval, \[ S_2=\frac{1}{2}a(2t_0)^2=4S_1 \] Hence, the distance covered in the second interval is \[ S_2-S_1=3S_1 \]
  • After the third interval, \[ S_3=\frac{1}{2}a(3t_0)^2=9S_1 \] Hence, the distance covered in the third interval is \[ S_3-S_2=5S_1 \]

Therefore, the distances covered in successive equal intervals of time are in the ratio

\[ S_1:3S_1:5S_1:\cdots=1:3:5:\cdots \]
The intervals need only be of equal duration; they need not be \(1\,\text{s}\).
Galileo's Law of Odd Numbers is valid only for motion starting from rest (\(u=0\)).
If the initial velocity is non-zero, the distances covered in successive equal intervals are no longer in the ratio \[ 1:3:5:\cdots \]
