Motion as a State

Qualitatively, motion is a state assigned to a body by an observer.

State as an Instantaneous Description

What do we mean by a state? A state is simply a description of how something is at a particular instant in time.

For example, a person may be happy or sad, awake or asleep, rich or poor, loud or quiet. A physical object may be hot or cold, charged or neutral etc.

Similarly, a physical object may be at rest or in motion. Motion is a natural phenomenon, and an object may therefore be said to be in a state of rest or in a state of motion.

However, not all states are of the same kind. Some states belong to an object itself, while others arise only when the object is compared with another object or an observer.

Type of State Examples Observer Dependent?
Intrinsic Mass, charge, temperature No
Relational Position, rest, motion Yes

The states of rest and motion belong to the second category. They are relational states, meaning that they acquire meaning only when a body is compared with an observer.

A useful analogy is the percentile system used in examinations. Suppose a student scores 150 marks. The score itself is an intrinsic attribute of the student's performance. However, being in the 95th percentile is a relational quantity because it only tells us how the student's performance compares with that of other candidates.

Similarly, a body cannot simply be said to be moving or at rest in isolation. These states only have meaning when we specify with respect to whom the body is being described.

Qualitative is important

Physics ultimately seeks quantitative descriptions of natural phenomena because mathematical descriptions allow us to communicate, analyze, and predict physical behaviour with great precision.

However, we do not perceive the physical world in terms of numbers and equations. We first recognize phenomena qualitatively and then develop mathematical tools to describe them precisely.

Thus, before studying motion quantitatively, it is useful to understand it qualitatively as a relational state assigned to a body by an observer.

In these notes, we shall often begin with qualitative descriptions before introducing mathematical formulations. Qualitative understanding nurtures the curiosity and intuition that make quantitative analysis meaningful and easier to appreciate.

State of Rest

How do we naturally decide if something is at rest?

Suppose you place a book on your study table. You note its location and look away to do something else. Then, after a minute, you look back to find the book exactly where you left it. Your intuition immediately tells you: "the book hasn't moved."

What exactly did your brain do in this process? It acted as an observer. It checked the position of the book relative to your own position across a given duration of time. Because that relative position remained identical as time ticked by, you assigned the book a specific state.

This everyday intuition leads directly to our formal definition: an object is in a state of rest with respect to an observer if its position relative to that observer does not change as time passes.

Physical Examples of Rest

  • A book on a desk: To a student sitting quietly in the room, the distance and direction of the book do not change over time. The book is at rest relative to the student.
  • India and Nepal: The relative positions of India and Nepal remain practically unchanged over ordinary human timescales. Thus, they may be regarded as being at rest relative to one another, even though the Earth itself rotates about its axis and revolves around the Sun.
  • Passengers in a moving vehicle: Consider two passengers sitting next to each other in a cruising airplane. Even though the plane is traveling at 800 km/h relative to the ground, the relative position of the two passengers is not changing. Therefore, they are in a state of rest relative to one another.

State of Motion

How do we naturally decide if something is in motion?

Suppose you are standing on a sidewalk and watching a car drive by. You note its location down the street, and a few seconds later, it passes right in front of you. Your intuition immediately tells you: "the car is moving."

Once again, what did your brain do? It acted as an observer. It tracked the position of the car relative to your own position across a given duration of time. Because that relative position continuously changed as time ticked by, you assigned the car a different state.

This leads directly to our formal definition: an object is in a state of motion with respect to an observer if its position relative to that observer changes as time passes.

Physical Examples of Motion

  • A falling apple: To a person sitting under a tree, an apple detaching from a branch continuously changes its position as it falls toward the ground. It is in a state of motion relative to the person.
  • The airplane passengers (from outside): Let's revisit the airplane passengers. To an observer standing on the ground looking up at the sky, the airplane and the passengers inside are changing their position continuously. Thus, the exact same passengers who are at rest relative to each other are in a state of motion relative to the ground observer.
  • The Sun in the sky: To an observer standing on Earth, the Sun appears to rise in the east, travel across the sky, and set in the west. Its position relative to the observer changes throughout the day. Therefore, the Sun is in a state of motion relative to an observer on Earth.

The Role of the Backdrop

So far, we have discussed rest and motion in terms of an observer. However, this is a slight simplification. In physics, we often begin with simple definitions that capture the essential idea and gradually refine them to describe reality more completely.

Consider the example of watching a car drive past you. How did your brain actually determine that the car was moving?

You did not consciously measure the distance between your eyes and the car at successive instants of time. Instead, you naturally perceived the car changing its position relative to the surrounding environment — the road, the trees, the parked vehicles and the buildings.

Thus, in everyday life, an observer judges the position of an object not merely relative to their own body, but relative to a surrounding environment whose parts remain fixed with respect to one another. We shall refer to such an environment as a rigid backdrop.

The importance of a rigid backdrop can be appreciated through a thought experiment. Imagine floating alone in a completely dark and empty space with only a single glowing ball visible. If the ball moves in a circle around you while maintaining a constant distance, it becomes difficult to determine whether the ball is moving, whether you are rotating, or whether both are rotating relative to something else.

Without a rigid backdrop, distinguishing certain kinds of motion may become ambiguous.

Thus, when we say that a body is moving or at rest relative to an observer, we implicitly mean that its position is being judged relative to the observer together with the rigid backdrop attached to that observer.

Every point of such a backdrop remains permanently at rest relative to every other point of the same backdrop.

In physics, this combination of an observer and their rigid backdrop forms what we formally call a Frame of Reference.
