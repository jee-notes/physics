Frame of Reference

The mathematical framework required to describe motion.

The Relativity of Motion

One of the most fundamental principles in classical mechanics is that motion is relative.

There is no such thing as absolute rest or absolute motion in the universe.

The mechanical state of an object—whether it is moving or stationary—cannot be defined in isolation. It can only be mathematically defined with respect to a specific observer or a chosen Frame of Reference.

This relativity manifests in two distinct mathematical ways: through the relativity of Position (static) and the relativity of Displacement (dynamic).

Relativity of Position (Static)

The fact that position is relative is intuitive and purely geometric. The coordinate of a point depends entirely on where we place our origin.

Consider a simple geographical example:

Iran is positioned to the West of India, but to the East of America.

The actual physical location of the landmass of Iran does not change.

However, its mathematical position vector flips direction entirely depending on whether the origin of our coordinate system is placed in New Delhi or Washington D.C.

Position being relative does not in itself make motion relative. It simply necessitates the existence of a reference point to mathematically define space (co-ordinate geometry).

Relativity of Displacement (Dynamic)

While the relativity of position is obvious, the relativity of displacement is dynamic and leads to a profound conclusion: the state of motion itself depends on the observer.

Consider the following scenario involving three people: Person A, Person B and Person C, situated at a building with an elevator (lift).

  • Person A and Person B enter the lift to go up.
  • Person C remains standing on the ground floor.

While the lift is moving upwards, let us mathematically define the mechanical state of Person A:

With respect to Person C (Ground Frame):

The position of Person A is continuously changing relative to Person C. Therefore, Person A has a non-zero displacement over time and is in a state of motion.

With respect to Person B (Lift Frame):

The position of Person A relative to Person B remains constant, as they are standing next to each other inside the cabin. Therefore, Person A has zero displacement over time and is in a state of rest.

The Core Deduction:
The exact same physical body (Person A) can have a non-zero displacement in one frame of reference and a zero displacement in another at the exact same instant.
Therefore, motion is not a property of the object alone. Motion is a combined mathematical property of the object and the observer.

This dynamic relativity of motion is the core phenomenon studied in classical mechanics. This is known as Galilean relativity, whereas the relativity of position is merely geometric relativity.

Defining the Observer

Once we understand that motion is relative (i.e., observer-dependent), the immediate challenge becomes: how do we mathematically standardize our description of motion?

The first conceptual hurdle is the word "observer" itself. Is an observer a living being with eyes?

In physics, an observer is not a biological entity. It is purely a mathematical construct.

An observer is simply a physical body or location to which we attach a coordinate system (spatial reference) and a clock (temporal reference).

If multiple people, sensors or cameras use the exact same origin, axes and clock to measure physical quantities, they are all said to be operating within the same Frame of Reference.

Therefore, physics equations are not written for individual people; they are written for specific frames of reference.

The Need for a Shared Frame: An Example

To understand why a formal Frame of Reference is strictly required over casual human observation, consider the following geometric scenario.

Imagine a rectangular room mapped to a coordinate plane, extending from \( (0,0) \) to \( (10,10) \).

  • Person A is standing at the center \( (5,5) \), facing the wall at \( x = 10 \).
  • Person B is located at \( (5,8) \), facing downwards towards Person A.
  • Person C is located at \( (5,2) \), facing upwards towards Person A.

Now, Person A moves from their initial position \( (5,5) \) to a new position at \( (7,5) \). How do B and C describe this motion?

Because Person B is facing downwards (towards negative \( y \)), they will report: "Person A moved 2 units to my left."
Because Person C is facing upwards (towards positive \( y \)), they will report: "Person A moved 2 units to my right."

Both descriptions ("left" and "right") describe the exact same physical event, yet they mathematically contradict each other. This happens because B and C are using local, body-centric references.

To study physics objectively, B and C must discard their local orientations and arrive at a consensus.

They do this by adopting a shared global geometry. Instead of using "left" or "right", they assign the wall at \( x = 10 \) as the positive \( x \)-direction (e.g., East).

Once this shared geometric axis is established, the contradiction vanishes.
Both Person B and Person C will now agree: "Person A underwent a displacement of \( +2 \) units along the \( +x \)-axis."
By agreeing on this shared coordinate system, Person B and Person C are now operating in the same Frame of Reference.

Observers of the Same Frame of Reference

Observers belonging to the same frame of reference must remain at rest relative to each other.

This is because all observers in a shared frame must agree upon:

  • the origin
  • the orientation of the axes
  • the unit system
  • the clock used to measure time

Thus, a frame of reference is not merely a single observer, but an entire shared mathematical framework used to describe physical events consistently.

If Only the Origin is Different

Different observers who are at rest relative to each other may choose different spatial locations for their origins. This causes the numerical position coordinates (\( x \)) of a particle to differ between observers.

However, because these observers are not moving with respect to each other, the displacement (\( \Delta x \)) between two points remains geometrically identical for both. The main condition for this to be true is that everything else like orientation (direction), unit of length, clock should be same. Only origin is different.

This mathematical property is why displacement behaves as a free vector. Its physical meaning depends only on the relative separation between the initial and final states, entirely independent of where the coordinate origin is placed.

Because displacement is a free vector, as long as observers are at rest relative to each other and share the same axis orientations, they belong to the same frame of reference.
Even if their chosen origins differ, only the position vector \( \vec{r} \) will be different.
Everything else — displacement, velocity, acceleration, momentum and force — will be mathematically identical.

Physically, we can visualize a frame of reference acting as a rigid background — like an invisible, infinite grid — against which all motion takes place.
