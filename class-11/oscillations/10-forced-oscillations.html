Forced Oscillations and Resonance

When an external periodic force drives a damped oscillator — the steady state, amplitude response, and the dramatic phenomenon of resonance.

Equation of Motion

A damped oscillator driven by an external periodic force \(F_0\cos\omega_d t\):

\[m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + b\frac{dx}{dt} + kx = F_0\cos\omega_d t\]

where \(\omega_d\) is the driving (angular) frequency. After initial transients die out, the system settles into a steady-state oscillation at the driving frequency.

Steady-State Solution

\[x(t) = A(\omega_d)\cos(\omega_d t - \delta)\]

The amplitude of the steady-state response:

\[A(\omega_d) = \frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)^2 + (2\gamma\omega_d)^2}}\]

The phase lag behind the driving force:

\[\tan\delta = \frac{2\gamma\omega_d}{\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2}\]
The system always oscillates at the driving frequency \(\omega_d\), not at its natural frequency \(\omega_0\). The driving force "forces" the system to follow its rhythm — the system's "choice" is only the amplitude and the phase lag with which it responds.

Resonance

The amplitude \(A(\omega_d)\) is maximum when \(\omega_d\) is close to \(\omega_0\). For light damping:

\[\omega_d^{\text{res}} \approx \omega_0 \qquad (\text{resonant frequency})\]

At resonance:

\[A_{\max} \approx \frac{F_0}{b\omega_0} = \frac{F_0 Q}{k}\]

where \(Q\) is the quality factor. High \(Q\) means a tall, narrow resonance peak; low \(Q\) means a broad, flat peak.

  • At \(\omega_d \ll \omega_0\): the amplitude approaches \(F_0/k\) (quasi-static limit — displacement follows force)
  • At \(\omega_d = \omega_0\): amplitude is maximum (\(Q\) times the static displacement)
  • At \(\omega_d \gg \omega_0\): amplitude decreases; the massive body cannot follow the rapid driving

Phase Response

  • \(\omega_d \ll \omega_0\): \(\delta \approx 0\) — oscillation is in phase with the driving force.
  • \(\omega_d = \omega_0\): \(\delta = \frac{\pi}{2}\) — oscillation lags by 90° (velocity is in phase with force → maximum power transfer).
  • \(\omega_d \gg \omega_0\): \(\delta \approx \pi\) — oscillation is nearly 180° out of phase.

Sharpness of Resonance

The width of the resonance peak (at half-maximum power, or \(1/\sqrt{2}\) of maximum amplitude) is:

\[\Delta\omega = 2\gamma = \frac{b}{m} = \frac{\omega_0}{Q}\]

A high-\(Q\) system has a sharp, narrow resonance (very selective in frequency); a low-\(Q\) system has a broad resonance (responds to a wide range of frequencies).

Examples of Resonance

  • Tuning a radio: The LC circuit is adjusted so its natural frequency matches the broadcast frequency — resonance amplifies only the desired signal.
  • Tacoma Narrows Bridge: Wind-driven oscillations at the bridge's natural frequency caused catastrophic resonance.
  • Pushing a swing: Pushing at the natural frequency (in phase with velocity) builds up amplitude efficiently.
  • MRI: Radio waves at the Larmor frequency cause nuclear spins to resonate.
Resonance can be beneficial (musical instruments, radio tuning) or destructive (bridge collapse, machine vibrations). Engineering design must account for natural frequencies to avoid unintended resonance.

Power Absorbed at Resonance

The time-averaged power absorbed by the oscillator from the driving force is:

\[\langle P \rangle = \frac{F_0^2}{2b} \cdot \frac{1}{1 + \left(\frac{\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2}{2\gamma\omega_d}\right)^2}\]

This is maximum at \(\omega_d = \omega_0\), where \(\langle P \rangle_{\max} = \frac{F_0^2}{2b}\). At resonance, all the absorbed power goes to sustaining the oscillation against damping.
