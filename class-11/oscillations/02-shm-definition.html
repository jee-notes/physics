Simple Harmonic Motion

The most fundamental oscillation — defined by the linear restoring force \(F = -kx\).

Definition

A particle undergoes simple harmonic motion if the net force on it is:

\[F = -kx\]

where \(x\) is the displacement from the equilibrium position and \(k\) is a positive constant (the "spring constant" or "force constant"). By Newton's second law:

\[ma = -kx \implies a = -\frac{k}{m}x = -\omega^2 x\]

where \(\omega = \sqrt{k/m}\) is the angular frequency.

The hallmark of SHM is that acceleration is proportional to displacement and always directed toward equilibrium. This single condition — \(a = -\omega^2 x\) — is the defining equation of SHM.

The Differential Equation

Writing the defining equation as a differential equation:

\[\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + \omega^2 x = 0\]

This is a second-order linear ODE with constant coefficients. Its general solution is:

\[x(t) = A\sin(\omega t + \phi)\]

or equivalently:

\[x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi')\]

or in the combined form:

\[x(t) = C_1\cos\omega t + C_2\sin\omega t\]

All three forms are equivalent. The choice depends on initial conditions.

Parameters of SHM

  • Amplitude \(A\) — The maximum displacement from equilibrium. Determined by initial conditions (energy), not by system properties.
  • Angular frequency \(\omega\) — Determined by the system's physical properties (\(\omega = \sqrt{k/m}\)). Independent of amplitude.
  • Phase constant \(\phi\) — Determined by the initial position and velocity. Fixes the starting point of the sinusoidal cycle.
  • Time period \(T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega}\) — The time for one complete oscillation. Independent of amplitude (isochronism).

Identifying SHM

To check whether a given motion is SHM, verify any one of these equivalent conditions:

  • \(F = -kx\) (restoring force proportional to displacement)
  • \(a = -\omega^2 x\) (acceleration proportional to displacement, opposite in sign)
  • \(\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + \omega^2 x = 0\) (the differential equation is satisfied)
  • The displacement can be written as \(x = A\sin(\omega t + \phi)\)

SHM as a Projection of Circular Motion

Consider a particle moving in a circle of radius \(A\) with constant angular speed \(\omega\). Its projection onto any diameter traces SHM:

\[x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\]

This geometric connection between uniform circular motion and SHM is extremely useful for:

  • Visualising the phase relationship between displacement, velocity, and acceleration
  • Adding (superposing) two SHMs using the phasor (rotating vector) method
  • Understanding the phase constant \(\phi\) geometrically as the initial angle of the phasor
SHM is the "shadow" of uniform circular motion. The amplitude is the radius of the circle, the angular frequency is the angular speed, and the phase is the angle measured from a reference direction.
