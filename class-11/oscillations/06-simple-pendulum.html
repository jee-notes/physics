Simple Pendulum

A point mass on a massless inextensible string — the classic oscillator, valid for small angles.

Setup and Restoring Force

A simple pendulum consists of a point mass (bob) \(m\) suspended by a light, inextensible string of length \(l\) from a rigid support. For small angular displacement \(\theta\):

\[\text{Restoring force} = -mg\sin\theta \approx -mg\theta = -\frac{mg}{l}s\]

where \(s = l\theta\) is the arc displacement from equilibrium. This is a linear restoring force (\(F \propto s\)), so the motion is SHM.

Time Period

\[T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}\]
  • Independent of mass of the bob
  • Independent of amplitude (for small \(\theta\))
  • Depends only on length and gravitational acceleration
The independence from mass was one of the earliest observed features of pendulum motion (Galileo). It arises because both the inertia (\(m\)) and the restoring force (\(mg\sin\theta\)) are proportional to \(m\), so mass cancels.

Effective Length

The effective length of a pendulum is the distance from the point of suspension to the centre of mass of the bob. For a uniform sphere of radius \(r\) on a string of length \(L\):

\[l_{\text{eff}} = L + r\]

Always use the effective length in the time-period formula.

Effective Gravity

When a pendulum is in a non-inertial frame or additional forces act, replace \(g\) with an effective gravitational acceleration \(g_{\text{eff}}\):

In a Lift

  • Accelerating upward: \(g_{\text{eff}} = g + a\) → \(T\) decreases (faster oscillation)
  • Accelerating downward: \(g_{\text{eff}} = g - a\) → \(T\) increases (slower oscillation)
  • Free fall (\(a = g\)): \(g_{\text{eff}} = 0\) → \(T \to \infty\) (no oscillation)

With a Horizontal Acceleration

If the support accelerates horizontally with acceleration \(a\):

\[g_{\text{eff}} = \sqrt{g^2 + a^2}\]

The equilibrium is no longer vertical — the string makes an angle \(\tan^{-1}(a/g)\) with the vertical.

In a Satellite (Weightlessness)

\(g_{\text{eff}} = 0\). A pendulum cannot oscillate in orbit.

Second's Pendulum

A second's pendulum has a time period of exactly 2 seconds (one second for each half-swing). Its length:

\[T = 2 \text{ s} \implies l = \frac{gT^2}{4\pi^2} = \frac{g}{\pi^2} \approx 1 \text{ m}\]

Large-Angle Corrections

For large amplitudes \(\theta_0\), the small-angle approximation breaks down. The exact period is given by an elliptic integral, but to first correction:

\[T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}\left(1 + \frac{\theta_0^2}{16} + \cdots\right)\]

The period increases with amplitude — the pendulum is no longer isochronous. For \(\theta_0 = 15°\), the correction is less than 0.5%.
