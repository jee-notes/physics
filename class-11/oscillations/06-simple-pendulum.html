Simple Pendulum

Prototype Oscillator for Angular SHM.

Angular SHM

Unlike the spring-block system, whose motion is described by linear displacement, the motion of a simple pendulum is naturally described by its angular displacement about the point of suspension.

For small angular displacements, the angular position of the bob is given by

\[ \theta=\theta_0\sin(\omega t+\phi) \]

where

  • \(\theta_0\) is the angular amplitude.
  • \(\omega\) is the angular frequency of the SHM.
  • \(\phi\) is the initial phase.

Differentiating with respect to time gives the angular velocity of the pendulum,

\[ \omega'=\frac{d\theta}{dt}=\omega\theta_0\cos(\omega t+\phi) \]
Do not confuse the two symbols.
  • \(\omega\) is the angular frequency (or angular speed of the SHM). It is a constant that determines how rapidly the oscillation repeats.
  • \(\omega'=\dfrac{d\theta}{dt}\) is the angular velocity of the pendulum. It changes continuously during the motion and becomes zero at the extreme positions.
Although the same Greek letter is traditionally used for both quantities, they represent completely different physical ideas.

Setup and Restoring Torque

A simple pendulum consists of a point mass (bob) of mass \(m\) suspended by a light, inextensible string of length \(l\) from a fixed support. Its motion is described by the angular displacement \(\theta\) from the equilibrium position.

When the pendulum is displaced through an angle \(\theta\), gravity produces a restoring torque about the point of suspension:

\[ \tau=-mgl\sin\theta \]

The negative sign indicates that the torque is always directed towards the equilibrium position.

For small angular displacements,

\[ \sin\theta\approx\theta \]

Hence,

\[ \tau\approx-mgl\,\theta \]

Comparing with the standard form of angular SHM,

\[ \tau=-C\theta \]

we identify

\[ \boxed{C=mgl} \]

where \(C\) is the restoring torque constant (or simply the torque constant) of the oscillator.

Applying Newton's Second Law for rotational motion,

\[ I\alpha=\tau=-C\theta \]

gives

\[ \alpha=-\frac{C}{I}\theta \]

Comparing with the standard equation of Angular SHM,

\[ \alpha=-\omega^2\theta \]

we obtain the general result

\[ \boxed{\omega=\sqrt{\frac{C}{I}}} \]
The simple pendulum is the angular analogue of the spring-block oscillator.
  • Linear SHM: \(F=-kx\), where \(k\) is the restoring force constant.
  • Angular SHM: \(\tau=-C\theta\), where \(C\) is the restoring torque constant.
For a simple pendulum, \[ C=mgl \] while for other angular oscillators (such as a torsion pendulum), the value of \(C\) is different. The form of the SHM equation, however, remains the same.

Time Period

The time period of any angular SHM is

\[ T=\frac{2\pi}{\omega}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{C}} \]

For a simple pendulum,

\[ I=ml^2, \qquad C=mgl \]

Hence,

\[ T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{ml^2}{mgl}}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{l}{g}} \]
  • Independent of the mass of the bob.
  • Independent of the amplitude (for small angular displacements).
  • Depends only on the effective length and the gravitational acceleration.
The expression \[ T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{C}} \] is the rotational analogue of the spring-block formula \[ T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} \] For a simple pendulum, \[ I=ml^2,\qquad C=mgl, \] so the mass cancels automatically. Thus, the time period depends only on the geometry of the pendulum (its effective length) and the strength of gravity.

Effect on Time Period

The time period of a simple pendulum is

\[ T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{l}{g}} \]

This is one of the most widely used results in Oscillations. It is not only important in solving problems, but has also been used historically to determine the local value of gravitational acceleration.

From the expression for the time period, we immediately observe that only two quantities determine the oscillation:

  1. The effective length of the pendulum.
  2. The effective gravitational acceleration.

Therefore, any physical situation that changes either the effective length or the effective gravity will also change the time period.

Effective Length

The effective length of a pendulum is the distance from the point of suspension to the centre of mass of the bob. For a spherical bob of radius \(r\) suspended by a string of length \(L\),

\[ l_{\rm eff}=L+r \]

Always use the effective length while applying the time-period formula.

Effective Gravity

If the pendulum is observed from a non-inertial frame or additional body forces act on it, the bob experiences an effective gravitational acceleration \(g_{\rm eff}\). The time period is then obtained by replacing \(g\) with \(g_{\rm eff}\):

\[ T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{l}{g_{\rm eff}}} \]

In a Lift

  • Lift accelerating upward: \[ g_{\rm eff}=g+a \] so the time period decreases.
  • Lift accelerating downward: \[ g_{\rm eff}=g-a \] so the time period increases.
  • Free fall: \[ g_{\rm eff}=0 \] so no restoring torque acts and the pendulum cannot oscillate.

With Horizontal Acceleration

If the support accelerates horizontally with acceleration \(a\),

\[ g_{\rm eff}=\sqrt{g^2+a^2} \]

The equilibrium position is no longer vertical. Instead, the string makes an angle

\[ \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{a}{g}\right) \]

with the vertical, and the pendulum oscillates about this new equilibrium position.

In a Satellite

Inside an orbiting satellite,

\[ g_{\rm eff}=0 \]

Since there is no restoring torque, a simple pendulum cannot execute SHM.

The role of gravity is fundamentally different in the two prototype SHM systems.
For the spring-block oscillator, \[ \omega=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \] Gravity shifts the equilibrium position but does not affect the restoring force constant \(k\). Consequently, it does not affect the time period.
For the simple pendulum, \[ \omega=\sqrt{\frac{g}{l}} \] Gravity not only determines the equilibrium position but also provides the restoring torque.
Consequently, any change in the effective gravitational acceleration changes both the equilibrium position and the time period.

Second's Pendulum

A second's pendulum has a time period of exactly 2 seconds (one second for each half-swing). Its length:

\[ T = 2 \text{ s} \implies l = \frac{gT^2}{4\pi^2} = \frac{g}{\pi^2} \approx 1 \text{ m} \]

Large-Angle Corrections

For large amplitudes \(\theta_0\), the small-angle approximation breaks down. The exact period is given by an elliptic integral, but to first correction:

\[ T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}\left(1 + \frac{\theta_0^2}{16} + \cdots\right) \]

The period increases with amplitude — the pendulum is no longer isochronous. For \(\theta_0 = 15°\), the correction is less than 0.5%.
