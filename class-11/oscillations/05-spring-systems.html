Mass–Spring Systems

The prototype SHM system — horizontal and vertical springs, combinations, and effective spring constants.

Horizontal Spring

A block of mass \(m\) attached to a spring of constant \(k\) on a frictionless horizontal surface executes SHM. The equilibrium is at the natural length of the spring:

\[T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}, \qquad \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}\]

The time period depends only on \(m\) and \(k\) — not on the amplitude, the direction of displacement, or gravity.

Vertical Spring

A mass hung from a vertical spring stretches it by \(x_0 = \frac{mg}{k}\) to a new equilibrium. If displaced from this equilibrium and released, it oscillates with the same period as the horizontal case:

\[T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}\]

Gravity shifts the equilibrium position but does not affect the time period. The SHM occurs about the new (stretched) equilibrium, not the natural length.

To find the time period, measure displacement from the equilibrium position (where the spring force balances gravity). The resulting equation of motion is \(ma = -kx\) — identical to the horizontal case. Gravity drops out.

Springs in Combination

Springs in Parallel

Two springs (constants \(k_1, k_2\)) attached side-by-side to the same mass share the displacement equally. The effective spring constant is:

\[k_{\text{eff}} = k_1 + k_2\] \[T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k_1 + k_2}}\]

Springs in Series

Two springs connected end-to-end. The total extension is the sum of individual extensions under the same force:

\[\frac{1}{k_{\text{eff}}} = \frac{1}{k_1} + \frac{1}{k_2} \implies k_{\text{eff}} = \frac{k_1 k_2}{k_1 + k_2}\] \[T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m(k_1 + k_2)}{k_1 k_2}}\]

Two-Mass Spring System

Two blocks \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) connected by a spring on a frictionless surface oscillate relative to each other. The reduced mass governs the SHM:

\[\mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\] \[T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{k}}\]

The centre of mass of the system remains stationary (or moves uniformly), and both blocks oscillate about the COM.

Cutting a Spring

A uniform spring of constant \(k\) and natural length \(L\) is cut into two pieces of lengths \(L_1\) and \(L_2\). The spring constant is inversely proportional to length:

\[kL = k_1 L_1 = k_2 L_2\]

So cutting a spring into a fraction \(n/N\) of its length gives a piece with spring constant \(\frac{N}{n}k\). A shorter piece is stiffer.

This result follows from the fact that the spring constant of a section depends on the number of coils: fewer coils mean less stretch per unit force, hence a larger spring constant.
