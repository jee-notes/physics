Periodic and Oscillatory Motion

Distinguishing between periodic, oscillatory, and simple harmonic motion — the hierarchy of repetitive motions.

Periodic Motion

A motion is periodic if it repeats itself at regular intervals of time. The smallest time interval after which the motion repeats is called the time period \(T\).

  • The Earth's revolution around the Sun (\(T \approx 365.25\) days)
  • Rotation of the Earth about its axis (\(T = 24\) hours)
  • The motion of a planet in an elliptical orbit
  • Uniform circular motion

Not all periodic motions involve back-and-forth movement. A planet orbiting a star traces the same path periodically but does not oscillate.

Oscillatory Motion

Oscillatory motion (or vibratory motion) is a periodic to-and-fro motion about a fixed equilibrium position. The body moves in one direction, reaches a maximum displacement, reverses, passes through equilibrium, reaches the extreme on the other side, and returns — completing one cycle.

  • A pendulum swinging
  • A mass on a spring bouncing up and down
  • A tuning fork vibrating
  • The needle of a sewing machine
All oscillatory motions are periodic, but not all periodic motions are oscillatory. Uniform circular motion is periodic but not oscillatory — there is no equilibrium position about which the motion occurs.

Period, Frequency, and Angular Frequency

Three interrelated quantities describe the timing of any periodic motion:

  • Time period \(T\) — the time for one complete cycle (seconds).
  • Frequency \(f\) — the number of cycles per second: \(f = \frac{1}{T}\) (hertz, Hz).
  • Angular frequency \(\omega\) — the rate of change of phase: \(\omega = 2\pi f = \frac{2\pi}{T}\) (rad/s).

Equilibrium and Restoring Force

For oscillatory motion to occur, two conditions must be met:

  • A stable equilibrium position exists — a point where the net force on the body is zero, and small displacements lead to forces that push the body back toward this point.
  • The restoring force increases with displacement — the further the body moves from equilibrium, the stronger the force pushing it back.

If the restoring force is proportional to displacement, the resulting motion is the simplest kind: simple harmonic motion.

Examples of Non-SHM Oscillations

Not all oscillatory motions are simple harmonic. The restoring force may be nonlinear:

  • A ball bouncing between two walls (force is impulsive, not proportional to displacement)
  • A pendulum at large angles (\(\sin\theta \neq \theta\))
  • A particle in a potential \(U = \alpha x^4\) (restoring force \(\propto x^3\))

These oscillations are still periodic, but their time period depends on amplitude, and the motion is not sinusoidal.
