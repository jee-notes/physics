Oscillations

Simple harmonic oscillation, energy exchange, and the physics of vibrations.

Conceptual Overview

Vibrations are among the most widespread phenomena in physics. Tesla even said that all dynamics in universe is just energy and vibrations. Vibrations occur in mechanical systems, electrical circuits, molecules, atoms, electromagnetic fields, sound, light, and countless other physical systems. Despite their diversity, these systems share a common physical principle.

These systems possess a stable equilibrium. When such a system is disturbed, the disturbance stores energy in one or more conservative energy reservoirs. Depending on the system, this stored energy may be elastic, gravitational, electrical, magnetic or some other form of potential energy. This conservative energy reservoir is what manifests the tendency to restore the equilibrium state.

The stored energy cannot be released instantaneously. Instead, it is returned gradually to the system while simultaneously being converted into other form of energy (in mechanics - it is kinetic energy,in electromagnetism - it is magnetic field energy). During this finite-time exchange, the system continues to move because of its existing motion even if the disturbance energy in the reservoir is spent. Consequently, it overshoots the equilibrium position again causing disturbance in the opposite direction.

After the overshoot, the conservative reservoir is loaded in the opposite sense. It now drives the system back towards equilibrium, where the process repeats. Thus, the system undergoes a continuous exchange of energy between kinetic and potential forms, producing oscillatory motion.

The universality of oscillations therefore arises from two fundamental ingredients:

  • a conservative mechanism capable of storing and returning energy, and
  • a finite-time exchange of energy between the system and the conservative energy reservoir.

The simplest mathematical model of vibration is Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM). In SHM, the restoring tendency of the system is proportional to the effect.

\[ F=-kx \]

Applying Newton's second law gives

\[ m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}=-kx\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}=-\omega^2x \]

Although the physical origin of the restoring force may differ from one system to another, the governing differential equation is identical. Consequently, all such systems exhibit the same idealized motion—Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM).

Just as the first-order differential equation

\[ \frac{dy}{dx}\propto y \]

has exponential functions as its solutions, the second-order equation

\[ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2}\propto -y \]

has sinusoidal functions as its solutions. Thus, irrespective of whether the system is mechanical, electrical or microscopic, whenever its dynamics reduce to this equation, its motion is described by sine and cosine functions.

The Central Equation

The defining equation of SHM is the second-order differential equation:

\[ \frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -\omega^2 x \]

Its general solution is:

\[ x(t) = A\sin(\omega t + \phi) \]

Every SHM problem reduces to identifying the angular frequency \(\omega\) from the physics and then applying this solution. The angular frequency depends only on the system parameters (spring constant and mass, pendulum length and gravity, etc.) — never on amplitude.

Energy in Oscillations

SHM is a perpetual exchange between kinetic and potential energy. At the equilibrium position, all energy is kinetic; at the extreme positions, all energy is potential. The total mechanical energy remains constant:

\[ E = \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2 A^2 = \text{constant} \]

This energy is proportional to the square of the amplitude — doubling the amplitude quadruples the energy. The energy oscillates between kinetic and potential forms at twice the frequency of the displacement.

The question is what is oscillating? Entire motion is oscillating. Position, velocity (momentum), acceleration (Force), Kinetic Energy, Potential Energy - everything oscillates and this what we learn.

In dynamics, we solve problems using oscillation of E or F.

Beyond Ideal SHM

Real oscillators lose energy to their environment through friction and air resistance. This leads to damped oscillations, where the amplitude gradually decreases. If an external periodic force drives the system, forced oscillations result, and the phenomenon of resonance occurs when the driving frequency matches the natural frequency — the amplitude grows dramatically.
