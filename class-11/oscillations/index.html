Oscillations

Periodic motion, simple harmonic oscillations, energy exchange, and the physics of swinging and vibrating systems.

Conceptual Overview

Oscillatory motion is one of the most pervasive phenomena in physics. From the swinging of a pendulum to the vibration of atoms in a crystal, from alternating electrical circuits to gravitational waves, oscillation appears at every scale. This chapter develops the theory of the simplest and most important type — simple harmonic motion (SHM).

SHM arises whenever a system experiences a restoring force proportional to its displacement from equilibrium. The resulting motion is perfectly sinusoidal, fully characterised by just three quantities: amplitude, angular frequency, and initial phase.

The Central Equation

The defining equation of SHM is the second-order differential equation:

\[ \frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -\omega^2 x \]

Its general solution is:

\[ x(t) = A\sin(\omega t + \phi) \]

Every SHM problem reduces to identifying the angular frequency \(\omega\) from the physics and then applying this solution. The angular frequency depends only on the system parameters (spring constant and mass, pendulum length and gravity, etc.) — never on amplitude.

Energy in Oscillations

SHM is a perpetual exchange between kinetic and potential energy. At the equilibrium position, all energy is kinetic; at the extreme positions, all energy is potential. The total mechanical energy remains constant:

\[ E = \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2 A^2 = \text{constant} \]

This energy is proportional to the square of the amplitude — doubling the amplitude quadruples the energy. The energy oscillates between kinetic and potential forms at twice the frequency of the displacement.

Beyond Ideal SHM

Real oscillators lose energy to their environment through friction and air resistance. This leads to damped oscillations, where the amplitude gradually decreases. If an external periodic force drives the system, forced oscillations result, and the phenomenon of resonance occurs when the driving frequency matches the natural frequency — the amplitude grows dramatically.
