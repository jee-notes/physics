Damped Oscillations

Real oscillators lose energy — the amplitude decays, the frequency shifts, and eventually the motion dies out.

Damping Force

In real systems, friction, air resistance, or internal dissipation exert a resisting force. For a viscous (velocity-dependent) damping force:

\[F_{\text{damp}} = -bv = -b\frac{dx}{dt}\]

where \(b\) is the damping coefficient (units: kg/s or N·s/m). The equation of motion becomes:

\[m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + b\frac{dx}{dt} + kx = 0\]

Solution: Underdamped Case

For light damping (\(b < 2\sqrt{km}\)), the solution is:

\[x(t) = A_0 e^{-\gamma t}\sin(\omega' t + \phi)\]

where:

  • \(\gamma = \frac{b}{2m}\) — the damping rate (determines how fast amplitude decays)
  • \(\omega' = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2}\) — the damped angular frequency (slightly less than natural frequency \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}\))
  • \(A_0 e^{-\gamma t}\) — the exponentially decaying amplitude envelope
The oscillation frequency decreases slightly due to damping (\(\omega' < \omega_0\)), but for light damping this shift is negligible. The dramatic effect is on the amplitude, which decays exponentially.

Three Damping Regimes

  • Underdamped (\(b < 2\sqrt{km}\)): The body oscillates with decreasing amplitude. Most physical oscillators fall in this category.
  • Critically damped (\(b = 2\sqrt{km}\)): The body returns to equilibrium in the shortest time without oscillating. Used in door closers, shock absorbers, electrical meters.
  • Overdamped (\(b > 2\sqrt{km}\)): The body returns to equilibrium slowly, without oscillating. Sluggish response.

Energy Decay

Since energy is proportional to amplitude squared:

\[E(t) = E_0 e^{-2\gamma t} = E_0 e^{-bt/m}\]

Energy decays exponentially at twice the rate of amplitude decay. The rate of energy loss equals the power dissipated by the damping force.

Quality Factor

The quality factor \(Q\) measures how many oscillations the system undergoes before its energy drops significantly:

\[Q = 2\pi \times \frac{\text{energy stored}}{\text{energy lost per cycle}} = \frac{\omega_0}{2\gamma} = \frac{m\omega_0}{b}\]
  • High \(Q\) → lightly damped, many oscillations before dying out (e.g., tuning fork)
  • Low \(Q\) → heavily damped, few oscillations (e.g., oscillation in honey)

A tuning fork may have \(Q \sim 1000\); a guitar string \(Q \sim 500\); a pendulum clock \(Q \sim 100\).
