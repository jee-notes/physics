Physical and Torsional Pendulums

Extended bodies oscillating under gravity, and shafts oscillating under torsional restoring torques.

Physical (Compound) Pendulum

A physical pendulum is any rigid body free to oscillate about a horizontal axis that does not pass through its centre of mass. For small angular displacement \(\theta\):

\[\tau = -Mgd\sin\theta \approx -Mgd\theta\]

where \(d\) is the distance from the pivot to the centre of mass. Using \(\tau = I\alpha\):

\[I\alpha = -Mgd\theta \implies \alpha = -\frac{Mgd}{I}\theta\]

This is the SHM equation with:

\[\omega^2 = \frac{Mgd}{I}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{Mgd}}\]

where \(I\) is the moment of inertia about the pivot axis.

Equivalent Simple Pendulum Length

Using the parallel axis theorem (\(I = I_{\text{CM}} + Md^2 = Mk^2 + Md^2\)):

\[T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{k^2 + d^2}{gd}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{l_{\text{eq}}}{g}}\]

where \(l_{\text{eq}} = \frac{k^2 + d^2}{d} = d + \frac{k^2}{d}\) is the equivalent simple pendulum length and \(k\) is the radius of gyration about the CM.

Minimum Time Period

Since \(l_{\text{eq}} = d + \frac{k^2}{d}\), by AM–GM inequality:

\[l_{\text{eq}} \geq 2k\]

Equality holds when \(d = k\) — the pivot is at a distance equal to the radius of gyration from the CM. The minimum time period is:

\[T_{\min} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2k}{g}}\]
Placing the pivot very close to the CM (\(d \to 0\)) makes \(T \to \infty\) (the pendulum barely responds to gravity). Placing it far away (\(d \to \infty\)) also makes \(T\) large (high inertia). The optimal balance occurs at \(d = k\).

Common Physical Pendulums

Uniform Rod (pivoted at one end)

\[I = \frac{1}{3}ML^2, \quad d = \frac{L}{2}\] \[T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2L}{3g}}\]

Uniform Disc (pivoted at rim)

\[I = \frac{3}{2}MR^2, \quad d = R\] \[T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{3R}{2g}}\]

Uniform Ring (pivoted at rim)

\[I = 2MR^2, \quad d = R\] \[T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2R}{g}}\]

Torsional Pendulum

A body suspended by a wire that provides a restoring torque proportional to the angle of twist:

\[\tau = -\kappa\theta\]

where \(\kappa\) is the torsional constant of the wire (units: N·m/rad). The equation of motion is:

\[I\alpha = -\kappa\theta \implies T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{\kappa}}\]

This is the rotational analogue of the mass–spring system, with \(\kappa\) playing the role of \(k\) and \(I\) playing the role of \(m\).

The torsional pendulum is used in experiments to measure the moment of inertia of irregularly shaped bodies and in instruments like the Cavendish apparatus for measuring \(G\).
