Uniform Circular Motion

Uniform Circular Motion (UCM) is the motion of a particle along the circumference of a circle with constant speed.

Even though the speed remains constant, this motion is accelerated motion, i.e. \(\vec{a}\neq 0\).

UCM provides a simple setting to establish the relationships between linear and angular kinematic quantities.

It also introduces a special form of acceleration called centripetal acceleration, which changes only the direction of the velocity vector without changing its magnitude.

The direction of the velocity vector continuously rotates as the particle moves along the circle. The rate of change of this rotating velocity vector gives rise to centripetal acceleration.

Thus, UCM provides a useful model for understanding how the time-dependent change of a vector is mathematically described when its magnitude remains constant but its direction continuously rotates.

Relation Between Linear and Angular Variables

We first establish the relation between linear and angular variables. Here, linear does not mean one-dimensional motion. It refers to the actual motion observed in the two-dimensional plane.

The particle moves in the \(XY\) plane, whereas the angular displacement, angular velocity and angular acceleration are represented along the \(Z\)-axis.

The bridge between angular and linear variables is the radius vector \(\vec r\), which lies in the plane of the circle.

Angular Position

Let \(r,\theta\) be the polar coordinates and \(x,y\) be the Cartesian coordinates. Here, \(\theta\) is measured from the positive \(X\)-axis.

\[ x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta \]

The position vector is given by:

\[ \vec r=r\cos\theta\,\hat i+r\sin\theta\,\hat j \]

This representation is valid not only for circular motion, but for any motion in two dimensions.

In general 2D motion, \(r=f(t)\). In circular motion, however, \(r\) remains constant.

Angular Displacement

Here, we establish the relations for circular motion. For generic 2D motion, we will study the corresponding relations using the polar basis later.

We already know that angular displacement and angular velocity are related through:

\[ d\theta=\omega\,dt \]

We now want to establish the relationship between linear displacement \(d\vec r\) and angular displacement \(d\theta\).

For circular motion, we use the fact that \(r\) is constant and therefore:

\[ dr=0 \]

The linear displacement vector is:

\[ d\vec r=dx\,\hat i+dy\,\hat j \]

We need to find the relationships between \(dx,d\theta\) and \(dy,d\theta\).

Differentiating the Cartesian coordinate expressions, with \(r\) held constant, we obtain:

\[ \begin{aligned} x&=r\cos\theta\\ dx&=-r\sin\theta\,d\theta \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} y&=r\sin\theta\\ dy&=r\cos\theta\,d\theta \end{aligned} \]

Substituting these expressions into the linear displacement vector:

\[ \boxed{d\vec r=-r\sin\theta\,d\theta\,\hat i+r\cos\theta\,d\theta\,\hat j} \]

Thus, the linear displacement is perpendicular to the radius vector:

\[ \vec r\cdot d\vec r=0 \]

Its magnitude is:

\[ |d\vec r|=r|d\vec\theta| \]

Cross Product Relation

Angular and linear variables can also be related through vector cross-product relations.

For planar motion in the \(XY\) plane, the infinitesimal angular displacement is represented by an axial vector along the \(Z\)-axis:

\[ d\vec\theta=d\theta\,\hat k \]

The linear displacement lies in the \(XY\) plane. Using the cross product of the angular displacement vector with the radius vector, we obtain:

\[ \boxed{d\vec r=d\vec\theta\times\vec r} \]

Equivalently:

\[ \boxed{d\vec r=d\theta\,(\hat k\times\vec r)} \]

Angular Velocity

Angular velocity is directed along the angular displacement axis, whereas instantaneous linear velocity is tangential to the circular path and along the infinitesimal linear displacement.

We directly use the cross-product relation and differentiate with respect to time:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{d\vec r}{dt} &=\frac{d\theta}{dt} (\hat k\times\vec r)\\ \vec v&=\omega(\hat k\times\vec r) \end{aligned} \]

Since the angular velocity vector is \(\vec\omega=\omega\hat k\), we obtain:

\[ \boxed{\vec v=\vec\omega\times\vec r} \]

More generally, for a vector \(\vec a\) carried by a rotating frame with angular velocity \(\vec\omega\), and whose magnitude remains constant, its instantaneous rate of change due to rotation is:

\[ \boxed{\frac{d\vec a}{dt}=\vec\omega\times\vec a} \]

We will use this general relationship to understand the time-dependent behaviour of rotating vectors.

Centripetal Acceleration (\(a_c\))

Centripetal acceleration (also known as radial or normal acceleration) is the component of the instantaneous acceleration directed towards the centre of the circular path.

Its sole physical role is to continuously change the direction of the velocity vector while leaving its magnitude unchanged.

It is important to appreciate that a change in velocity does not necessarily imply a change in speed. Velocity is a vector quantity possessing both magnitude and direction. Consequently, changing either of these attributes produces an acceleration.

In circular motion, even if the particle moves with a constant speed, the direction of the velocity vector changes continuously as the particle progresses along the curved path. Therefore, the particle must possess a non-zero acceleration.

This component of acceleration responsible exclusively for changing the direction of motion is called the centripetal acceleration.

For a particle moving along a circular path of radius \(R\) with instantaneous speed \(v\), its magnitude is given by

\[ a_c =\frac{v^2}{R} = R\omega^2 = v\omega \]
  • \(v\) : Instantaneous linear speed.
  • \(\omega\) : Instantaneous angular velocity.
  • \(R\) : Radius of the circular path.

Units and Dimensions

  • SI Unit : metre per second squared (\(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\))
  • Dimensional Formula : \([M^0L^1T^{-2}]\)

Direction of Centripetal Acceleration

At every instant, the velocity vector is tangential to the circular path.

To continuously bend this tangential velocity and keep the particle constrained to the circle, the acceleration must always act towards the centre of curvature.

Consequently, centripetal acceleration is directed radially inward and is opposite to the outward radial unit vector.

\[ \vec a_c = -\omega^2\vec r \]

The negative sign indicates that the centripetal acceleration is antiparallel to the radius vector.

Centripetal acceleration is an absolute requirement for circular motion.
It exists in every circular motion, including Uniform Circular Motion (UCM), where the speed remains constant.
Its presence signifies that changing the direction of a velocity vector requires acceleration, even when the magnitude of the velocity remains unchanged.
Without centripetal acceleration, the particle would no longer follow a curved trajectory and would continue moving along the instantaneous tangent in accordance with inertia.
The particle may rotate clockwise or anticlockwise, but the centripetal acceleration is always directed towards the centre of the circular path.
