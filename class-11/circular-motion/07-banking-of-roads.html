Banking of Roads and Level Turns

How physical geometry and friction help vehicles navigate curves safely.

Friction

Before discussing banking of roads, it is useful to revisit an important aspect of friction.

For simple systems (point particles or bodies in translatory motion), such as a block sliding on a rough horizontal fixed surface, friction always acts opposite to the direction of velocity of the block.

However, friction always acts in a way so as to oppose the relative slipping between the contacting surfaces. Thus, behaviour of friction force is different for complex system of particles.

A complex system like a walking person or a car can manipulate the direction of the frictional force generated at the contact surface in order to achieve a desired state of motion.

For example, a person walking eastward who wishes to accelerate pushes the ground backward with greater force. The ground, in turn, exerts a static frictional force on the person directed eastward, thereby increasing the person's speed.

Similarly, a person moving eastward who wishes to slow down may alter the motion of their legs such that the static friction exerted by the ground acts westward, thereby reducing the speed of the person's centre of mass.

The same principle applies to automobiles. By appropriately controlling the rotation of the wheels, a car can utilize friction to accelerate, decelerate or negotiate curved paths. Thus, friction is a versatile interaction that allows complex bodies to control their state of motion.

By appropriately changing their gait, humans can use friction to accelerate, decelerate, turn and maintain balance.
Automobiles exploit the same principle. Through controlled wheel rotation, friction becomes a means of controlling motion rather than merely opposing it.

Negotiating a Turn

The study of negotiating a turn concerns the conditions under which a vehicle can safely execute a circular turn of a given radius.

The problem may be viewed as follows:

  • A vehicle, such as a car, is a complex system of particles capable of utilizing friction to control its state of motion.
  • The vehicle intends to negotiate a circular turn of radius \(R\).
  • The mass \(m\) of the vehicle and the acceleration due to gravity \(g\) are assumed to be constant.
  • The coefficient of static friction \(\mu\) between the tyres and the road surface is assumed to be constant.
  • Depending upon the geometry of the road and the available friction, there exists a range of permissible speeds within which the vehicle can safely negotiate the turn.
  • Of particular importance is the maximum permissible speed \(v_{max}\), beyond which the tyres lose their static grip and the vehicle skids.

Thus, the determination of permissible speeds for a vehicle negotiating a circular path of radius \(R\) is commonly referred to as the study of negotiating a turn.

Banking as an Engineering Solution

Banking the road is an engineering solution employed to increase the maximum permissible speed for a given coefficient of friction, thereby making curved roads safer and reducing the dependence on frictional forces.

Relying purely on friction is extremely dangerous, especially when the road is wet, icy, or oily, which drastically lowers \( \mu\).

Additionally, forcing the tires to supply massive lateral friction forces at high speeds causes severe wear and tear.

Banking is the engineering solution to this problem.

It involves raising the outer edge of the curved road relative to the inner edge by an angle \( \theta \). By doing this, the road surface is tilted inward.

Consequently, the Normal Reaction \( N \), which is always perpendicular to the surface, is tilted inward as well.

The primary purpose of banking is to utilize a horizontal component of the Normal Force (\( N \sin \theta \)) to provide the necessary centripetal force, reducing or entirely eliminating the reliance on friction.

The study of banked roads can be systematically understood by considering four cases of increasing generality.

  • Case 1 : A level road where friction alone provides the required centripetal force.
  • Case 2 : A smooth banked road where the entire centripetal force is supplied by the normal reaction.
  • Case 3 : A rough banked road where banking and friction together determine the maximum permissible speed.
  • Case 4 : A rough banked road where banking and friction together determine the minimum permissible speed.

Among these, Case 3 represents the most general situation. The simpler Cases 1 and 2 naturally emerge as special limiting cases of this analysis.

Negotiating a turn on a Level Road

When a car travels along a flat, level circular road of radius \( R \) at speed \( v \), it requires a centripetal force of magnitude \( mv^2/R \) directed toward the center of the turn. Since the road is perfectly horizontal, the Normal Reaction \( N \) is entirely vertical and merely balances gravity (\( N = mg \)).

The only available horizontal force is the static friction \( f_s \) between the tires and the road surface.

\[ f_s = \frac{mv^2}{R} \]

However, static friction has a strict upper limit defined by the coefficient of static friction \( \mu_s \) and the normal force:

\[ f_s \leq \mu_s N \implies f \leq \mu mg \]

To avoid skidding radially outward, the required centripetal force cannot exceed this maximum available static friction. Setting them equal gives the maximum safe speed on a level unbanked road:

\[ \frac{mv_{max}^2}{R} = \mu mg \implies v_{max} = \sqrt{\mu Rg} \]
If the driver exceeds \( v_{max} \), the tires lose their static grip, kinetic friction takes over (which is weaker), and the car skids off the road in a direction tangent to the curve.
Notice that the maximum safe speed is independent of the car's mass \( m \) — heavy trucks and light cars slide at the same speed under identical tire-asphalt conditions.
Thus, there is a single safe speed mentioned along the road on the signboard for all vehicles, irrespective of their mass.

Negotiating a turn on a Smooth Banked Road

Let the maximum speed at which a vehicle can negotiate a turn on smooth banked road be \( v_{max} \) . The angle of banking called as Banking Angle is \( \theta \) . At this speed, the horizontal component of the normal force provides the required centripetal force.

Let's resolve the forces on the vehicle along vertical and horizontal axes:

  • Vertical Axis (Equilibrium): The upward component of the normal force balances the vehicle's weight.
    \[ N \cos \theta = mg \]
  • Horizontal Axis (Centripetal Acceleration): The inward component of the normal force supplies the centripetal acceleration.
    \[ N \sin \theta = \frac{mv_{max}^2}{R} \]

By dividing the horizontal equation by the vertical equation, we eliminate the Normal force \( N \):

\[ \tan \theta = \frac{v_{max}^2}{Rg} \]

From this relation, the optimum (or ideal) speed is:

\[ v_{max} = \sqrt{Rg \tan \theta} \]
As \( N \cos \theta = mg \) , the normal force is constant as \(m\) , \(g\) , \(\theta\) are all constant (irrespective of any value of speed \(v\)) .
If \( v \gt v_{max} \), the normal component isn't enough for this radius; the car tends to drift outward (up the bank), so as to increase the radius of the turn.
If \( v \lt v_{max} \), the normal component is too strong for this radius; the car tends to drift inward (down the bank), so as to decrease the radius of the turn.

Negotiating a turn on a Rough Banked Road

We now consider the most general and practical scenario: calculating the maximum permissible speed \( v_{max} \) on a banked road where friction is present.

When a vehicle travels at a high speed, the required centripetal force \( (mv^2/R) \) is large. The horizontal component of the normal reaction alone \( (N \sin \theta) \) is insufficient to provide this force.

Consequently, the vehicle has a tendency to skid radially outward (up the incline). To oppose this relative slipping, static friction \( f_s \) acts inward (down the incline).

At the maximum safe speed \( v_{max} \), the static friction reaches its limiting maximum value, \( f_s = \mu N \). Let's resolve the forces on the vehicle along the vertical and horizontal axes:

  • Vertical Axis (Equilibrium): The upward vertical component of the normal force balances both the vehicle's weight and the downward vertical component of friction.
    \[ N \cos \theta = mg + f_s \sin \theta \implies N \cos \theta - \mu N \sin \theta = mg \]
    Factoring out \( N \), we get:
    \[ N (\cos \theta - \mu \sin \theta) = mg \]
  • Horizontal Axis (Centripetal Acceleration): Both the inward horizontal component of the normal force and the inward horizontal component of friction contribute to the required centripetal force.
    \[ N \sin \theta + f_s \cos \theta = \frac{mv_{max}^2}{R} \implies N \sin \theta + \mu N \cos \theta = \frac{mv_{max}^2}{R} \]
    Factoring out \( N \), we get:
    \[ N (\sin \theta + \mu \cos \theta) = \frac{mv_{max}^2}{R} \]

Since the normal force \( N \) is an unknown variable, we can eliminate it by dividing the horizontal equation by the vertical equation. Notice that the mass \( m \) also conveniently cancels out:

\[ \frac{v_{max}^2}{Rg} = \frac{\sin \theta + \mu \cos \theta}{\cos \theta - \mu \sin \theta} \]

To simplify this expression and reveal its relationship to the banking angle tangent, we divide the numerator and the denominator on the right side by \( \cos \theta \):

\[ \frac{v_{max}^2}{Rg} = \frac{\tan \theta + \mu}{1 - \mu \tan \theta} \]

Solving for \( v_{max} \), we obtain the expression for the maximum safe speed:

\[ v_{max} = \sqrt{Rg \left( \frac{\mu + \tan \theta}{1 - \mu \tan \theta} \right)} \]

Substitute \( \mu = \tan \phi \) , where \( \phi \) is angle of repose:

\[ v_{max} = \sqrt{Rg \tan ( \theta + \phi)} \]
This general formula beautifully encompasses our previous simpler cases as special limits:
1. Reduction to Case 1 (Level Road):
If the road is not banked, \( \theta = 0^\circ \). Consequently, \( \tan 0^\circ = 0 \).
Substituting this into our formula yields \( v_{max} = \sqrt{\tan{\phi} Rg} = \sqrt{\mu Rg} \), which exactly matches our result for a flat level road.

2. Reduction to Case 2 (Smooth Banked Road):
If the road is perfectly smooth, the coefficient of friction is zero (\( \mu = 0 \)).
Substituting this yields \( v_{max} = \sqrt{Rg \tan \theta} \), precisely matching our optimum speed derived in Case 2.

Negotiating a turn on a Rough Banked Road : Minimum Speed

We now consider the scenario where a vehicle is moving slowly on a steeply banked road.

For a given vehicle and road, parameters such as the mass \(m\), acceleration due to gravity \(g\), banking angle \(\theta\), and the coefficient of friction \(\mu\) are fixed constants. The only parameter the driver can actively control is the vehicle's speed \(v\).

When a vehicle travels at a low speed, the required centripetal force \( (mv^2/R) \) is small. The inward horizontal component of the normal reaction \( (N \sin \theta) \) might be significantly greater than the required centripetal force.

Consequently, the vehicle has a tendency to slide radially inward (down the incline). To oppose this relative slipping, static friction \( f_s \) acts outward (up the incline).

At the minimum safe speed \( v_{min} \), the static friction reaches its limiting maximum value, \( f_s = \mu N \), acting upwards along the bank. Let's resolve the forces on the vehicle:

  • Vertical Axis (Equilibrium): The upward vertical components of both the normal force and friction balance the vehicle's weight.
    \[ N \cos \theta + f_s \sin \theta = mg \implies N \cos \theta + \mu N \sin \theta = mg \]
    Factoring out \( N \), we get:
    \[ N (\cos \theta + \mu \sin \theta) = mg \]
  • Horizontal Axis (Centripetal Acceleration): The required centripetal force is provided by the inward horizontal component of the normal force minus the outward horizontal component of friction.
    \[ N \sin \theta - f_s \cos \theta = \frac{mv_{min}^2}{R} \implies N \sin \theta - \mu N \cos \theta = \frac{mv_{min}^2}{R} \]
    Factoring out \( N \), we get:
    \[ N (\sin \theta - \mu \cos \theta) = \frac{mv_{min}^2}{R} \]

Once again, we divide the horizontal equation by the vertical equation to eliminate the unknown normal force \( N \) (the mass \( m \) also cancels out, confirming the speed limit is independent of the vehicle's weight):

\[ \frac{v_{min}^2}{Rg} = \frac{\sin \theta - \mu \cos \theta}{\cos \theta + \mu \sin \theta} \]

Dividing the numerator and the denominator by \( \cos \theta \) reveals the tangent relationship:

\[ \frac{v_{min}^2}{Rg} = \frac{\tan \theta - \mu}{1 + \mu \tan \theta} \]

Solving for \( v_{min} \), we obtain the expression for the minimum safe speed:

\[ v_{min} = \sqrt{Rg \left( \frac{\tan \theta - \mu}{1 + \mu \tan \theta} \right)} \]

Substitute \( \mu = \tan \phi \), where \( \phi \) is the angle of repose:

\[ v_{min} = \sqrt{Rg \tan ( \theta - \phi)} \]
What if \( \mu \ge \tan \theta \)?
If the coefficient of friction is large enough (specifically, \( \mu \ge \tan \theta \) or \( \phi \ge \theta \)), the term inside the square root becomes zero or negative.
Physically, this means \( v_{min} = 0 \). The vehicle can be completely parked on the banked road without sliding down because friction is strong enough to hold it in place against gravity.

Side Note : Why is it called Banking?

This section is merely an intuitive side note and is not required for solving problems.

The word bank often conveys the idea of a raised boundary, an embankment, or a structure that guides and regulates movement within a prescribed region.

For example, if water is poured onto a perfectly flat surface, it tends to spread in all directions. However, if two raised boundaries are constructed, the water becomes confined within the channel formed between them and is guided along a preferred path.

Banking of roads employs a somewhat similar idea. By slightly raising the outer edge of a curved road, the geometry of the surface is modified so that vehicles can negotiate the turn more safely and efficiently.

Thinking of a "bank" as a mechanism for regulating flow provides an interesting way to connect seemingly unrelated ideas.
River banks guide the flow of water.
Embankments guide canals.
Cushions guide balls on a pool table.
Banked roads guide vehicles along curved paths.
The term "bank" is perhaps most commonly encountered today in the context of financial institutions.
Financial banks may also be viewed as human-made institutions designed to regulate, channel and stabilize the flow of money within an economy.
Viewed from this perspective, banking of roads and financial banks share a common underlying theme: introducing structure to guide and regulate flow.
