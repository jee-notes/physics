Centripetal and Tangential Acceleration

The two orthogonal components of acceleration in a non-linear path.

NLM Dynamics

So far, in Newton's Laws of Motion (NLM), our standard problem-solving strategy has relied heavily on identifying the direction of acceleration.

In linear dynamics, once the direction of acceleration is known, it is often convenient to choose one coordinate axis along the acceleration and the other perpendicular to it.

This choice is particularly useful because the acceleration has a non-zero component only along its own direction, while its component along the perpendicular direction is zero.

We can then resolve all external forces along these axes, applying \[ \sum F = ma \] along the direction of acceleration and \[ \sum F = 0 \] along the perpendicular direction.

Most of the situations encountered so far involve acceleration that is either constant or whose direction remains fixed. Although variable-magnitude acceleration has already been studied in one-dimensional motion, dealing with an acceleration vector whose direction continuously changes presents an additional difficulty.

Since forces are usually resolved along the direction of acceleration, a continuously changing acceleration implies that our preferred axes of resolution must also continuously rotate.

Circular motion provides a natural solution to this problem. The geometry of a circle suggests two mutually perpendicular directions at every instant: the radius and the tangent.

Consequently, the acceleration is conveniently resolved into a centripetal (radial) component and a tangential component. This decomposition allows us to retain our familiar NLM framework of resolving forces along known directions of acceleration and greatly simplifies the dynamics of circular motion.

Centripetal Acceleration (\(a_c\))

Centripetal acceleration (also known as radial or normal acceleration) is the component of the instantaneous acceleration directed towards the centre of the circular path.

Its sole physical role is to continuously change the direction of the velocity vector while leaving its magnitude unchanged.

It is important to appreciate that a change in velocity does not necessarily imply a change in speed. Velocity is a vector quantity possessing both magnitude and direction. Consequently, changing either of these attributes produces an acceleration.

In circular motion, even if the particle moves with a constant speed, the direction of the velocity vector changes continuously as the particle progresses along the curved path. Therefore, the particle must possess a non-zero acceleration.

This component of acceleration responsible exclusively for changing the direction of motion is called the centripetal acceleration.

For a particle moving along a circular path of radius \(R\) with instantaneous speed \(v\), its magnitude is given by

\[ a_c =\frac{v^2}{R} = R\omega^2 = v\omega \]
  • \(v\) : Instantaneous linear speed.
  • \(\omega\) : Instantaneous angular velocity.
  • \(R\) : Radius of the circular path.

Units and Dimensions

  • SI Unit : metre per second squared (\(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\))
  • Dimensional Formula : \([M^0L^1T^{-2}]\)

Direction of Centripetal Acceleration

At every instant, the velocity vector is tangential to the circular path.

To continuously bend this tangential velocity and keep the particle constrained to the circle, the acceleration must always act towards the centre of curvature.

Consequently, centripetal acceleration is directed radially inward and is opposite to the outward radial unit vector.

\[ \vec a_c = -\omega^2\vec r \]

The negative sign indicates that the centripetal acceleration is antiparallel to the radius vector.

Centripetal acceleration is an absolute requirement for circular motion.
It exists in every circular motion, including Uniform Circular Motion (UCM), where the speed remains constant.
Its presence signifies that changing the direction of a velocity vector requires acceleration, even when the magnitude of the velocity remains unchanged.
Without centripetal acceleration, the particle would no longer follow a curved trajectory and would continue moving along the instantaneous tangent in accordance with inertia.
The particle may rotate clockwise or anticlockwise, but the centripetal acceleration is always directed towards the centre of the circular path.

Tangential Acceleration (\(a_t\))

Tangential acceleration is the component of the instantaneous acceleration that acts exactly along the tangent to the circular path.

While centripetal acceleration is responsible for changing the direction of motion, the sole physical purpose of tangential acceleration is to change the magnitude of the velocity vector — that is, the speed of the particle.

If the speed of the particle is changing with time, a tangential component of acceleration must be present. Its magnitude is simply the rate of change of speed:

\[ a_t = \frac{dv}{dt} = R\alpha \]
  • \(v\) : Instantaneous linear speed.
  • \(\alpha\) : Instantaneous angular acceleration.
  • \(R\) : Radius of the circular path.

Units and Dimensions

  • SI Unit : metre per second squared (\(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\))
  • Dimensional Formula : \([M^0L^1T^{-2}]\)

Direction of Tangential Acceleration

Because it acts along the tangent, tangential acceleration is always collinear with the instantaneous velocity vector. However, its exact direction depends on whether the particle is speeding up or slowing down:

  • Speeding up: \(a_t\) is directed parallel to the velocity vector (it points in the same direction as the motion).
  • Slowing down: \(a_t\) is directed antiparallel to the velocity vector (it points opposite to the direction of motion, acting as deceleration).

Consider a ceiling fan that has just been switched on. At the exact instant \(t = 0\), the fan blades are at rest (\(\omega = 0\)). Since \(a_c = R\omega^2\), the centripetal acceleration is zero. However, to start rotating, the motor must provide an angular acceleration \(\alpha\), resulting in a non-zero tangential acceleration (\(a_t \neq 0\)).

It is the tangential acceleration that initiates the motion and builds up the speed. The moment the particle acquires any speed, centripetal acceleration immediately comes into existence to continuously bend that straight-line tendency into a circular path.

Can acceleration components be zero?

In Uniform Circular Motion (UCM), the speed \(v\) is strictly constant, meaning \( \frac{dv}{dt} = 0 \). Therefore, tangential acceleration can be zero indefinitely.
In contrast, centripetal acceleration (\(a_c = v^2/R\)) can never be zero for an extended period if the particle is to remain on a circular path. It can only be zero momentarily (for a single instant) if the particle temporarily comes to rest before reversing its direction along the circle.

Net Acceleration (\(a_{net}\))

In a general circular motion, a particle may simultaneously experience a change in the magnitude of its velocity as well as a change in its direction.

As discussed earlier, these two distinct aspects of the motion are associated with two mutually perpendicular components of acceleration:

  • Centripetal Acceleration (\(a_c\)) — Responsible solely for continuously changing the direction of the velocity vector.
  • Tangential Acceleration (\(a_t\)) — Responsible solely for changing the magnitude (speed) of the velocity vector.

The actual acceleration of the particle is therefore the combined effect of these two independent contributions.

At every point on a circular path, the radial direction and the tangential direction are perpendicular to each other. Consequently, centripetal acceleration and tangential acceleration are also mutually orthogonal vectors.

The net acceleration vector is simply obtained by vector addition:

\[ \vec{a}_{net} = \vec{a}_c + \vec{a}_t \]

Since these two components are always perpendicular, the magnitude of the resultant acceleration can be calculated using the Pythagorean theorem:

\[ a_{net} = \sqrt{a_c^2 + a_t^2} \]

Substituting the expressions for the two components gives

\[ a_{net} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{v^2}{R}\right)^2 + (R\alpha)^2} \]

Direction of Net Acceleration

The net acceleration vector generally lies somewhere between the inward radial direction and the tangent to the circular path.

Let \( \phi \) denote the angle made by the net acceleration vector with the inward radial direction.

Since \(a_c\) and \(a_t\) form the two perpendicular sides of a right triangle, the direction of the resultant acceleration is given by

\[ \tan \phi = \frac{a_t}{a_c} \]

The value of \( \phi \) depends upon the relative magnitudes of the two acceleration components.

  • If \(a_t = 0\), then \(\phi = 0^\circ\), and the acceleration is purely centripetal. This corresponds to Uniform Circular Motion (UCM).
  • If \(a_c = 0\), then \(\phi = 90^\circ\), and the acceleration is purely tangential. This may occur momentarily when a particle starts from rest.
  • If both \(a_c\) and \(a_t\) are non-zero, the acceleration vector lies somewhere between the radial and tangential directions.
Circular motion can be viewed as the simultaneous execution of two independent tasks:
One component of acceleration continuously bends the trajectory, while the other speeds up or slows down the particle.
The net acceleration simply represents the combined effort required to accomplish both tasks simultaneously.
