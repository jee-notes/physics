Circular Kinematics: Angular Variables

Defining the geometry and motion parameters of rotational paths.

Circular Kinematics

In kinematics, we study how to mathematically describe the parameters of motion, primarily position, velocity and acceleration.

Alongside these, we study related quantities such as displacement, distance, speed, average values and graphical representations of motion.

Many of the kinematic concepts studied in the chapter "Motion in 1D" will be revisited here, but using a different mathematical framework.

We have already studied that, in 2D motion, we employ two Cartesian coordinates, such as \((x,y)\), to describe the position of a particle. We used this for 2D projectile motion with constant acceleration.

However, 2D planar motion can also be described using an alternative framework based on a radial coordinate and an angular coordinate.

In planar motion, Cartesian coordinates \((x,y)\) represent two independent perpendicular coordinate distances, whereas polar coordinates \((r,\theta)\) represent one length and one angle.

A length is defined between two points. Therefore, to specify a radial length, we need a reference point from which it is measured. This point is the origin in polar coordinate system.

Circular motion is the simplest case of 2D polar motion because the radial coordinate remains constant. Consequently, the entire 2D motion can be described using a single angular coordinate. This makes circular motion a natural chapter for learning angular kinematics.

Thus, instead of using Cartesian coordinates \((x,y)\) to describe position, we use polar coordinates \((r,\theta)\).

The coordinate \(r\) is called the radial coordinate or radial position.

The coordinate \(\theta\) is called the angular coordinate or angular position.

In circular motion, the radial coordinate remains fixed. Therefore, the kinematics can be described entirely through the changing angular coordinate or angular position.

The study of motion using these angular quantities is called angular kinematics.

Angular Position (\(\theta\))

Angular position has no meaningful role in one-dimensional motion because the position vector can only be in angular positions of \(0^\circ\) or \(180^\circ\). This directional information is already encoded in the sign convention adopted for the cartesian coordinate.

Definition of position always requires a reference. Angular position requires a reference origin and a reference ray for full definition.

Angular position is measured from the chosen reference ray according to a specified sense of rotation (usually anticlockwise taken as positive).

To facilitate comparison between Cartesian and angular descriptions, the positive \(x\)-axis is commonly chosen as the reference ray, although any fixed ray in the plane may be used.

Angular Position as a kinematic variable is not same as the geometric orientation of the position vector.

While the geometric orientation of a ray is defined only modulo \(2\pi\), the angular position of a particle performing circular motion also keeps track of the number of revolutions completed.

Thus, a particle completing two full revolutions has an angular position differing by \(4\pi\) from its initial position, even though the radius vector has returned to its original orientation.

Angular Displacement (\(\Delta\theta\))

Just as displacement is defined as the change in position, angular displacement is defined as the change in angular position (\(\theta\)).

Angular displacement is the angle swept out by the radius vector of a particle about a specified centre or axis of rotation.

Thus, an important insight is that the angular displacement is not that of the particle but actually it is the angular displacement of its radius vector.

Let the radius vector sweep an infinitesimal angle (\(d\theta\)) in time (\(dt\)), while the particle undergoes an infinitesimal displacement (\(ds\)) along the circumference of a circle of radius \(R\). We have:

\[ d\theta=\frac{ds}{R} \]

Here, \(ds\) represents the infinitesimal arc length. It is important to distinguish this from the magnitude of the displacement vector.

For a finite motion, the arc length and the magnitude of the net displacement are generally different. Consider a particle moving through a quarter circle. Its angular displacement is:

\[ \Delta\theta=\frac{\pi}{2} \]

The arc length traversed is:

\[ s_{\text{arc}}=\frac{\pi R}{2} \]

However, the magnitude of the net displacement is the length of the chord joining the initial and final positions:

\[ |\Delta\vec r|=\sqrt{2}R \]

Thus, we must distinguish between arc length traversed and the magnitude of the net displacement. The relation between angular displacement and arc length remains valid for finite motion along a circle:

\[ \boxed{\Delta\theta=\frac{s_{\text{arc}}}{R}} \]

As discussed earlier, angular kinematics in this chapter deals exclusively with planar (2D) motion. As a particle moves along a circular path, its radius vector continuously changes its orientation and sweeps out an angle.

The term angle swept is important because angular displacement behaves as an accumulated measure of rotation.

It is not reset after \(2\pi\). For example, a particle completing two full revolutions undergoes an angular displacement of \(4\pi\), even though its radius vector has returned to its initial geometric orientation.

Units and Dimensions

Angular displacement is a dimensionless quantity because it is defined as the ratio of two lengths.

Angular displacement is treated as a vector quantity in planar circular motion. Consequently, it may be regarded as a dimensionless axial vector.

  • SI Unit: radian (rad)
  • Dimensional Formula: \([M^0L^0T^0]\)

Direction of Angular Displacement

Since angular displacement is treated as a vector quantity, it also possesses a direction.

As the motion is planar, angular displacement is associated with an axis perpendicular to the plane containing the motion.

Its direction is assigned using the Right-Hand Thumb Rule. Curl the fingers of your right hand in the sense of rotation of the radius vector. The extended thumb then points in the positive direction of the angular displacement vector.

For example, if a particle rotates anticlockwise in the \(xy\)-plane, the angular displacement is directed along the positive \(z\)-axis. Conversely, clockwise rotation corresponds to an angular displacement directed along the negative \(z\)-axis.

\[ d\vec{\theta} = d \theta \, \hat{k} \]
The primary purpose of assigning a direction to angular displacement is to distinguish between the two possible senses of rotation in a plane.
For infinitesimally small rotations (\(d\theta\)), angular displacement can be represented by an axial vector perpendicular to the plane of motion.
For planar rotations about the same fixed axis, successive angular displacements add algebraically.
However, finite rotations about different axes in 3D do not obey the usual laws of vector addition.
Such subtleties lie beyond the scope of exam.

Angular Velocity (\(\omega\))

Just as linear velocity describes how displacement is distributed over the time domain, angular velocity describes how angular displacement is distributed over the time domain.

Angular velocity is defined as the rate of change of angular displacement with respect to time. It specifies how rapidly a particle rotates or revolves about a given centre or axis of rotation.

\[ \omega = \lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0} \frac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta t} = \frac{d\theta}{dt} \]
  • \(\omega\) : Instantaneous angular velocity
  • \(d\theta\) : Infinitesimal angular displacement
  • \(dt\) : Infinitesimal time interval

Units and Dimensions

Angular velocity has dimensions of inverse time and is a vector quantity.

  • SI Unit: radian per second (rad/s)
  • Dimensional Formula: \([M^0L^0T^{-1}]\)

Direction of Angular Velocity

Unlike finite angular displacement, angular velocity is a true axial vector. Its direction is perpendicular to the plane of rotation and is determined using the Right-Hand Thumb Rule.

Curl the fingers of your right hand in the sense of rotation. The extended thumb then points in the direction of the angular velocity vector.

Thus, for anticlockwise motion in the \(xy\)-plane, the angular velocity vector points along the positive \(z\)-axis, whereas clockwise motion corresponds to a vector directed along the negative \(z\)-axis.

Angular Acceleration (\(\alpha\))

Angular acceleration is the rate of change of angular velocity with respect to time. It occurs when the rotation of the object speeds up or slows down.

\[ \alpha = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta \omega}{\Delta t} = \frac{d\omega}{dt} = \frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} \]
  • \( \alpha \): Instantaneous angular acceleration.
  • \( d\omega \): Change in angular velocity.

Units and Dimensions

  • SI Unit: radian per second squared (rad/s²)
  • Dimensional Formula: \([M^0L^0T^{-2}]\)
Angular acceleration is an axial vector.
As we are confining motion in a single plane, the axis of angular acceleration and angular velocity is same.
As a result, angular acceleration and angular velocity behave like 1D motion in circular motion.
The angular acceleration can only speed up or speed down angular velocity - i.e. change its magnitude only.
If the angular speed is increasing, \( \vec{\alpha} \) is in the same direction as \( \vec{\omega} \).
If the angular speed is decreasing (deceleration), \( \vec{\alpha} \) is opposite to \( \vec{\omega} \).
