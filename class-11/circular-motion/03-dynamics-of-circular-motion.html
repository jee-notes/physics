Dynamics and Centripetal Force

The real, physical forces that maintain curved trajectories.

1. What is Centripetal Force?

From kinematics, we know that any object moving in a circle of radius \( R \) with speed \( v \) must experience a centripetal acceleration \( a_c = v^2/R \) directed toward the center. According to Newton's Second Law (\( F_{net} = ma \)), this acceleration must be caused by a net force directed toward the center. This net radial force is called the Centripetal Force.

\[ F_c = m a_c = \frac{mv^2}{R} = mR\omega^2 \]
  • \( F_c \): Centripetal force.
  • \( m \): Mass of the particle.
  • \( v \): Linear speed of the particle.
  • \( R \): Radius of the circular path.
SI Unit: Newton (N) or \(\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2\)
Dimensional Formula: \([M L T^{-2}]\)
Crucial Note: Centripetal force is not a new, distinct fundamental force of nature (like gravity or electromagnetism). Instead, it is a role played by one or more physical forces. When solving physics problems, you should never draw a separate "centripetal force" vector on a free-body diagram. You simply identify the real forces (tension, normal, friction, gravity) and find their net component pointing toward the center.

2. Real-World Agents

To maintain circular motion, different physical situations utilize different "agents" to provide the necessary centripetal force:

  • Tension (\(T\)): A stone tied to a string and whirled in a horizontal circle on a frictionless table. The string's tension provides the inward pull.
  • Static Friction (\(f_s\)): A car taking a turn on a flat, unbanked road. The friction between the tires and the asphalt pushes the car toward the center of the curve.
  • Gravity (\(F_g\)): The moon orbiting the Earth, or satellites in geostationary orbit. The gravitational attraction acts as the centripetal force.
  • Normal Force (\(N\)): A motorcycle rider performing stunts on the walls of a 'Death Well' (Rotor ride). The wall pushes inward on the rider.
  • Magnetic Force (\(F_B\)): A charged particle entering a uniform magnetic field perpendicularly experiences a Lorentz force that forces it into a circular path.

3. Horizontal Circular Motion

Often, circular motion occurs in a horizontal plane while being influenced by vertical forces like gravity. A classic example is the Conical Pendulum.

The Conical Pendulum

Consider a bob of mass \( m \) tied to a string of length \( L \), whirling in a horizontal circle of radius \( R \). The string sweeps out a cone, making an angle \( \theta \) with the vertical. Let \( T \) be the tension in the string.

We resolve the tension \( T \) into two orthogonal components:

  1. A vertical component \( T \cos \theta \) which balances the gravitational force (weight).
  2. A horizontal component \( T \sin \theta \) which points toward the center of the circle and provides the required centripetal force.
\[ T \cos \theta = mg \] \[ T \sin \theta = \frac{mv^2}{R} \]

Dividing the second equation by the first equation eliminates the tension \( T \):

\[ \tan \theta = \frac{v^2}{Rg} \]

From this, we can determine the necessary speed \( v \) for a given angle \( \theta \):

\[ v = \sqrt{Rg \tan \theta} \]

Since the radius \( R \) is related to the string length \( L \) by geometry (\( R = L \sin \theta \)), we can also find the time period \( P \) of one complete revolution (\( P = 2\pi R / v \)):

\[ P = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{L \cos \theta}{g}} \]
In horizontal circular motion setups, gravity is completely balanced by the vertical component of a force (like tension or normal reaction), while a different, un-cancelled horizontal component acts as the sole provider of the inward centripetal pull.
