Circular Motion

Studying objects traveling along curved paths and the forces that bind them.

Conceptual Overview

Circular motion is the motion of a particle (point mass) along the circumference of a circle.

It is another category of motion that we study alongside motion along a straight line and general motion in a plane. The central idea behind these classifications is that different types of motion can be described using different mathematical tools.

By a circle, we mean a path of fixed radius lying entirely in a single plane. Thus, circular motion does not include spiral motion or a folded circle, where two connected circular arcs lie in different planes. Although the path remains connected, it does not form a single circular path in one plane.

Circular motion does include motion along an arc of a circle; a complete revolution is not necessary. The mathematical relations developed in this chapter apply to circular arcs as well.

We study circular motion for a special reason. A vector has two inherent characteristics: magnitude and direction.

Taking the centre of the circle as the origin, the radius vector serves as the position vector of the particle. Its magnitude remains fixed due to the circular geometry. As the particle moves along the circle, only the direction of the position vector changes.

This makes circular motion particularly interesting from a mathematical perspective:

  • We study special cases of motion to understand their mathematical relationships and generalise them wherever possible.
  • We introduce the polar coordinate system, which reduces the description of circular motion in a 2D plane to one independent coordinate, the angular position, because the radial distance remains fixed.

Thus, the special mathematical tools used to describe position, velocity, acceleration and other quantities make circular motion a distinct and useful model of motion.

Circular motion is one of the simplest examples of curvilinear motion, i.e., motion along a curved path.

In rectilinear motion, a particle moving with constant speed has zero acceleration because its velocity remains unchanged.

In circular motion, however, even if the speed remains constant, the velocity vector continuously changes its direction as the particle moves along the circle. This continuous change in velocity requires an inward acceleration directed towards the centre of the circular path.

Kinematics

Circular motion kinematics uses a special mathematical description to simplify the description of 2D motion in a plane to a single independent coordinate.

In Cartesian kinematics, we use two coordinates to describe 2D motion: \(x\) and \(y\). Both coordinates need to be tracked with time:

\[ x=f(t)\qquad y=g(t) \]

In circular motion, however, we need to track only one independent variable with time: the angular position.

\[ \theta=f(t) \]

This is because the radius remains fixed. The Cartesian coordinates can be expressed as standard geometric functions of the angular position and therefore need not be tracked independently.

The reason this works is because, any vector physical quantity has two inherent characteristics: magnitude and direction. The magnitude of the position vector corresponds to its length, while its direction in a plane is specified by its angular orientation.

In circular motion, the length of the position vector remains fixed due to the circular geometry. Consequently, only its direction changes. The change in direction of any vector is measured by its rotation.

We will expand this idea further in the Rotation chapter, where we study extended bodies. A ceiling fan in operation is an example of an extended body comprising a system of particles. The centre of the fan remains at rest, and every point on its blades moves along a circular path. Describing the motion of each particle separately using Cartesian coordinates quickly becomes cumbersome.

Since all particles rotate about a common axis, it is more convenient to characterize their motion using angular quantities.

The primary motivation for introducing angular quantities is the ability to describe the motion of extended bodies in a compact manner.
For a rotating ceiling fan, different particles on the blades travel through different linear displacements and possess different linear velocities.
However, all particles complete the same angular displacement in the same interval of time.
Thus, a single angular coordinate is sufficient to describe the rotational state of the entire body.

Linear and Angular

Consider a particle moving along a circle of radius \(r\). Taking the centre of the circle as the origin, its Cartesian coordinates can be expressed in terms of its angular position:

\[ x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta \]

Notice that the Cartesian coordinates are now related to a single angular coordinate through the geometry of the circle. Once we know the angular position \(\theta=f(t)\), we can determine the corresponding Cartesian coordinates.

This brings us to the most important feature of circular motion: its geometry establishes definite relationships between linear and angular quantities. These are geometrical relations, and they hold universally for any circle.

For example, the arc length travelled along a circle is geometrically related to its angular displacement:

\[ s=r\theta \]

Differentiating with respect to time gives the relationship between linear and angular velocity:

\[ v=r\omega \]

Similarly, the relationship between tangential and angular acceleration is:

\[ a_t=r\alpha \]

Thus, the radius \(r\) acts as the link between linear and angular quantities. It is these relationships that allow us to describe linear displacement, velocity and acceleration using their angular counterparts.

The defining feature of circular motion is precisely these geometrical (mathematical) relations. These relations enable us to isolate (\(\theta=f(t)\)) and geometry takes care of everything else.

Therefore, the special mathematical treatment of circular motion is built upon the geometry of a circle and the relationships it establishes between linear and angular quantities.

Resolving Acceleration

A key idea in circular motion is the decomposition of acceleration into two mutually perpendicular components: centripetal acceleration and tangential acceleration.

In circular motion, the velocity vector continuously changes because its direction changes as the particle moves along the circular path. Consequently, the particle possesses acceleration even when its speed remains constant.

However, a change in velocity does not necessarily imply a change in speed. For example, when a ceiling fan is switched on, its speed initially increases. After some time, the fan attains a constant speed, even though the direction of motion of every point on its blades continues to change.

It is therefore useful to resolve acceleration according to the two distinct roles played by its components in changing the velocity vector.

  • Centripetal Acceleration — Responsible solely for changing the direction of the velocity vector. It is always directed towards the centre of the circular path.
  • Tangential Acceleration — Responsible solely for changing the magnitude of the velocity vector, i.e., the speed of the particle. It acts along the tangent to the circular path.

This decomposition provides a clear physical interpretation of acceleration in circular motion and serves as the foundation for understanding both its kinematics and dynamics.

Centripetal force is not a new type of force.
It is the name given to the net inward (radial) force required to continuously change the direction of motion.
Whenever we encounter circular motion, the first step in solving a problem is to identify the actual physical interaction — such as tension, gravity, friction or normal reaction — that provides this inward force.

Dynamics

The main reason for decomposing acceleration into centripetal and tangential components becomes apparent when we study the dynamics of circular motion.

In dynamics, we study how different forces contribute to providing these two components of acceleration in different physical configurations.

For example, we know that normal reaction and friction are perpendicular to each other, just as centripetal and tangential accelerations are mutually perpendicular. Depending on the configuration, normal reaction may provide the centripetal acceleration while friction provides the tangential acceleration. In other configurations, their roles may be reversed.

Thus, resolving acceleration into these two components helps us identify and analyse the actual forces responsible for the motion.
