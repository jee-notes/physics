Circular Kinematics: Angular Variables

Defining the geometry and motion parameters of rotational paths.

Circular Kinematics

Kinematics is concerned with mathematically describing the state of motion of a point object. This involves defining quantities such as position, displacement, velocity and acceleration and studying how they evolve with time.

Dynamics, on the other hand, seeks to explain why the particle possesses a particular acceleration by investigating the physical interactions responsible for producing it. Thus, acceleration usually marks the end point of kinematics and the starting point of dynamics.

For one-dimensional motion, the position of a particle is conveniently specified by a single Cartesian coordinate.

In two-dimensional motion, we employ two Cartesian coordinates, such as \((x,y)\), to describe the position of the particle. We studied how to apply it in 2D projectile motion with constant acceleration.

However, angles are fundamentally geometric quantities defined in a plane.

Consequently, planar motion may also be described using an alternative framework based on a radial coordinate and an angular coordinate.

This leads to the development of angular kinematics, involving angular position, angular displacement, angular velocity and angular acceleration.

Circular motion represents the simplest and most important special case of such motions.

Since the particle remains at a fixed distance from a centre, the radial coordinate remains constant and the entire motion can be described using a single angular coordinate. Thus, circular motion provides the natural setting for introducing angular kinematics.

The ideas developed here later form the basis for polar coordinates, rigid body rotation and other rotational motions.

Angular Position (\(\theta\))

In one-dimensional motion, the position of a particle is conveniently specified by a single Cartesian coordinate \(x\). In higher dimensions, the position is usually represented by a position vector or an equivalent set of coordinates.

For planar (2D) motions, an alternative description is possible using a radial coordinate and an angular coordinate. The angular coordinate, called the angular position, specifies the orientation of the radius vector with respect to a chosen reference direction in the plane.

Unless specifically mentioned, angular kinematics in this chapter refers only to planar (2D) motion.

Angular position has no meaningful role in one-dimensional motion because the radius vector can only be aligned or anti-aligned with the chosen axis, corresponding to angular positions of \(0^\circ\) or \(180^\circ\). This directional information is already encoded in the sign convention adopted for the scalar coordinate.

Just as the definition of position requires a reference origin, the definition of angular position requires a reference origin and a reference ray.
The angular position is measured from the chosen reference ray according to a specified sense of rotation (usually anticlockwise taken as positive).
To facilitate comparison between Cartesian and angular descriptions, the positive \(x\)-axis is commonly chosen as the reference ray, although any fixed ray in the plane may be used.
It is important to distinguish between the geometric orientation of a radius vector and its angular position as a kinematic variable.
While the geometric orientation of a ray is defined only modulo \(2\pi\), the angular position of a particle performing circular motion also keeps track of the number of revolutions completed.
Thus, a particle completing two full revolutions has an angular position differing by \(4\pi\) from its initial position, even though the radius vector has returned to its original orientation.

Angular Displacement (\(\Delta\theta\))

Just as displacement is defined as the change in position, angular displacement is defined as the change in angular position.

Angular displacement is the angle swept out by the radius vector of a particle about a specified centre or axis of rotation.

As discussed earlier, angular kinematics in this chapter deals exclusively with planar (2D) motion. As a particle moves along a circular path, its radius vector continuously changes its orientation and sweeps out an angle.

The term angle swept is important because angular displacement behaves as an accumulated measure of rotation. Consequently, it is not reset after \(2\pi\). For example, a particle completing two full revolutions undergoes an angular displacement of \(4\pi\), even though its radius vector has returned to its initial geometric orientation.

For circular motion, the angular displacement is related to the arc length traversed by the particle through

\[ \Delta\theta=\frac{s}{R} \]
  • \(\Delta\theta\) : Angular displacement
  • \(s\) : Arc length traversed by the particle
  • \(R\) : Radius of the circular path

Units and Dimensions

Angular displacement is a dimensionless quantity because it is defined as the ratio of two lengths.

As per exam syllabus, angular displacement is treated as a vector quantity. Consequently, angular displacement may be regarded as a dimensionless vector quantity.

SI Unit: radian (rad)
Dimensional Formula: \([M^0L^0T^0]\)

Direction of Angular Displacement

Since angular displacement is treated as a vector quantity, it must also possess a direction.

For planar motion, angular displacement is conventionally associated with an axis perpendicular to the plane containing the motion.

Its direction is assigned using the Right-Hand Thumb Rule. Curl the fingers of your right hand in the sense of rotation of the radius vector. The extended thumb then points in the positive direction of the angular displacement vector.

For example, if a particle rotates anticlockwise in the \(xy\)-plane, the angular displacement is directed along the positive \(z\)-axis. Conversely, clockwise rotation corresponds to an angular displacement directed along the negative \(z\)-axis.

The primary purpose of assigning a direction to angular displacement is to distinguish between the two possible senses of rotation in a plane.
For infinitesimally small rotations (\(d\theta\)), angular displacement may be represented by an axial vector perpendicular to the plane of motion.
Finite angular displacements, however, do not obey the usual laws of vector addition and are therefore not true vectors in the strict mathematical sense.
Such subtleties lie beyond the scope of this chapter and may safely be ignored for exam.

Angular Velocity (\(\omega\))

Just as linear velocity describes how displacement is distributed over the time domain, angular velocity describes how angular displacement is distributed over the time domain.

Angular velocity is defined as the rate of change of angular displacement with respect to time. It specifies how rapidly a particle rotates or revolves about a given centre or axis of rotation.

\[ \omega = \lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0} \frac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta t} = \frac{d\theta}{dt} \]
  • \(\omega\) : Instantaneous angular velocity
  • \(d\theta\) : Infinitesimal angular displacement
  • \(dt\) : Infinitesimal time interval

Units and Dimensions

Angular velocity has dimensions of inverse time and is a vector quantity.

SI Unit: radian per second (rad/s)
Dimensional Formula: \([M^0L^0T^{-1}]\)

Direction of Angular Velocity

Unlike finite angular displacement, angular velocity is a true axial vector. Its direction is perpendicular to the plane of rotation and is determined using the Right-Hand Thumb Rule.

Curl the fingers of your right hand in the sense of rotation. The extended thumb then points in the direction of the angular velocity vector.

Thus, for anticlockwise motion in the \(xy\)-plane, the angular velocity vector points along the positive \(z\)-axis, whereas clockwise motion corresponds to a vector directed along the negative \(z\)-axis.

Relation to Linear Velocity

For a particle performing circular motion, the magnitude of the linear velocity is directly proportional to the angular velocity and the radius of the circular path.

\[ v = R\omega \]

In vector form, the linear velocity is related to angular velocity through

\[ \vec v = \vec\omega \times \vec r \]

Thus, the tangential velocity vector is always perpendicular to both the angular velocity vector and the radius vector.

Angular Acceleration (\(\alpha\))

Angular acceleration is the rate of change of angular velocity with respect to time. It occurs when the rotation of the object speeds up or slows down.

\[ \alpha = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta \omega}{\Delta t} = \frac{d\omega}{dt} = \frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} \]
  • \( \alpha \): Instantaneous angular acceleration.
  • \( d\omega \): Change in angular velocity.
SI Unit: radian per second squared (rad/s²)
Dimensional Formula: \([M^0 L^0 T^{-2}]\)
Angular acceleration is an axial vector. If the angular speed is increasing, \( \vec{\alpha} \) is in the same direction as \( \vec{\omega} \). If the angular speed is decreasing (deceleration), \( \vec{\alpha} \) is opposite to \( \vec{\omega} \).

Relation to Tangential Acceleration

The linear acceleration along the tangent to the circular path, which is responsible for changing the particle's speed, is called tangential acceleration (\( a_t \)).

\[ a_t = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d(R\omega)}{dt} = R\frac{d\omega}{dt} = R\alpha \]

In vector notation, the tangential acceleration is given by:

\[ \vec{a}_t = \vec{\alpha} \times \vec{r} \]
