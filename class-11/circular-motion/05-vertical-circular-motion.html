Vertical Circular Motion under Energy Dynamics

Vertical circular motion is a race between gravity and tension.

Physical Set-up

Consider a point mass (like a bob) of mass \( m \) tied to a massless, inextensible string of length \( R \). The other end of the string is fixed, and at the bottom-most point, the bob is given an initial horizontal velocity \( v_L \).

By assigning different values to the initial launch speed \( v_L \), we can study the entirely different trajectories the bob can take. The complete study of \(0 \lt v_L \lt \infty \) can be elegantly summarized by finding a few critical boundary values of \( v_L \).

However, before jumping into the mathematics, it is vital to understand the unique physical struggle occurring in this setup.

The Race Between Gravity and Tension
To understand the complexity of vertical circular motion, consider two extreme, isolated environments:
1. A Gravity-Free Environment: Here, tension does no work. It only provides centripetal acceleration, continuously changing the direction of the velocity vector while the speed remains perfectly constant.
2. A Tension-Free Environment: Here, gravity cannot alter the horizontal component of velocity. The bob simply acts as a projectile, following a parabolic path as gravity accelerates it downwards.
In vertical circular motion, both happen at once.
As the bob moves up, tension is desperately trying to steer the velocity vector along the circular curve. Simultaneously, gravity is doing negative work, relentlessly eating away at the bob's kinetic energy and converting it into potential energy.
If tension can successfully steer the velocity vector over the top before gravity depletes the kinetic energy, the circular path survives. But if gravity "wins the race" and eats up too much speed before tension completes the turn, the required centripetal force drops below the inward pull of gravity, the string goes slack, and the circular motion is destroyed!

Tension Analysis

Consider the particle at an angle \( \theta \) measured from the lowest point of the circle (the bottom).

The forces acting on the particle along the radial direction (along the string) are the tension \( T \) pulling inward and the radial component of gravity \( mg \cos \theta \) pulling outward.

According to Newton's Second Law, the net radial force must provide the necessary centripetal acceleration:

\[ T - mg \cos \theta = \frac{mv^2}{R} \implies T = mg \cos \theta + \frac{mv^2}{R} \]

The velocity \( v \) at any angle \( \theta \) can be found using the principle of conservation of mechanical energy.

If \( v_L \) is the speed at the bottom, the vertical height gained by the bob is \( h = R(1 - \cos \theta) \). Equating the loss in kinetic energy to the gain in potential energy gives:

\[ \frac{1}{2}mv_L^2 - \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = mgh \implies \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{1}{2}mv_L^2 - mgR(1 - \cos \theta) \] \[ \implies v^2 = v_L^2 - 2gR + 2gR \cos \theta \]

Extreme Points:

We use the general formula \( T = mg \cos \theta + \frac{mv^2}{R} \) , in which \( T \) is a function of \( \theta \) and \( v \) . Ultimately \( v \) too is a function of \( v_L \) and \( \theta \) as per \( v^2 = v_L^2 - 2gR + 2gR \cos \theta \)

  • At Bottom (\(\theta = 0^\circ, \cos \theta = 1\)): The tension is absolutely maximized because it must fight the full weight of the particle while simultaneously providing maximum centripetal force (since speed is highest).
    \[ T_{Bottom} = mg + \frac{mv_L^2}{R} \]
  • At Top (\(\theta = 180^\circ, \cos \theta = -1\)): The tension is minimized because gravity directly assists in providing the inward centripetal pull, and the speed is at its lowest.
    \[ T_{Top} = \frac{mv_{Top}^2}{R} - mg \]

The Four Dynamical Regions

Rather than merely memorizing the critical speeds \( \sqrt{2gR} \) and \( \sqrt{5gR} \), it is pedagogically much stronger to classify the motion by its qualitatively distinct dynamical behaviours. Depending on the initial launch speed \( u \), the particle will reach a maximum angle \( \theta \).

The entire motion can be divided into four distinct regions, each characterized by a unique struggle between tension and gravity.

Two Modes of Failure
Vertical circular motion may be viewed as a competition between two possible failures before reaching the top:
  • Failure 1 (Lower Half): The particle runs out of kinetic energy first (\(v = 0\)) while tension is still positive.
  • Failure 2 (Upper Half): The string runs out of tension first (\(T = 0\)) while the particle still has kinetic energy (\(v \gt 0\)).
The horizontal position (\(\theta = 90^\circ\)) acts as the exact boundary separating these two failure mechanisms, while the highest point (\(\theta = 180^\circ\)) marks the threshold for absolute success.

Region 1: The Lower Quadrant (\( 0^\circ \le \theta \lt 90^\circ \))

Condition: \( 0 \lt u \lt \sqrt{2gR} \)

If the launch speed is low, the particle oscillates like a simple pendulum in the lower half of the circle. Here, the particle always runs out of speed (\( v = 0 \)) before the string goes slack (\( T \gt 0 \)).

The Physical Struggle: Tension is actively converting the direction of initial velocity from horizontal to vertical.

By doing this, tension is essentially "supplying food" for gravity because gravity cannot impact horizontal velocity.

Gravity gladly eats this vertical velocity, converting kinetic energy into potential energy (a toll of \( mgR(1 - \cos \theta) \)).

The particle fails by running out of kinetic energy.

Region 2: The Horizontal Boundary (\( \theta = 90^\circ \))

Condition: \( u = \sqrt{2gR} \)

This is the exact boundary separating the two failure mechanisms. The particle reaches exactly the horizontal position.

At this precise instant, the velocity becomes zero (\( v = 0 \)) and the tension becomes zero (\( T = 0 \)) simultaneously.

It then falls back down cleanly along the same path.

Region 3: The Upper Quadrant (\( 90^\circ \lt \theta \lt 180^\circ \))

Condition: \( \sqrt{2gR} \lt u \lt \sqrt{5gR} \)

Once the particle crosses the horizontal plane, it possesses some non-zero horizontal velocity.

Gravity only acts vertically, so it cannot impact this horizontal velocity component.

The Physical Struggle: The roles reverse. Now, tension is desperately trying to "save" the remaining kinetic energy from being converted to potential energy by changing the direction of vertical velocity back into the horizontal direction.

However, if the initial speed isn't high enough, the required centripetal force drops below the inward component of gravity.

The tension drops to zero (\( T = 0 \)) while the particle still has speed (\( v \gt 0 \)).

The string goes slack, and the particle leaves the circular path, continuing as a free-fall parabolic projectile because only 2 forces are acting on particle and one of them became 0.

Region 4: The Full Loop (\( \theta \ge 180^\circ \))

Condition: \( u \ge \sqrt{5gR} \)

For the particle to successfully complete the full vertical circle, the string must never go slack. The most dangerous point is the absolute top (\( \theta = 180^\circ \)).

Setting \( T_{Top} \ge 0 \) gives the critical minimum speed at the top: \( v_{Top} = \sqrt{gR} \). Applying energy conservation between the bottom and the top (a height of \( 2R \)) yields the famous boundary condition:

\[ \frac{1}{2}mu^2 = \frac{1}{2}m(\sqrt{gR})^2 + mg(2R) \implies u = \sqrt{5gR} \]

If \( u \ge \sqrt{5gR} \), tension successfully steers the velocity vector fully horizontal at the top. Once the particle crosses \( 180^\circ \), gravity begins to assist the motion, and the particle completes the full circle!
