Differential Wave Equation & Kinematics

Mathematical formulation of one-dimensional traveling waves, wave equation derivation, and particle kinematics.

General Mathematical Form of a 1D Traveling Wave

Consider a localized disturbance or wave pulse propagating along a taut string along the \( +x \)-axis with a constant, non-dispersive speed \( v \).

Suppose at the initial instant \( t = 0 \), the shape of the string is given by some mathematical function:

\[ y(x, 0) = f(x) \]

As time progresses, the disturbance translates rigidly to the right by a distance \( \Delta x = v t \) in time \( t \) without changing its shape. Therefore, the displacement of a particle at position \( x \) at time \( t \) is identical to the displacement that the particle at position \( (x - vt) \) experienced at \( t = 0 \):

Traveling Wave Representation: \[ y(x, t) = f(x - vt) \quad \text{(Wave traveling in } +x \text{ direction)} \] \[ y(x, t) = g(x + vt) \quad \text{(Wave traveling in } -x \text{ direction)} \]

More generally, any single-valued function whose arguments combine in the linear form \( (kx - \omega t) \), \( (kx + \omega t) \), \( (t - x/v) \), or \( (ax \pm bt) \) represents a traveling wave, where the wave speed is given by the ratio of coefficients:

\[ v = \frac{\text{Coefficient of } t}{\text{Coefficient of } x} = \frac{\omega}{k} = \frac{b}{a} \]

Conditions for a Function to Represent a Traveling Wave

Not every mathematical function of the form \( f(ax \pm bt) \) represents a physically acceptable wave pulse. For a function to describe a real, physical wave on a string, it must satisfy three rigorous conditions:

  1. Single-Valued: At any point \( x \) and time \( t \), the displacement \( y(x,t) \) must have one and only one unique value (a piece of string cannot be in two places simultaneously).
  2. Finite / Bounded Everywhere: The displacement \( y(x,t) \) must remain finite for all \( x \in (-\infty, \infty) \) and all \( t \ge 0 \). It must not diverge to \( \pm \infty \).
  3. Continuous and Differentiable: The partial derivatives \( \frac{\partial y}{\partial x} \) (slope) and \( \frac{\partial y}{\partial t} \) (particle velocity) must exist, be continuous, and be finite at all points.

Example: Evaluating Valid Wave Functions

Case A: \( y(x,t) = \frac{4}{(2x - 3t)^2 + 5} \)

Analysis: The argument is of the form \( (ax - bt) \). The denominator is always \( \ge 5 > 0 \), so \( y(x,t) \) is single-valued, smooth, bounded (maximum value \( = 4/5 \)), and finite for all \( x \) and \( t \).
Conclusion: Valid traveling wave traveling along \( +x \) with speed \( v = 3/2 = 1.5 \text{ m/s} \).

Case B: \( y(x,t) = \ln(x - vt) \)

Analysis: For \( (x - vt) \le 0 \), the logarithm is undefined in real space, and as \( (x - vt) \to 0^+ \), \( y \to -\infty \).
Conclusion: Not a physically valid wave function over the full domain.

The Differential Wave Equation

The fundamental partial differential equation governing all classical non-dispersive wave phenomena in one dimension is known as the Linear Wave Equation.

Mathematical Derivation from Wave Profile

Let \( y(x,t) = f(u) \), where \( u = x - vt \). Applying the multivariable chain rule:

\[ \frac{\partial y}{\partial x} = f'(u)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = f'(u) \cdot 1 = f'(u) \] \[ \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} = f''(u) \cdot 1 = f''(u) \]

Now computing the time derivatives:

\[ \frac{\partial y}{\partial t} = f'(u)\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = f'(u) \cdot (-v) = -v f'(u) \] \[ \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = -v f''(u) \cdot (-v) = v^2 f''(u) \]

Substituting \( f''(u) = \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} \) into the second time derivative yields:

One-Dimensional Differential Wave Equation: \[ \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} = \frac{1}{v^2} \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} \]
Any physical disturbance \( y(x,t) \) that satisfies this linear second-order partial differential equation represents a valid wave propagating with speed \( v \). Because the equation is linear, it satisfies the Principle of Superposition: if \( y_1 \) and \( y_2 \) are solutions, then \( y = c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2 \) is also an exact solution.

Wave Velocity vs Particle Velocity & Acceleration

A critical source of conceptual confusion in wave mechanics is the distinction between the motion of the wave and the motion of individual medium particles:

Quantity Definition & Formula SI Unit & Dimensions Physical Meaning
Wave Velocity (\( v \)) \[ v = \frac{dx}{dt}\Big|_{\text{phase}} = \frac{\omega}{k} \] \( \text{m/s} \)
\( [M^0 L^1 T^{-1}] \) 		The constant speed at which the wave profile/energy travels along the string (\( x \)-axis).
Particle Velocity (\( v_p \)) \[ v_p = \frac{\partial y}{\partial t} \] \( \text{m/s} \)
\( [M^0 L^1 T^{-1}] \) 		The instantaneous transverse velocity of an individual string element moving along the \( y \)-axis.
Particle Acceleration (\( a_p \)) \[ a_p = \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} \] \( \text{m/s}^2 \)
\( [M^0 L^1 T^{-2}] \) 		The instantaneous transverse acceleration of the string element, directly caused by net tension forces.

Slope Relation for Particle Velocity: vp = -v (dy/dx)

There is a direct mathematical and physical connection between the transverse velocity of a particle and the geometric slope of the string at that point.

For a wave traveling in the positive \( x \)-direction (\( y(x,t) = f(x - vt) \)):

\[ \frac{\partial y}{\partial t} = -v f'(x - vt) \] \[ \frac{\partial y}{\partial x} = f'(x - vt) \]

Eliminating \( f'(x - vt) \) gives the fundamental kinematic slope relationship:

Particle Velocity – Slope Relationship: \[ v_p = -v \left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right) \quad \text{(For wave moving in } +x\text{)} \] \[ v_p = +v \left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right) \quad \text{(For wave moving in } -x\text{)} \]
Physical Rule of Thumb: For a wave moving to the right (\(+x\)):
  • If the string slope is negative (\(\partial y / \partial x < 0\)), the particle is moving upward (\(v_p > 0\)).
  • If the string slope is positive (\(\partial y / \partial x > 0\)), the particle is moving downward (\(v_p < 0\)).
  • At the crests and troughs, the slope is momentarily zero, meaning the particles are at their extreme positions with zero instantaneous velocity.
