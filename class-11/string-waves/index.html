Waves on a String

One-dimensional transverse mechanical wave propagation, energy transmission, and normal modes.

Conceptual Overview

Wave motion represents one of the most fundamental mechanisms in nature by which energy and momentum are transferred from one region of space to another without any net transport of matter. In ordinary particulate mechanics, moving matter transports kinetic energy and momentum. In wave mechanics, a local disturbance created in an elastic medium propagates through the collective, coupled oscillations of adjacent medium particles.

A taut, flexible string serves as the quintessential one-dimensional physical system for understanding mechanical waves. When an element of the string is displaced transversely from its equilibrium position, the tension in the string exerts a restoring force that pulls the element back toward equilibrium. Because the string possesses inertia (mass per unit length), the element overshoots its equilibrium position, initiating oscillations. Simultaneously, through intermolecular cohesive forces and tension, neighboring elements are set into motion with a systematic time delay, giving rise to a traveling transverse wave.

Particles of the medium oscillate locally about their fixed equilibrium positions; it is the physical state of disturbance and energy that travels globally along the medium.

Mechanical Wave Fundamentals

A mechanical wave strictly requires a material medium possessing two essential physical properties:

  • Elasticity (Restoring Tendency): Enables medium elements to exert forces on displaced neighbors, providing the restoring force necessary to pull particles back to equilibrium.
  • Inertia (Mass/Density): Allows moving medium elements to store kinetic energy and overshoot their equilibrium positions, sustaining continuous oscillations.

In waves on a string, the transverse restoring force is supplied by the tension \( T \) (elastic property), while the inertia is governed by the linear mass density \( \mu \) (inertial property).

Wave speed is purely an intrinsic property of the medium. Changing the frequency or amplitude of the source alters the wavelength, but does not affect the speed of wave propagation through a homogeneous medium.

Kinematics & Dynamics of String Waves

The mathematical formulation of a one-dimensional wave involves functions of two independent variables: position \( x \) and time \( t \). Any disturbance maintaining a constant profile while traveling at constant velocity \( v \) along the \( \pm x \)-direction satisfies the fundamental traveling wave condition \( y(x,t) = f(kx \mp \omega t) \).

Applying Newton's second law to an infinitesimal segment of a stretched string yields the classical 1D linear wave differential equation:

\[ \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} = \frac{1}{v^2} \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} \]

where the wave propagation speed is given by the celebrated relation:

\[ v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} \]

A crucial conceptual distinction in wave kinematics exists between the wave speed \( v \) (the constant rate at which the phase/disturbance moves along the string) and the particle velocity \( v_p = \frac{\partial y}{\partial t} \) (the time-varying transverse velocity of an individual medium particle).

Energy Transport & Power in Strings

As a wave propagates along a stretched string, medium elements continuously exchange kinetic energy (due to particle velocity) and potential energy (due to elastic stretching and tension).

Remarkably, for a progressive traveling wave, the kinetic energy density and potential energy density are exactly equal at every instant and at every point in space:

\[ u_k(x,t) = u_p(x,t) = \frac{1}{2}\mu \omega^2 A^2 \cos^2(kx - \omega t) \]

The total energy density is maximum at the equilibrium positions (where the slope and particle velocity are both maximal) and vanishes at the wave crests and troughs (where the particles momentarily come to rest and the string is unstretched locally). The average rate of energy transmission across any cross-section gives the average power:

\[ P_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{2} \mu v \omega^2 A^2 \]

Boundary Behavior, Interference & Normal Modes

When a traveling wave encounters a boundary or discontinuity, boundary conditions dictate how the incident wave is partitioned into reflected and transmitted components:

  • Rigid / Fixed End: Total displacement is clamped to zero, resulting in total reflection with an abrupt phase shift of \( \pi \) radians (pulse inversion).
  • Free / Open End: Transverse restoring force is zero, resulting in total reflection with zero phase shift (reflected without inversion).

When two identical harmonic waves travel in opposite directions along a string bounded by fixed boundaries, their linear superposition produces standing waves (stationary waves):

\[ y(x,t) = \left(2A \sin kx\right) \cos \omega t \]

Standing waves feature stationary points of permanent zero motion called nodes and points of maximum oscillatory amplitude called antinodes. Because no net energy crosses a node, energy remains trapped in discrete normal modes characterized by quantized resonant frequencies:

\[ f_n = \frac{n v}{2L} = \frac{n}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots \]
