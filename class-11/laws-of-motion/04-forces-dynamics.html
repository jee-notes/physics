Forces in Dynamics

Newton's Laws tell us how forces affect the motion of bodies.

The next step is to study the common forces that repeatedly appear in mechanics problems.

In this chapter, we shall develop the physical understanding, direction, point of application and mathematical models of these forces.

  • Weight (Gravitational Force)
  • Normal Force
  • Tension Force
  • Spring Force
  • Pseudo Force

Spring force requires an understanding of elasticity, while pseudo force arises only in non-inertial frames of reference. Therefore, these two forces are discussed in later chapters.

We first study the three forces that appear in almost every mechanics problem: weight, normal force and tension.

Weight

Weight is the gravitational force exerted on a body by the Earth (or any other astronomical body) near its surface.

It is a non-contact force arising from the gravitational interaction.

Every pair of massive bodies in the universe exerts gravitational force on each other. Consequently, every body is simultaneously attracted by the Earth, the Moon, the Sun and countless other astronomical bodies. However, when we speak of the weight of a body, we specifically refer to the gravitational force exerted by the dominant astronomical body. Near the Earth's surface, this dominant body is the Earth itself.

Near the Earth's surface, the Earth's gravitational field is approximately uniform. Consequently, the weight of a body is given by

\[ W = mg \]

where \(m\) is the mass of the body and \(g\) is the local acceleration due to gravity.

Since the Earth's gravitational field is approximately uniform over ordinary distances, the resultant weight acts through the centre of mass of the body and is directed towards the centre of the Earth. As the Earth's curvature is negligible over small regions, this direction is simply referred to as vertically downward.

In fact, the vertical direction is defined as the direction of the local gravitational field (weight). The horizontal direction is any direction perpendicular to the vertical.

Mass is an intrinsic property of a body, whereas weight depends on the gravitational field in which the body is placed.
Consequently, the mass of a body remains unchanged, but its weight varies from one astronomical body to another.

In everyday language, people often say, "My weight is 60 kg." Strictly speaking, 60 kg is the mass. The corresponding weight on the Earth's surface is approximately 60 kgf (kilogram-force) or about 600 N.

Normal

The normal force is a contact force exerted by a solid surface on a solid body in contact with it.

In mechanics, solid bodies are modelled as perfectly rigid bodies, while liquids and gases are modelled as fluids. To understand rigidity, compare a hard steel ball with wet clay. A steel ball does not appreciably deform under ordinary forces, whereas wet clay readily changes its shape.

Consequently, two rigid bodies cannot interpenetrate. The normal force arises to enforce this non-penetration constraint.

The word normal means perpendicular. Therefore, the normal force always acts along the common normal to the two surfaces at the point of contact.

The normal force always pushes the two contacting bodies away from each other. It can push, but it can never pull.

When a body is pressed against a surface, the atoms of the two bodies are brought extremely close together. The resulting electromagnetic interaction prevents the bodies from occupying the same space and gives rise to the normal force.

The magnitude of the normal force is not fixed. Instead, it automatically adjusts itself according to the mechanical interaction between the two bodies.

In mechanics problems, its magnitude is often determined using constraint conditions. From the viewpoint of constraints, the normal force adjusts itself so as to enforce the non-penetration constraint.

Suppose a body approaches a surface along the common normal. At the instant contact is established, the normal interaction acts impulsively to eliminate the approaching relative velocity. Thereafter, while contact is maintained, the normal force continuously adjusts itself so that the contacting points do not accelerate into each other along the common normal.

Thus, if \(u_A \neq u_B\) just before contact is established, the impulsive normal interaction acts so that \(v_A=v_B\). Once contact has been established, the continuous normal force acts so that \(a_A=a_B\). Here, \(A\) and \(B\) denote the contacting points on the two bodies, and all quantities are measured along the common normal.

For example, if you press a marble against a rigid wall, the wall first exerts an impulsive normal force to stop the marble from moving into it. As you continue to push, the wall continuously exerts the required normal force to oppose your push and prevent penetration.

The constraint condition associated with the normal force is often sufficient to solve mechanics problems.
Many problems can be solved directly from the constraint without explicitly calculating the magnitude of the normal force.

Tension

Tension is the contact force exerted by a taut string, rope or cable on the bodies attached to its ends.

In mechanics, strings and ropes are modelled as perfectly flexible and inextensible. They can bend freely but cannot stretch.

Consequently, the distance between the two ends of an ideal taut string along its length remains constant.

The tension force arises to enforce this inextensibility constraint.

Unlike the normal force, which acts along the common normal of two contacting surfaces, the tension force always acts along the length of the string.

The tension force always pulls the attached bodies towards the rope. It can pull, but it can never push.

The magnitude of the tension is not fixed. Instead, it automatically adjusts itself according to the mechanical interaction between the connected bodies.

In mechanics problems, the magnitude of tension is often determined using constraint conditions. From the viewpoint of constraints, the tension force adjusts itself so as to enforce the inextensibility constraint.

Suppose the two ends of a string initially move with different velocities along the string. As the string becomes taut, the tension interaction acts impulsively to establish the string constraint. Thereafter, while the string remains taut, the tension continuously adjusts itself so that the connected points satisfy the inextensibility condition.

Thus, if the two ends of the string have different displacements just before the string becomes taut, the impulsive tension interaction acts to establish the string constraint. Once the string is taut, the tension force acts so that the connected points have equal acceleration along the length of the string, thereby maintaining a constant string length.

For example, if two blocks are connected by a light inextensible string, pulling one block immediately causes the string to pull the other block. The tension continuously adjusts itself so that the length of the string remains unchanged.

The string constraint associated with tension is often sufficient to solve mechanics problems.
Many problems can be solved directly from the constraint without explicitly determining the magnitude of the tension.

Normal and Tension

Normal Force and Tension Force are common in many ways.

Both are macroscopic manifestations of inter-molecular forces.

Normal can only push and Tension can only pull. But inter-molecular forces sustain rigidity at both sides. They can push as well as pull.

Normal enforces the non-penetration (compression) constraint, while tension enforces the inextensibility (extension) constraint.

Both are such that their directions are determined by geometry. So, if geometry is fixed, their directions are fixed.

Their magnitudes is determined by the requirement to sustain the constraint.
