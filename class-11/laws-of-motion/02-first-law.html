Newton's First Law

Force, inertia and the principle that governs motion.

Newton's First Law

Newton's First Law of Motion states:
Every body continues in its state of rest or of uniform motion unless compelled to change that state by the action of a net external force.

When I read this statement for the first time, I was confused due to usage of formal language. Such language is used in physics to express ideas precisely and concisely, but a beginner needs to understand what each part of the statement means. Let us take it word-by-word.

  • Every body: Newton is referring to a physical body, that is, a piece of matter that possesses mass. Initially, we study bodies by modelling them as point particles, so that we can focus on their motion without considering their size or shape.
  • State of rest or of uniform motion: Newton is referring to the state of motion of a body. A body can be at rest or in uniform motion. In physics, uniform motion means motion with constant velocity, that is, with both constant magnitude and constant direction. Rest is the special case in which the velocity is zero.
  • Action of a net external force: Newton is referring to "net" forces exerted on the body. More than one force may act on a body simultaneously, so we must consider their combined effect. This is represented by the vector sum of the individual forces: \(\sum_i \vec{F}_i=\vec{F}_{\text{net}}\) . The word external becomes important when we study multi-particle bodies as systems, where we distinguish between forces exerted by bodies outside the system and forces acting between particles within the system.

The central idea of this law is that force is associated with a change in the state of motion of a body. For a single particle, we consider the net force acting on that particle. For a system of particles, the net external force determines the acceleration of the centre of mass and hence the change in its translational state of motion.

As already discussed, force represents a mechanical interaction capable of changing the motion of a body. Such a change may involve starting motion, stopping motion, speeding up, slowing down or changing the direction of motion.

In kinematics, we learned that the state of motion of a body is indicated by its velocity. Any change in velocity corresponds to a change in the body's state of motion. The rate of change of velocity is called acceleration. Thus, the effect of a net force on the motion of a body is associated with the production of acceleration.

When several forces act simultaneously on a body, their combined effect is represented by the net force. The net force may be zero even when individual forces are acting on the body, provided their vector sum is zero. Therefore, we must consider the resultant of all the forces rather than examine each force in isolation.

With this understanding, Newton's First Law has an important implication: if the net external force acting on a body is zero, the body's state of motion does not change. In other words, the body continues with the same velocity it already possesses.

\[ \vec{F}_{\text{net}}=0 \quad \implies \quad \vec{a}=0 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \vec{v}=\text{constant} \]

This means that a body initially at rest remains at rest, while a body already moving continues with constant velocity in a straight line, provided the net external force acting on it is zero.

The reverse inference is also used. If a body has constant velocity, its acceleration is zero and so, the net force acting on the body is zero.

However, Newton's First Law expresses the causal implication in one direction that zero net force implies zero acceleration.

It is this very causal relation that forms the central idea of Newton's First Law.

A natural question now arises: Why does a body continue in the same state of motion when no net force acts on it? Why does the absence of a net force mean that the existing state of motion persists?

Newton gave the concept of inertia as an inherent property of matter itself, that is responsible for this persistence in the state of motion.

For this reason, Newton's First Law is also known as the Law of Inertia.

Inertia

Newton's First Law describes something we already observe in our everyday experience. Suppose you keep a book on a shelf in the morning, intending to read it later. After finishing your day's work, you return in the evening and find the book at the same place. You expect this because the book will not move away from its position on its own. The forces acting on the book balance one another, so the net force is zero and its state of motion remains unchanged.

Galileo took such familiar observations further. He performed experiments which recognised that the persistence of a body's state of motion, is a general property of matter itself. Thus, every material body possesses this property: in the absence of a net external force, it continues in its existing state of motion.

Before Galileo, the commonly accepted understanding of motion was largely influenced by Aristotle's ideas about natural motion and forced motion.
According to Aristotle, a stone falling downwards undergoes natural motion and does not require an external force to sustain that motion.
Lifting the stone upwards, however, is forced motion: the stone cannot lift itself and requires an external agent to move it upwards.
Galileo challenged this understanding through his investigations of motion, and developed the idea that the persistence of motion does not require a continuously acting force.
This was a fundamental shift in how motion was understood.

Galileo performed experiments with inclined planes to assert that bodies tend to preserve their existing state of motion on their own. In the absence of external influences that change their motion, a body would continue moving indefinitely with constant velocity.

The fact that moving objects eventually stop in everyday situations is due to opposing forces, such as friction, rather than an inherent tendency of matter to stop moving.

This tendency of a body to preserve its existing state of motion is known as inertia. Intuitively, a body is already doing something. Left alone, it keeps doing that same thing. In simple words, the body's existing history of motion tends to continue, and force is what interrupts that continuity.

This makes inertia almost a characteristic of matter itself. Just as matter possesses mass and occupies space, matter possesses inertia—the tendency to preserve its existing state of motion.

Newton formalised this principle through his First Law of Motion. The law states that every body continues in its state of rest or uniform motion unless compelled to change that state by a net external force.

The word inherent means that the property belongs to the body itself rather than arising from its surroundings. For example, a body's mass does not change simply because it is moved to a different location, although its weight can change with the local gravitational field

To understand inertia more precisely, we need to identify how the state of motion of a body is described mathematically, so that we may determine a change in it.

We describe the "state of motion of a body" using its velocity vector. Since inertia is associated with the persistence of this state, it manifests as the tendency to preserve the velocity vector when no net force acts on the body.

The existing state of motion is described by the initial velocity vector. Depending on this initial state, the persistence of motion can be understood through three manifestations of inertia:

  • Inertia of Rest: If the initial velocity is zero, the body tends to remain at rest.
  • Inertia of Motion: If the body is already moving, it tends to continue moving with the same speed.
  • Inertia of Direction: If the body is already moving in a particular direction, it tends to continue moving in that same direction.
Mass is the quantitative measure of inertia and this concept is important for defining momentum.
Inertia and electric charge are both intrinsic properties of matter.
Mass governs the behaviour of matter in mechanical interactions, whereas electric charge governs its behaviour in electromagnetic interactions.

Significance of Newton's First Law

Newton's First Law plays a foundational role in mechanics. It establishes the causal relationship between net force (acting on the body) and a change in the state of motion (of the body).

However, the significance of the First Law goes beyond this relationship. It also establishes a conceptual framework (or grounding) within which the other laws of motion operate.

Why do we need such a framework? Because quantities such as velocity and acceleration depend on the reference frame from which motion is observed. The same physical situation can produce different descriptions of motion when viewed from different reference frames.

Imagine that you are sitting in a stationary bus near a window and looking at a tree. Initially, both you and the tree are at rest relative to the ground. Now suppose the bus accelerates forward. You observe the tree accelerates backwards relative to you, even though the net force acting on the tree remains zero.

In the bus frame, the tree is observed to have changed its state of motion without a corresponding net force. If we tried to apply Newton's First Law in this frame, it fails because as per the First Law, the tree should continue to be at rest.

The problem is not with the law. The problem is that the bus is an accelerating reference frame. The observed acceleration of the tree relative to the bus arises because the reference frame itself is accelerating.

So, we cannot apply Newton's Laws in every reference frame. We need to identify the special class of reference frames in which the relationship between physical forces and observed acceleration holds as stated by Newton's Laws.

Newton's First Law provides a way to identify inertial frames of reference through its definition.

We check whether the First Law holds in a particular frame. If a body experiencing zero net force has zero acceleration in that frame, the frame satisfies the First Law and is called an inertial frame of reference.

Thus, an inertial frame of reference is simply a frame in which Newton's First Law holds true.

Newton's First Law does not merely describe how bodies move when no net force acts on them. It also tells us which reference frames are suitable for applying Newton's Laws of Motion.
In an inertial frame, the First Law gives the causal implication: \[ \boxed{\vec{F}_{\text{net}}=0\;\Rightarrow\;\vec{a}=0} \] That is, if the net force acting on a body is zero, its acceleration is zero and its velocity remains constant.
