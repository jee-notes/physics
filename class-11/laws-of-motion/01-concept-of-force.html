Concept of Force

The qualitative idea of force.

Concept

In physics, we study well-defined physical quantities that can be expressed mathematically. Like, we studied formal quantitative definitions of position, displacement etc. in kinematics.

We will study force under a similar framework. But before defining it mathematically, we need to understand the qualitative idea of Force.

The idea of force is much older than Newton and arose from attempts to understand motion.

Living things can move on their own, but non-living things require something from outside to move - as is generally observed.

We can set living things in motion from outside as well by pushing or pulling.
But non-living things cannot move on their own.

This distinction connected the early idea of force with life force itself — the ability to be alive was indicated by the ability to initiate motion.

Aristotle believed that a continuous force was required to sustain uniform motion of a body undergoing forced motion. If the force stopped acting, the motion would eventually stop.

Galileo challenged this idea. He realized that a force is not required to sustain uniform motion; rather, a force is required to change the state of motion.

Moving objects in everyday life stop because of opposing forces such as friction.

Newton later formalized this insight through his First Law of Motion.

Even a mighty thinker like Aristotle had incomplete ideas about force and motion. This only shows how difficult it can be to understand nature correctly. What appears obvious from everyday experience may not reveal the actual physical principles governing a phenomenon.

The simplest description is perhaps that force is a push or pull. It is not an exhaustive definition, but it provides a basic qualitative understanding of force.

A push or pull can produce different effects. It can change the motion of a body, or it can deform the body. For example, pressing a soft object can change its shape, while pushing a book can set it in motion or change its existing motion.

In this chapter, our primary concern is the effect of force on motion.

If I ask you to move a book from location A to location B, you need to apply force appropriately to initiate its motion, change its speed or direction when required, and bring it to rest at the desired location.

Thus, although force can produce several physical effects, we will primarily study how force affects the motion of bodies. This is the central idea that leads us to the Laws of Motion.

Force

If I have to define force as comprehensively as possible, then Force is a mechanical interaction between two entities that exert force on each other.

In our everyday experience, we encounter different kinds of forces. Muscular force allows us to push, pull, carry and throw objects. Contact between bodies gives rise to normal force and friction. Stretched strings and ropes exert tension, while compressed or elongated springs exert restoring forces. We also encounter buoyancy, viscous force, fluid pressure and surface tension.

At the microscopic level, we encounter electric and magnetic forces, forces between atoms and molecules, and nuclear forces involving the constituents of atomic nuclei.

At first, these forces may appear to be entirely different. However, a major insight of physics is that the many forces observed in different physical situations arise from only a small number of fundamental interactions in nature.

Consider the force exerted by a spring. When a spring is stretched or compressed, the relative positions of its neighbouring atoms change. The electromagnetic interactions between the charged constituents of these atoms produce a net restoring force that tends to bring the atoms back towards their equilibrium positions. Thus, the familiar spring force has its origin in electromagnetic interactions.

Similarly, normal force, tension, friction and the forces responsible for chemical bonding arise primarily from electromagnetic interactions between the constituents of matter. These are examples of derived forces: their macroscopic behaviour emerges from more fundamental interactions. The detailed mechanisms can be complex, even when the resulting force can be described by a simple mathematical law.

At present, four fundamental interactions are recognised in nature:

  • Gravitational interaction: The interaction between bodies possessing mass. It is responsible for the weight of objects near the Earth's surface and the motion of planets and other astronomical bodies. It acts over an unlimited range and governs motion on astronomical scales.
  • Electromagnetic interaction: The interaction between electrically charged entities. It underlies electrical and magnetic phenomena and is responsible for most familiar contact forces between ordinary bodies.
  • Strong nuclear interaction: The interaction responsible for binding quarks into composite particles and, through its residual effects, binding protons and neutrons within atomic nuclei. Its residual force between nucleons acts over distances comparable to nuclear dimensions.
  • Weak nuclear interaction: The interaction involved in processes such as beta decay and other transformations of elementary particles. The nuclear forces (strong or weak) operate over extremely short distances, and unlike gravity and familiar electromagnetic forces, they are not directly experienced in ordinary mechanical interactions.

In mechanics, we primarily encounter gravitational forces and macroscopic forces arising from electromagnetic interactions. Understanding this connection helps us recognise that normal force, friction, tension and spring force are not separate fundamental interactions; they are different manifestations of underlying physical interactions.

This has an important consequence. In principle, the laws governing derived forces, such as spring force and friction, are not independent of the laws governing fundamental interactions. Since these forces arise from fundamental interactions, their behaviour must ultimately be understood in terms of those underlying interactions. However, deriving their macroscopic behaviour can be extremely complex.

Laws of Physics

Teachers often say that real physics starts with this chapter because it introduces the laws of physics. Although this statement is not entirely true, it serves as a way of building interest in the subject. However, before studying Newton's Laws of Motion, it is useful to understand what we mean by the term "law" in physics.

This is the first chapter in physics, in which we encounter a law of physics - a statement that scientifically describes a natural phenomenon. To understand the idea, let us consider the phenomenon of motion.

The phenomenon of motion had been studied for centuries before Newton. Philosophers and scientists such as Aristotle and, much later, Galileo observed moving bodies and attempted to understand the principles governing their motion. They sought to identify general rules that could explain how bodies behave under different circumstances.

When we observe nature, individual events may appear different from one another. A stone falls to the ground, a ball rolls across a surface, and a planet moves around the Sun. However, by studying many such events, we try to identify a common pattern in all of this. This pattern is expressed as a general rule that applies to a class of physical situations.

Such rules are expressed as concise and precise statements that summarize the observed behaviour of nature. Depending on the phenomenon being studied, a law of physics may provide a qualitative description, a quantitative relationship, or both.

A qualitative law describes how a physical system behaves, whereas a quantitative law expresses a measurable relationship between physical quantities.

Thus, a statement expressing a general rule that describes a reproducible natural phenomenon is called a physical law. Scientists formulate such laws by systematically observing natural phenomena, identifying regularities in their behaviour, and testing the proposed rules against experimental observations.

Newton formulated three fundamental laws describing the motion of bodies. These are collectively known as Newton's Laws of Motion. They established a mathematical framework for studying how forces affect the motion of bodies and became the foundation of what we call Newtonian Mechanics.

This does not mean that nobody studied motion before Newton. Earlier thinkers had already made important observations and discoveries. Galileo, for example, had developed important ideas about motion, while Kepler had established mathematical laws describing planetary motion. Newton built upon such earlier work rather than starting from nothing.

Newton's contribution was not simply that he observed motion or collected the discoveries of earlier scientists. He established a systematic mathematical description that unified many previously studied phenomena under general principles.

His work illustrates how physics progresses: observations lead to the identification of patterns, these patterns are expressed as general laws, and the resulting framework is tested against the behaviour of nature.
