Newton's First Law

Force, inertia and the principle that governs motion.

What are Laws of Motion?

Before studying Newton's Laws of Motion, it is worth asking what we mean by the term "Laws of Motion". This is the first chapter in which students encounter the idea of "laws" describing a natural phenomenon.

The phenomenon of motion had been studied for centuries before Newton. Historical records show that philosophers and scientists such as Aristotle and, much later, Galileo carefully observed moving bodies and sought to understand the principles governing their motion.

Their objective was to uncover the general rules that explain how bodies move under different circumstances. Such rules are expressed as concise, precise statements that summarize the behavior of nature. Depending on the phenomenon being studied, a law may provide a qualitative description, a quantitative relationship, or both.

These statements are called laws. Newton's greatest contribution was to formulate three fundamental laws that describe the motion of bodies. These are collectively known as Newton's Laws of Motion.

Newton's First Law

Newton's First Law of Motion states:

Every body continues in its state of rest or of uniform motion unless compelled to change that state by the action of an external force.

Before expressing the law mathematically, it is useful to understand it qualitatively. We are already familiar with the ideas of rest and uniform motion from kinematics. There, we primarily described the motion of point particles. Here, however, the law refers to a body. For the present discussion, we may imagine the body to be a small object such as a marble and treat it as a point particle. The only new idea introduced by the law is force.

To understand Newton's First Law, we must first understand what is meant by force.

What is Force?

The concept of force existed long before the development of physics, even in the time of Aristotle. In everyday life, we associate force with actions such as pushing, pulling, lifting or stretching objects. These actions appear to influence how objects move. However, it was Newton who gave the concept of force a precise role within a mathematical theory of motion.

In intuitive terms, a force may be regarded as a push or a pull exerted on a body to influence its motion.
Because forces possess both magnitude and direction, force is a vector quantity.

In mechanics, we shall learn that force represents a mechanical interaction capable of changing the motion of a body. Such a change may involve starting motion, stopping motion, speeding up, slowing down or changing the direction of motion.

Since the motion of a body is described by its velocity, any change in velocity corresponds to a change in its motion. Thus, force is fundamentally associated with the production of acceleration.

When several forces act simultaneously on a body, their combined effect is represented by the net force acting on the body.

With this understanding, Newton's First Law has an important implication: if the net external force acting on a body is zero, the body's motion does not change.

\[ \sum \vec{F}=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \vec{a}=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \vec{v}=\text{constant} \]

Thus, a body either remains at rest or continues to move with constant velocity in a straight line whenever the net external force acting on it is zero.

Reverse implication is also true. If \(\vec{v} = \text{constant}\) then \(\Sigma \vec{F} = 0\) .

It is precisely this causal implication relation that forms the central idea of Newton's First Law.

A natural question now arises: Why does a body continue in the same state of motion when no net force acts on it? In other words, why does this causal implication relation hold? Newton answered this by proposing that every body possesses an inherent property called inertia, which resists any change in its state of motion.

For this reason, Newton's First Law is also known as the Law of Inertia.

Inertia

Long before Newton, scientists noticed that bodies tend to resist changes to their motion. This idea was strongly advocated by Galileo, who argued that in the absence of external influences, a body would continue moving indefinitely with constant velocity.

This was a major intellectual breakthrough, challenging the long-held belief that a continuous force is required to sustain motion.

This tendency of a body to preserve its existing state of motion is known as inertia. Intuitively, a body is already doing something. Left alone, it keeps doing that same thing. In simple words, the body's existing history of motion tends to continue, and force is what interrupts that continuity.

Newton adopted this idea from Galileo and proposed that the First Law holds because every body possesses an inherent property called inertia. Inherent means that this property belongs to the body itself and does not depend on its surroundings.

To understand inertia more precisely, we represent the state of motion of a body by its velocity vector. Since inertia tends to preserve the existing state of motion, it opposes any change in the velocity vector.

The manner in which this existing state can continue depends on the initial state of the velocity vector. This naturally gives rise to three manifestations of inertia:

  • Inertia of Rest: If the initial velocity is zero, the body tends to remain at rest.
  • Inertia of Motion: If the body is already moving, it tends to continue moving with the same speed.
  • Inertia of Direction: If the body is already moving in a particular direction, it tends to continue moving in that same direction.
Rest mass is the quantitative measure of inertia.
Inertia and charge are both intrinsic properties of matter.
Mass governs the behaviour of matter in mechanical interactions, whereas charge governs its behaviour in electromagnetic interactions.

Significance of Newton's First Law

Newton's first law plays a foundational role in mechanics. It clarifies the relationship between force and motion and establishes the conceptual framework within which the other laws of motion operate.

First, the law provides an operational way to define force. If a body experiences a net external force, its velocity changes. Thus force can be understood as the physical cause responsible for producing acceleration.

Second, the law identifies special reference frames in which this principle holds true. These frames are called inertial frames of reference.

An inertial frame is one in which a body with zero net force either remains at rest or moves with constant velocity.

Newton's second and third laws are valid only within such frames. In this sense, the first law establishes the very framework within which the rest of Newtonian mechanics is formulated.

Newton's first law is not merely a statement about motion; it defines the class of reference frames in which the laws of mechanics are valid.

Applications

Newton's first law has two main applications:

  • Equilibrium Argument
  • Inertial Frame of Reference
