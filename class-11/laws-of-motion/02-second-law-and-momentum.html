Momentum and Newton's Second Law

Quantitative relationship between force and motion.

Linear Momentum

The linear momentum (\(\vec{p}\)) of a point mass is defined as the product of its mass (\(m\)) and its velocity (\(\vec{v}\)).

\[ \vec{p} = m\vec{v} \]

Momentum is a vector quantity and its direction is the same as the direction of velocity. The SI unit of momentum is \(kg \cdot m/s\).

Momentum is what separates kinematics from dynamics. Kinematics merely describes motion; it can describe the motion of any identifiable pattern in space. For example, the motion of a shadow across a wall can be described in terms of position, velocity, and acceleration.

However, a shadow does not possess momentum. It is not a physical carrier of momentum; it is only the absence of light produced by an obstruction. Thus the motion of a shadow, though describable kinematically, is not subject to the principles of dynamics.

Dynamics applies only to entities that possess momentum. Momentum is therefore the quantity through which motion becomes subject to the laws of dynamics. Matter and physical fields possess momentum, and their motion can be governed by dynamical laws.

Newton's Second Law

Newton's second law states that the net force acting on a body is equal to the rate of change of its momentum.

\[ \vec{F} = \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt} \]

The direction of the force is the same as the direction of the change in momentum.

Force and Acceleration

For a body of constant mass:

\[ \vec{F} = \frac{d(m\vec{v})}{dt} \]

Since mass is constant,

\[ \vec{F} = m\frac{d\vec{v}}{dt} = m\vec{a} \]
The equation \( \vec{F} = m\vec{a} \) is a special case of Newton's second law that applies when the mass of the system remains constant. If the mass of the system changes (for example, in a rocket), the full momentum equation must be used.

Impulse

Impulse measures the effect of a force acting over a short time interval. It is equal to the change in momentum produced by the force.

\[ \vec{J} = \int \vec{F} \, dt = \Delta \vec{p} \]

Thus, a large force acting for a short time can produce the same change in momentum as a smaller force acting for a longer time.

Significance of Newton's Second Law

Newton's second law establishes a precise relation between force and the change of momentum of a body. Force is therefore the physical cause responsible for changing the state of motion.

An important consequence is that force does not produce velocity itself; it produces a change in momentum. If the net external force on a body is zero, its momentum remains constant and its motion does not change.

Force and acceleration are simultaneous. There is no time delay between cause and effect: as long as a net force acts on a body, its momentum changes and the body accelerates. When the force vanishes, the acceleration immediately vanishes.

This has a direct implication in situations where an object is said to be dropped. When an object X is dropped from another body Y, it is merely released; no force is applied to X at that instant. Therefore X retains the velocity that it had while moving with Y, but it does not retain Y's acceleration.

After the release, the motion of X is determined only by the forces acting on it (such as gravity), not by the previous acceleration of Y.
