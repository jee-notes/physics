Equilibrium Argument

If no effect, then no cause.

Force Causes Acceleration

Newton's First Law establishes a causal relationship between net external force and acceleration. Whenever a net external force acts on a body, its state of motion changes, producing acceleration.

Thus, net external force is the cause and acceleration is the effect.

The equilibrium argument uses the reverse direction of this causal reasoning. Instead of predicting the effect from the cause, we observe the effect and infer the cause.

Therefore, if a body has zero acceleration, the net external force acting on it must also be zero.

Illustration

Consider a book resting on a table. Two forces act on the book:

  • Weight acting downward: \(m\vec{g}\)
  • Normal force exerted by the table acting upward: \(\vec{N}\)

The weight of the book can be calculated from its mass, but the magnitude of the normal force is not known beforehand.

We only know that the normal force is a constraint force exerted by the table to prevent the book from penetrating its surface.

To determine the magnitude of the normal force, we use the equilibrium argument.

The book is observed to remain at rest. Since its state of motion does not change, its acceleration is zero.

Applying the equilibrium argument, zero acceleration implies that the net external force acting on the book must also be zero.

\[ \vec{a}=0 \] \[ \Downarrow \] \[ \sum\vec{F}=0 \] \[ m\vec{g}+\vec{N}=0 \] \[ N=mg \]

Thus, the normal force is not assumed to be equal to the weight. Rather, it is inferred from the equilibrium condition. The upward normal force exactly balances the downward weight, producing zero net force and hence zero acceleration.
