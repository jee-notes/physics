Free Body Diagram

The visual tool for solving dynamics problems.

Concept

A Free Body Diagram (FBD) is a visual (or diagrammatic) representation of a single body or a subsystem of bodies isolated from its surroundings, showing all the forces acting on it.

Basically, we are visually representing our study of the dynamics of the system.

Dynamics concerns the forces acting on the system and their effect on its motion.

Crucially, an FBD shows forces acting on the body, and not the forces exerted by the body.

The main use of an FBD is to represent the forces acting on the system as vectors.

It allows us to visualize their directions and resolve them into components along convenient coordinate axes.

Steps to draw an FBD

There is a well-defined algorithm for drawing an FBD.

Learning this procedure is important initially because this is the first time students learn to represent a physical system diagrammatically for the analysis of dynamics.

Later in physics, we study circuit diagrams and ray diagrams that fulfil a similar purpose.

By then, students are expected to be comfortable with the basic skill of representing a physical situation through a diagram, which is developed here through FBDs.

  1. Identify the body or system to be analyzed.
  2. Isolate the body or system and represent it as a point or a simple shape.
  3. Identify and draw all external forces acting on it:
    • Field Forces: Weight (\(mg\)) acting vertically downwards.
    • Contact Forces: Normal force (\(N\)), Tension (\(T\)), Friction (\(f\)), etc.
  4. Choose a convenient coordinate system and define the corresponding unit vectors. The axes are usually chosen along important directions such as the expected acceleration, motion, or constraints.
  5. Resolve the forces into components along the chosen axes.

Vector Equation

We need to write 1D vector equations that are equal in both magnitude and direction.

\[ \Sigma \vec{F}_x=m\vec{a}_x \qquad \Sigma \vec{F}_y=m\vec{a}_y \qquad \Sigma \vec{F}_z=m\vec{a}_z \]

The FBD provides the physical information about the directions of all the forces acting on the body.

We use this information while constructing the 1D vector equation.

This directional information cannot be ignored because an equation between vectors must represent equality of both magnitude and direction.

In one dimension, direction is represented mathematically by the \(+\) and \(-\) signs.

Therefore, we first use the FBD to anticipate the direction of acceleration and choose that direction as positive.

The forces acting along this direction are then written with positive signs, while forces acting opposite to it are written with negative signs.

For example, if \(F_1,F_2\) act along the anticipated direction of \(a_x\), while \(F_3,F_4\) act opposite to it, the FBD is translated into the 1D vector equation:

\[ \Sigma \vec{F}_x = F_1+F_2-F_3-F_4 = ma_x \]

The signs are therefore not arbitrary algebraic choices. They are required by the vector equation so that the equation remains equal in both magnitude and direction.

The FBD contains the physical directional information, while the \(+\) and \(-\) signs in the 1D vector equation are the mathematical representation of that information.
Changing a sign arbitrarily changes the direction represented by the equation and hence changes the physical equation itself.
The direction of a force is determined from the FBD. For example, weight acts downwards.
The choice available to us is whether to assign the downward direction as positive \((+)\) or negative \((-)\).
This choice does not change the fact that the weight still acts downwards.
