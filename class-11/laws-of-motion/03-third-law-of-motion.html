Newton's Third Law

The reciprocal nature of forces.

Why Another Law?

Newton's Second Law provides a quantitative relationship between the net external force acting on a body and the rate of change of its momentum.

A natural question now arises: What is the fundamental nature of force itself? Newton's Third Law answers this question.

The Third Law reveals that a force is not an isolated entity. Every force is one half of a mechanical interaction between two bodies. This is the fundamental reason why force is described as a mechanical interaction.

Later, we shall see that the same mechanical interaction can be viewed in different ways. Depending on what aspect of the interaction is being studied, it may be described as exerting a force, imparting an impulse, transferring mechanical energy, or delivering power.

These are not different interactions; they are different physical descriptions of the same underlying mechanical interaction.

Third Law

Newton's Third Law states that whenever two bodies interact mechanically, they exert forces on each other that are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.

\[ \vec{F}_{A/B}=-\vec{F}_{B/A} \]

Here, \(\vec{F}_{A/B}\) is the force exerted on body A by body B, while \(\vec{F}_{B/A}\) is the force exerted on body B by body A.

This is the fundamental reason why a force is called a mechanical interaction. A force never exists in isolation; it is always born as a pair of twins.

Whenever one body exerts a force on another, the second body simultaneously exerts an equal and opposite force on the first.

The two forces are traditionally called an "action-reaction pair." However, it is better to think of them as an interaction pair because they are created simultaneously as two aspects of the same mechanical interaction.

For example, if you gently slap your own cheek, your hand exerts a force on your cheek, and your cheek exerts an equal-magnitude force on your hand in the opposite direction.

Although the interaction forces are exactly equal and opposite, the effects on the two bodies need not be the same. The response of each body depends on its own physical properties such as mass, stiffness, shape, and material. In this example, the cheek undergoes greater deformation and also feels more pain because biological tissues are softer and more sensitive than the hand.

From the viewpoint of dynamics, a slap and a clap are the same kind of mechanical interaction.
Only the interacting bodies are different.

Nature of Interaction

We now understand that a force is a mechanical interaction. The next natural question is: What kinds of mechanical interactions exist in nature (physical world)?

It has been found that every force observed in nature ultimately arises from just four fundamental interactions.

  • Gravitational Interaction
  • Electromagnetic Interaction
  • Strong Nuclear Interaction
  • Weak Nuclear Interaction

The strong and weak nuclear interactions operate over extremely short distances inside atomic nuclei. Their quantitative study belongs to nuclear and particle physics and is beyond the scope of exam syllabus.

In elementary mechanics, we shall deal almost entirely with gravitational and electromagnetic interactions.

A natural question now arises: If only these two interactions are being studied, what about forces such as the normal force, tension, friction, spring force, buoyancy, chemical bonding force?

These are not fundamental forces. They are macroscopic manifestations of the electromagnetic interaction between atoms and molecules. Thus, all ordinary contact forces—including those acting in solids and fluids—ultimately have an electromagnetic origin.

Characteristics of Action-Reaction Pairs

  • They act on different bodies. Therefore, they never cancel each other to produce equilibrium for a single body.
  • They are of the same nature (for example, both gravitational, both normal, etc.).
  • They occur simultaneously. There is no time lag between action and reaction.
A common misconception is that action and reaction cancel each other.
This is true only when the two interacting bodies are treated as a single system.
For an individual body, the two forces act on different bodies and therefore cannot cancel.
Another common misconception is that \(N=mg\) follows from Newton's Third Law.
It does not. The equality \(N=mg\) follows from Newton's First Law (or Newton's Second Law with \(a=0\)), since both forces act on the same body and must balance to produce zero acceleration.

Same Cause, Different Effects

Newton's Third Law reveals the true nature of force: force is a mechanical interaction. Every interaction produces an equal and opposite pair of forces acting on the two interacting bodies.

At first, this may appear to contradict our everyday experience. For example, when you slap your own cheek, the cheek hurts much more than the hand. It is tempting to conclude that the cheek experienced a larger force.

However, this conclusion is incorrect. According to Newton's Third Law, the force exerted by the hand on the cheek and the force exerted by the cheek on the hand are exactly equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.

The important point is that the cause is the same, but the effects need not be. The response of a body depends not only on the interaction force but also on its own physical properties such as mass, stiffness, elasticity, geometry and, in living tissues, biological sensitivity.

The same idea applies to gravitational interaction. The Earth pulls you with a gravitational force, and you simultaneously pull the Earth with an equal and opposite gravitational force. The forces are identical as an interaction pair, but the resulting accelerations are vastly different because the Earth has an enormously larger mass.

In the present chapter, our focus is on mechanics, where force is studied through its effect on the motion of bodies. However, force is not limited to changing motion. The same mechanical interaction can also deform bodies. Later, in the chapter on Elasticity, we shall study force from this perspective and quantitatively relate force to deformation.

Conservation of Momentum

Within Newtonian mechanics, the principle of conservation of linear momentum is derived from Newton's Second and Third Laws.

For an isolated system, no net external force acts on the system, while all internal forces occur in equal and opposite pairs.

Consequently, the total linear momentum of the system remains constant.

Since Newton's Laws are valid only in inertial frames of reference, conservation of momentum is applied only in inertial frames in all mechanics (Newtonian) problems.

In Newtonian Mechanics, Newton's Laws are the fundamental laws. The Impulse-Momentum Theorem, Work-Energy Theorem, and the corresponding conservation laws are all derived from them.
The word theorem itself means that it is a statement that is derived from more fundamental axioms or previously established results.
Since these results are derived from Newton's Laws, they inherit the same domain of applicability. Therefore, momentum conservation and energy conservation are applied only in inertial frames of reference.
In Modern Physics, Newtonian mechanics is replaced by more general operating frameworks such as Lagrangian Mechanics, Hamiltonian Mechanics, and Quantum Mechanics. Momentum and energy remain conserved in these frameworks as well, but their conservation is established from the fundamental principles of those theories rather than from Newton's Laws.

Thus, within Newtonian mechanics, conservation of momentum and energy are derived results (existing as theorems derived from Newton's Laws of Motion). But, in a more general formulation of physics, they remain valid as fundamental conservation principles.
