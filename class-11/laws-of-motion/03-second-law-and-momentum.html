Newton's Second Law of Motion

Quantitative relationship between force and motion.

Why Do We Need More Laws?

Newton's First Law introduces the concepts of force, inertia and inertial frames of reference. It provides a qualitative description of how force is related to a change in the state of motion.

The Second Law tells us how much the motion changes in response to a given net force. To establish this quantitative relationship, we need to mathematically relate force, inertia and the state of motion of a body.

Newton's Second Law establishes the mathematical relationship between the net external force acting on a body and a physical quantity called momentum.

The idea is significant because both inertia and the state of motion belong to the description of the body itself. Inertia is quantified by mass, while the state of motion is described by velocity in a specified inertial frame. Force, on the other hand, represents a mechanical interaction exerted on the body by its surroundings.

Newton combines inertia and the state of motion into a single physical quantity called momentum. Momentum is defined as the product of mass and velocity:

\[ \boxed{\vec{p}=m\vec{v}} \]

Thus, a body possesses momentum, which incorporates both its inertia and its state of motion. Newton's Second Law then relates the external interaction acting on the body to the rate of change of this combined quantity.

This gives us a quantitative connection between three concepts: force, inertia and the state of motion. Instead of relating force separately to mass and velocity, Newton relates force to the rate of change of momentum.

Later, when we study systems consisting of multiple particles, we will distinguish between external forces, exerted by bodies outside the system, and internal forces, arising from interactions between particles within the system.

Momentum

The momentum (\(\vec{p}\)) of a particle is defined as the product of its mass (\(m\)) and its velocity (\(\vec{v}\)).

\[ \boxed{\vec{p}=m\vec{v}} \]

Momentum combines two important characteristics of a body: its inertia, quantified by mass, and its state of motion, described by velocity. Thus, momentum represents both how much inertia a body possesses and the state of motion in which that body exists.

Momentum is an instantaneous vector quantity, like velocity. Its direction is the same as that of the velocity vector. Since mass is a scalar quantity, it affects the magnitude of momentum but not its direction.

The SI unit of momentum is \(kg\cdot m/s\), and its dimensions are \([M][L][T]^{-1}\).

Momentum is the quantity that separates kinematics from dynamics.

Kinematics describes motion in terms of quantities such as position, displacement, velocity and acceleration. It can describe the motion of any identifiable pattern in space, whether or not that pattern corresponds to a physical body.

For example, consider the shadow of an object moving across a wall. We can identify the position of the shadow at different instants and describe how its position changes with time. We can assign it a velocity and acceleration just as we do when describing the motion of a physical body.

However, a shadow does not possess momentum. A shadow is a pattern formed by the absence of light reaching a region of a surface. Although its motion can be described kinematically, the shadow itself is not a material body carrying momentum. Its apparent motion results from the physical arrangement and motion of the objects, light source and receiving surface that produce the shadow.

This illustrates an important distinction. Kinematics allows us to describe motion without necessarily asking what physical entity carries that motion or what causes it. Dynamics, on the other hand, studies the motion of physical entities in relation to the interactions acting on them.

Dynamics applies to physical entities that possess momentum. In this sense, momentum is the physical quantity through which the state of motion of a body is related to the laws governing its dynamical behaviour. A shadow can have a describable velocity, but it is not an independent physical entity to which we assign momentum and apply the usual equations of particle dynamics.

Momentum is not restricted to material bodies.
Physical fields can also possess momentum.
Matter and fields are physical entities whose dynamical behaviour can be studied using the relevant laws of physics.

Momentum Interpretation

In dynamics, momentum is interpreted as the amount of motion possessed by a body. This is an intuitive interpretation of the quantity defined mathematically by \(\vec{p}=m\vec{v}\).

The idea is that both the mass and the velocity of a body contribute to its momentum. A body with greater mass has greater momentum than a body with smaller mass when both have the same velocity. Similarly, for a fixed mass, increasing the magnitude of velocity increases the magnitude of momentum.

For example, consider a bicycle of mass \(5\,kg\) and a truck of mass \(1000\,kg\), both moving with the same velocity of \(50\,m/s\). Their momenta are: \(p_{\text{bicycle}}=5\times 50=250\,kg\cdot m/s\) , \(p_{\text{truck}}=1000\times 50=50000\,kg\cdot m/s\) .

Although their velocities are identical, the truck possesses \(200\) times the momentum of the bicycle because its mass is \(200\) times greater.

This difference is important when studying collisions. A moving truck generally carries much greater momentum than a bicycle moving at the same velocity, so bringing the truck to rest involves a much larger change in momentum.

The actual severity of a collision also depends on how the collision occurs and how momentum and energy are transferred, but the comparison illustrates why mass must be considered along with velocity when describing motion dynamically.

Thus, momentum combines the body's inertia and its state of motion into a single physical quantity. This is precisely why it is useful in dynamics: it gives us a quantity possessed by a body whose change can be related to the external force acting on it through Newton's Second Law.

Newton's Second Law

Newton's Second Law of Motion states:
The net external force acting on a body is equal to the rate of change of its momentum with respect to time.
\[ \vec{F}_{\text{net}}=\frac{d\vec{p}}{dt} \]

The SI unit of force is \(N\) (stands for Newton - hence capitalized) or \(kg\cdot m/s^2\), and its dimensions are \([M][L][T]^{-2}\).

So far, we have defined momentum only for a point particle.

Therefore, we shall initially apply Newton's Second Law to point particles. Later, we shall extend the same idea to systems consisting of multiple particles.

Notice that Newton's Second Law is a vector equation.

It states that the net force and the rate of change of momentum are equal in both magnitude and direction. The net force acting on the body points in the direction of the rate of change of its momentum which is acceleration in most cases.

We have already studied the motion of bodies under gravity in kinematics. If we examine the same motion from the viewpoint of Newton's Second Law then the gravitational force on the projectile body \(F_g = mg\) downward and so its acceleration is given by:

\[ \vec{a} = \frac{\vec{F}_{\text{net}}}{m}=\frac{\vec{F}_g}{m} = \frac{-mg \, \hat{j}}{m} = -g \, \hat{j} \]

Force and Acceleration

We can determine the mathematical characteristics of force by examining the relation given by Newton's Second Law.

To understand this relation, we first consider a particle on which a single force acts. In this case, that force is the net force acting on the particle. The same reasoning applies to the net force when multiple forces act simultaneously.

Force is an instantaneous vector quantity. It specifies the rate at which the momentum of a body changes at a particular instant.

Since the momentum of a particle is given by \(\vec{p}=m\vec{v}\), Newton's Second Law gives:

\[ \vec{F}(t)=\frac{d\vec{p}(t)}{dt}=\frac{d}{dt}\left[m(t)\vec{v}(t)\right] \]

Using the product rule of differentiation, we obtain:

\[ \boxed{\vec{F}(t)=m(t)\frac{d\vec{v}(t)}{dt}+\vec{v}(t)\frac{dm(t)}{dt}} \]

To determine the force at a particular instant \(t_1\), we evaluate this expression at \(t=t_1\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \vec{F}(t_1) ={}&m(t_1) \left.\frac{d\vec{v}(t)}{dt}\right|_{t=t_1}\\ &+\vec{v}(t_1) \left.\frac{dm(t)}{dt}\right|_{t=t_1} \end{aligned} \]

For a material point particle in ordinary Newtonian mechanics, the mass remains constant with time. Therefore, \(m(t)=m\) and \(\frac{dm(t)}{dt}=0\) . The second term consequently vanishes, leaving:

\[ \vec{F} = m\frac{d\vec{v}}{dt} \]

Since the rate of change of velocity is acceleration, we obtain:

\[ \boxed{\vec{F} = m\vec{a}} \]

This is the familiar form of Newton's Second Law. It follows from the general momentum relation when the mass is constant.

The familiar equation \(\vec{F}_{\text{net}}=m\vec{a}\) is a constant-mass form of Newton's Second Law.
For a constant-mass particle, the net force and acceleration are parallel and point in the same direction.
The magnitude of the acceleration is proportional to the magnitude of the net force and inversely proportional to the mass.
For a system whose mass changes, such as a rocket expelling fuel, we must also account for the momentum carried by the mass entering or leaving the system.

Significance of Newton's Second Law

Newton's Second Law establishes a quantitative relationship between the net external force acting on a body and the rate of change of its momentum.

We already knew that force is the cause responsible for changing the state of motion of a body. Now we also know quantitatively how the momentum of a body changes in response to the net force acting on it.

Physically, a body may be regarded as possessing momentum, while force is exerted on the body as part of a mechanical interaction with its surroundings.

The body's momentum represents its existing state of motion together with its inertia, whereas force describes the external interaction responsible for changing that momentum.

Force does not create motion; it changes the body's existing momentum. A body may already be at rest or moving before a force acts on it. The force changes its momentum, thereby changing its state of motion.

In Newtonian mechanics, the net force and the resulting acceleration are simultaneous (in space and time - called as now-here): the acceleration at any instant is related to the net force acting at that exact same instant. There is no time delay between cause and effect in this description.

As long as a net external force acts, the body's momentum changes at the rate specified by Newton's Second Law. When the net force becomes zero, the rate of change of momentum becomes zero as well. The momentum then remains constant.

This has an important implication when an object is dropped or detached from another body.

At the instant of release, the object retains the momentum—and hence the velocity—it possessed immediately before release. The act of release does not automatically reset its velocity to zero or make it acquire the velocity of some other body.

However, the object does not retain the acceleration of the body from which it was released. Acceleration is determined by the net force acting on the object at that instant. Once the object separates, the forces that acted through its previous connection may disappear, and the net force acting on it may change.

Therefore, the subsequent motion of the object is determined by the forces acting on it after release, not by the previous acceleration of the body from which it separated.

Consider a stone being swung in a circle while tied to a string. While the string remains taut, its tension provides the inward force needed for circular motion. When the string is released, the stone retains its instantaneous velocity, which points along the tangent to the circle at the point of release.

After release, the string no longer provides the inward tension. If gravity is neglected, the stone continues along the tangent in a straight line with constant velocity. When gravity is included, its subsequent trajectory curves downward like that of a projectile.

The velocity just after release is inherited from the motion just before release, but the acceleration just after release is determined by the forces acting on the body just after release.
