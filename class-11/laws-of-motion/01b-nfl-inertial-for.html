Inertial Frame of Reference

Reference frames in which Newton's laws of motion hold true.

Need for a Reference Frame

The position and motion of a body can only be described relative to something else. Therefore, before describing motion, we must first choose a reference frame.

A reference frame provides a stationary background against which the position and velocity of a body are measured. In practice, it consists of a coordinate system together with a clock used to measure position and time.

Since all measurements are made relative to the chosen reference frame, different observers may describe the same motion differently if they use different reference frames.

For example, consider a passenger sitting inside a train moving with constant velocity. To the passenger, the train and everything attached to it, such as the seats and the floor, appear to be at rest. However, to an observer standing on the ground, the entire train is moving with constant velocity.

Thus, the description of motion is not absolute; it always depends on the chosen reference frame.

This means that the state of motion itself is frame-dependent. Since dynamics seeks to explain motion by identifying its causes, we must understand how the choice of reference frame influences the observed motion. Only then can we correctly relate the observed effect (acceleration) to its physical cause (net external force).

Definition of an Inertial Frame

Since acceleration depends on the chosen reference frame, Newton's laws cannot be applied indiscriminately in every frame.

We require a special class of reference frames in which the causal relationship between net external force and acceleration remains valid. Such frames are called inertial frames of reference.

An inertial frame is a reference frame in which a body on which no net external force acts either remains at rest or continues to move with constant velocity in a straight line.

\[ \sum \vec{F}=0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \vec{a}=0 \]

Thus, in an inertial frame, the absence of a net external force always implies the absence of acceleration. This is precisely the causal implication expressed by Newton's First Law.

Newton's laws of motion are formulated for inertial frames of reference.
If a non-inertial frame is chosen, additional pseudo forces must be introduced before the laws can be applied.

Can an Absolutely Inertial Frame be Identified?

An inertial frame is defined as a reference frame in which a body free from net external forces remains at rest or moves with constant velocity.

However, the inertiality of a reference frame cannot be determined in an absolute sense. Unlike properties such as mass or charge, inertiality is not an inherent property of a reference frame. It is a relational property.

This is because acceleration itself is measured only relative to a chosen reference frame. If we change to another frame that accelerates with respect to the original one, the measured acceleration of every body changes accordingly.

Since inertiality is defined through the absence of acceleration for a force-free body, and acceleration itself is frame-dependent, no reference frame can be declared inertial in isolation.

A frame is regarded as inertial or non-inertial only with respect to another inertial frame.

Therefore, in practice, we choose a convenient reference frame and assume it to be inertial for the phenomena under study. For most problems in elementary mechanics, the frame attached to the Earth's surface is taken to be an inertial frame to a very good approximation.

Ground Frame is Not Perfectly Inertial

To a good approximation, frames attached to the surface of the Earth are treated as inertial frames for most everyday mechanical phenomena.

For example, when analysing the motion of a falling object, a moving vehicle, or a projectile, we normally assume ground frame to be an inertial frame.

Strictly speaking, the Earth is not perfectly inertial because it rotates and revolves around the Sun. However, the accelerations associated with these motions are small enough that they can usually be neglected in elementary mechanics.

Similarly, a train or a car moving with constant velocity with respect to ground also behaves as an inertial frame for observers inside it.

Non-Inertial Frames

A reference frame that is accelerating with respect to an inertial frame is called a non-inertial frame of reference.

A reference frame that is rotating with respect to an inertial frame is also a non-inertial frame of reference.

In such frames Newton's first law does not hold in its usual form.

For example, consider a passenger standing inside a bus that suddenly accelerates forward. The passenger feels a backward jerk. From the perspective of the bus, it appears as if a non-existing ghost force is pushing the passenger backward.

However, an observer standing on the ground sees the situation differently. The passenger tends to remain in his original state of motion due to inertia while the bus accelerates forward.

Thus the apparent backward force arises because the bus is an accelerating (non-inertial) frame.

Importance in Mechanics

The primary objective of dynamics is to relate the cause (net external force) to the effect (acceleration). However, acceleration depends on the chosen reference frame. Therefore, Newton's laws cannot be applied indiscriminately in every frame.

The concept of an inertial frame provides the necessary guardrails within which this cause-and-effect relationship remains valid.

Once an inertial frame has been chosen, Newton's laws can be applied directly to determine forces and predict motion.

If a non-inertial frame is chosen instead, the observed acceleration is influenced by the motion of the frame itself. To preserve the validity of Newton's laws, additional correction terms called pseudo forces must be introduced.

Inertial frames are the guardrails of Newtonian mechanics.
They ensure that the observed effect (acceleration) can be correctly attributed to its physical cause (net external force).
