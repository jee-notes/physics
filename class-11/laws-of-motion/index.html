Laws of Motion

Understanding how forces produce changes in motion.

From Kinematics to Dynamics

In kinematics, we describe the motion of a body using quantities such as position, displacement, velocity and acceleration.

These quantities describe how a body moves. However, kinematics does not address the physical interactions responsible for that motion.

The study of these interactions and their relation to motion is the domain of dynamics.

In this chapter, we introduce three fundamental concepts of dynamics: force, inertia and momentum.

Newton's Laws of Motion

The fundamental principles of dynamics were formulated by Newton in his landmark work Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica ("Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy").

Newton condensed the behaviour of motion into three simple but powerful statements, now known as the Laws of Motion.

  • First Law — If the net force acting on a body is zero, its state of motion does not change.
  • Second Law — When a net force acts on a body, it produces acceleration and relates force to the rate of change of momentum.
  • Third Law — Forces between two interacting bodies always occur in equal and opposite pairs.
These three laws form the foundation of classical mechanics and allow us to analyse most everyday motions.

Fundamental Nature

Newton's laws (or any physical law) represent a synthesis of centuries of experimental observations about motion.

Newton built upon the ideas of earlier scientists such as Galileo, Copernicus, Kepler and Aristotle.

Newton's laws are considered fundamental principles of mechanics. In fact, the branch of mechanics based on these laws is called Newtonian Mechanics.

The laws successfully describe the motion of objects in almost all everyday situations.

When dealing with regimes where classical mechanics is no longer adequate — such as motion at speeds close to the speed of light or phenomena at microscopic scales — more general theories such as relativity and quantum mechanics are required.

Force as an Interaction

In everyday language, a force is described as a push or a pull.

More precisely, a force is an interaction between two bodies or between a body and its environment.

  • Your hand pushes a car.
  • A rope pulls a load while lifting it.
  • The Earth pulls objects downward through gravity.

Since forces can act in different directions, force is a vector quantity. Direction is therefore an essential part of describing a force.

If I tell you to lift something off the ground and you push it towards the ground, you will never be "lifting" it.

Types of Forces

Forces can be broadly classified into two categories.

Contact Forces

These arise when two bodies are physically touching. Common examples include:

  • Normal Force — exerted by a surface perpendicular to itself.
  • Friction — acts parallel to the surface and opposes motion.
  • Tension — the pulling force transmitted through a stretched rope or string.

Non-Contact Forces

Some forces act even when bodies are separated by empty space. Examples include:

  • Gravitational Force — the attraction between masses.
  • Magnetic Force — interactions between magnets.

The gravitational force exerted by a planetary body on an object near its surface is called its weight. Planetary body here implies a large planet like body - including moon. For an object at moon's surface, the gravitational force exerted on it by the moon is its weight on the moon.

Superposition of Forces

When multiple forces act on a particle body simultaneously, their combined effect is equivalent to a single net force equal to their vector sum.

If two forces \( \vec{F}_1 \) and \( \vec{F}_2 \) act at a point, their resultant force is

\[ \vec{R} = \vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2 \]

This principle is known as the superposition of forces. It allows us to analyse complex force systems by resolving forces into components along convenient directions.

Just like displacement vectors, force vectors can be resolved into components (Fx, Fy), which greatly simplifies problem solving.
