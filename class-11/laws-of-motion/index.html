Laws of Motion

The causal link between force and the change in motion.

Conceptual Overview

Dynamics is the branch of mechanics that investigates why objects move the way they do. While kinematics describes the path and timing of motion, dynamics identifies Force as the agent responsible for any change in a body's state of rest or uniform motion.

Sir Isaac Newton formalized these principles into three laws that form the bedrock of classical mechanics. These laws explain how the internal and external environments of a system interact to dictate its future evolution.

Historical Evolution

Before Newton, the Aristotelian view held that a force was necessary to maintain motion. Galileo challenged this, proposing the concept of Inertia—the inherent tendency of an object to resist changes to its state of motion.

Newton synthesized Galileo's observations into a comprehensive mathematical framework, proving that force is not required for motion itself, but for acceleration.

Force as a Vector

Force is a vector quantity, meaning it has both magnitude and direction. It follows the principle of superposition; when multiple forces act on a body, their effect is equivalent to a single Net Force (\(\Sigma \vec{F}\)) obtained through vector addition.

A body is in equilibrium if the vector sum of all forces acting on it is zero. Equilibrium does not necessarily mean rest; it means the velocity is constant.

Core Topics

  • Inertia and First Law — Defining the resistance to change and inertial frames.
  • Momentum and Second Law — Quantitative relationship between force, mass, and acceleration.
  • Reaction and Third Law — The reciprocal nature of interactions between two bodies.
  • Free Body Diagrams — The systematic method for isolating a system and identifying forces.
  • Friction — The resistive force that opposes relative motion or the tendency of motion.
