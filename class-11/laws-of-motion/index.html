Laws of Motion

Understanding how forces produce changes in motion.

From Kinematics to Dynamics

In the study of kinematics, we described motion using quantities such as distance, displacement, time, speed, velocity and acceleration. These quantities tell us how an object moves, but they do not explain why the motion occurs.

To answer that question we enter the domain of dynamics — the branch of mechanics that investigates the causes of motion. In this chapter we introduce three new physical quantities that allow us to analyse motion more deeply: Force, Mass and Momentum.

Newton's Laws of Motion

The fundamental principles of dynamics were formulated by Newton in his landmark work Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica ("Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy").

Newton condensed the behaviour of motion into three simple but powerful statements, now known as the Laws of Motion.

  • First Law — If the net force acting on a body is zero, its state of motion does not change.
  • Second Law — When a net force acts on a body, it produces acceleration and relates force to the rate of change of momentum.
  • Third Law — Forces between two interacting bodies always occur in equal and opposite pairs.
These three laws form the foundation of classical mechanics and allow us to analyse most everyday motions.

Fundamental Nature

Newton's laws were not derived mathematically from deeper principles. Instead, they represent a synthesis of centuries of experimental observations about motion. Newton built upon the ideas of earlier scientists including Copernicus, Kepler and especially Galileo Galilei.

Because of this, Newton's laws are considered fundamental principles of classical physics. They successfully describe the motion of objects in almost all everyday situations.

Only under extreme conditions — such as objects moving close to the speed of light or motion inside atoms — must these laws be modified using modern theories like relativity and quantum mechanics.

Force as an Interaction

In everyday language, a force is described as a push or a pull. A more precise definition is that a force is an interaction between two bodies or between a body and its environment. For example:

  • Your hand pushes a stalled car.
  • A rope pulls a load during lifting.
  • The Earth pulls objects downward through gravity.

Since forces can act in different directions, force is a vector quantity. If I tell you to lift something off the ground and you push it towards ground, you will never be "lifting" it.

Types of Forces

Forces can be broadly classified into two categories.

Contact Forces

These arise when two bodies are physically touching. Common examples include:

  • Normal Force — exerted by a surface perpendicular to itself.
  • Friction — acts parallel to the surface and opposes motion.
  • Tension — the pulling force transmitted through a stretched rope or string.

Non-Contact Forces

Some forces act even when bodies are separated by empty space. Examples include:

  • Gravitational Force — the attraction between masses.
  • Magnetic Force — interactions between magnets.

The gravitational force exerted by the Earth on an object is called its weight.

Superposition of Forces

When multiple forces act on a body simultaneously, their combined effect is equivalent to a single net force equal to their vector sum.

If two forces \( \vec{F}_1 \) and \( \vec{F}_2 \) act at a point, their resultant force is

\[ \vec{R} = \vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2 \]

This principle is known as the superposition of forces. It allows us to analyse complex force systems by resolving forces into components along convenient directions.

Just like displacement vectors, force vectors can be resolved into components (Fx, Fy), which greatly simplifies problem solving.
