Conservation of Mechanical Energy

When only conservative forces act, the total mechanical energy of a system remains constant.

Statement

If only conservative forces do work on a system, the total mechanical energy — the sum of kinetic energy and potential energy — remains constant throughout the motion:

\[E_{\text{mech}} = K + U = \text{constant}\] \[K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f\]

This is the principle of conservation of mechanical energy. It is one of the most powerful tools in classical mechanics because it relates the state at any two instants without requiring knowledge of the motion in between.

Conservation of mechanical energy is a special case of the broader law of conservation of energy. When non-conservative forces (like friction) act, mechanical energy is not conserved, but the total energy (including heat and other forms) always is.

Derivation

From the work–energy theorem: \(W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K\).

When only conservative forces act, \(W_{\text{net}} = W_{\text{conservative}} = -\Delta U\).

Substituting:

\[-\Delta U = \Delta K\] \[\Delta K + \Delta U = 0\] \[\Delta (K + U) = 0\] \[K + U = \text{constant}\]

Thus, conservation of mechanical energy follows directly from the work–energy theorem and the definition of potential energy.

When Non-Conservative Forces Are Present

When friction or other non-conservative forces also do work, mechanical energy is not conserved. The generalised equation is:

\[W_{\text{non-conservative}} = \Delta E_{\text{mech}} = (K_f + U_f) - (K_i + U_i)\]

Since non-conservative forces like friction always remove energy from the mechanical system:

\[W_{\text{friction}} = -f_k \cdot d < 0\] \[K_f + U_f=K_i + U_i - f_k \cdot d\]

where \(f_k\) is the kinetic friction force and \(d\) is the distance over which it acts.

The energy removed by friction does not vanish — it reappears as heat. The first law of thermodynamics guarantees that total energy (mechanical + thermal + all other forms) is always conserved, even when mechanical energy alone is not.

Standard Applications

1. Object Falling from Rest

An object of mass \(m\) released from height \(h\) above the ground:

\[mgh + 0 = 0 + \frac{1}{2}mv^2 \implies v = \sqrt{2gh}\]

The result is independent of the shape of the path — whether it falls vertically, slides down a frictionless incline, or rolls down a frictionless curve. This universality is the power of energy methods.

2. Spring–Block System

A block of mass \(m\) attached to a spring (\(k\)) compressed by \(x_0\) and released:

\[\frac{1}{2}kx_0^2 = \frac{1}{2}mv_{\max}^2 \implies v_{\max} = x_0\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}\]

Maximum speed occurs at the equilibrium position (\(x = 0\)) where \(U = 0\) and all energy is kinetic.

Energy Diagrams

A plot of \(U(x)\) versus \(x\) is called a potential energy diagram. From it, we can read off:

  • Equilibrium positions: where \(dU/dx = 0\) (slope is zero → force is zero).
  • Turning points: where \(K = 0\), i.e., where \(U = E_{\text{total}}\). The object cannot pass beyond these points (classically).
  • Maximum speed: where \(U\) is minimum and \(K\) is maximum.
  • Forbidden regions: where \(U > E_{\text{total}}\) — kinetic energy would have to be negative, which is impossible.
