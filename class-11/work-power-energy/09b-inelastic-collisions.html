Inelastic and Perfectly Inelastic Collisions

Interactions where momentum is conserved but macroscopic kinetic energy is lost to internal forms.

Understanding Inelastic Collisions

In an inelastic collision, some of the initial kinetic energy is converted into other forms of energy such as thermal energy, sound, or potential energy of deformation during the impact. While total energy is always conserved in the universe, the mechanical kinetic energy of the interacting macroscopic bodies is not.

Despite this loss of kinetic energy, the total momentum of the system remains strictly conserved, provided there are no significant external impulse forces acting during the short interaction time.

Energy Lost in a Perfectly Inelastic Collision

A completely or perfectly inelastic collision occurs when the relative velocity of separation is zero—that is, the objects stick together and move with a common final mass and velocity.

For a perfectly inelastic collision where mass \(m_1\) with velocity \(u_1\) strikes a stationary mass \(m_2\):

\[v_f = \frac{m_1 u_1}{m_1 + m_2}\]

The total kinetic energy lost in such a collision is the difference between initial and final kinetic energies:

\[\Delta K = K_i - K_f = \frac{1}{2}m_1 u_1^2 - \frac{1}{2}(m_1+m_2)v_f^2\]

Substituting the expression for \(v_f\) gives the standard loss formula:

\[\Delta K = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1+m_2} u_1^2\]

This energy appears as heat, sound, and structural deformation. This mathematical form utilizes the "reduced mass" \( \mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1+m_2} \). Note that this energy loss is always positive — you can never spontaneously gain macroscopic kinetic energy in an inelastic collision.

The Ballistic Pendulum: Application of Inelastic Collisions

A classic physics problem combining perfectly inelastic collisions and mechanical energy conservation is the ballistic pendulum, used to measure projectile speed.

A bullet of mass \(m\) is fired at speed \(v\) into a large wooden block of mass \(M\) suspended by strings. The bullet embeds itself (perfectly inelastic collision). Then the momentum conservation immediately after impact yields the block's initial swing velocity \(V\):

\[mv = (m + M)V \implies V = \frac{mv}{m+M}\]

Following this collision, the block-bullet system swings upward like a pendulum, where its kinetic energy is converted entirely to gravitational potential energy at its maximum height \(h\):

\[\frac{1}{2}(m + M)V^2 = (m + M)gh \implies V = \sqrt{2gh}\]

Combining these allows the calculation of the bullet's original speed:

\[v = \frac{m+M}{m} \sqrt{2gh}\]
Critical Concept: We cannot use energy conservation from the very beginning (bullet to maximum height) because a substantial amount of mechanical energy is dissipated as heat when the bullet buries itself in the wood! Energy is only conserved *after* the collision concludes.
