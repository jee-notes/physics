Work, Power & Energy

The scalar framework for understanding motion, interaction and change.

Conceptual Overview

Work, energy, and power provide a scalar description of classical mechanics.

In Newtonian mechanics, motion is analysed through forces and accelerations at every instant. The energy framework approaches the same physics from a different perspective: instead of tracking how motion changes moment by moment, it focuses on the overall change between two states of a system.

The chapter begins with the concept of work, which quantifies how a force transfers energy to or from a system. This naturally leads to kinetic energy, the energy associated with motion, and potential energy, the energy associated with the configuration of a system.

Together, these ideas lead to the principle of conservation of mechanical energy. Combined with Newton's laws and momentum conservation, energy methods form one of the most powerful tools for analysing mechanical systems.

Physics often advances by introducing new mathematical objects that reveal new ways of studying the same phenomenon.
Newtonian mechanics is built primarily on vector quantities such as force, velocity and acceleration.
The work–energy framework introduces scalar quantities such as work and energy, allowing many mechanical problems to be analysed using scalar algebra rather than vector algebra.
Thus, the energy framework is not a new theory of mechanics, but a new mathematical lens that expands our problem-solving toolkit.

Energy as the Central Idea

Energy is the unifying concept throughout all of physics.

In mechanics, we encounter energy primarily in the form of kinetic and potential energy.

Later, in thermodynamics and other branches of physics, the same concept reappears in broader forms, revealing energy as a quantity that transcends individual topics.

Energy is a scalar quantity, making them easier to manipulate mathematically than vector quantities such as force and acceleration.

Unlike the force-based approach, which analyses motion instant by instant, energy methods often allow us to compare the initial and final states of a system without tracking every intermediate stage of the motion.

This makes energy methods particularly useful for problems involving variable forces, complex trajectories or situations where only the overall change in the system is important.

Like momentum, energy is a property of a physical system. Mechanical interactions (like force exertion) can transfer energy between systems or transform it from one form to another.

In Newtonian mechanics, force changes the momentum of a system. In the work–energy framework, work changes the energy of a system.

These are not competing descriptions; they are complementary ways of analysing the same physical process.

The idea that:
Force \(\rightarrow\) Vector
Energy \(\rightarrow\) Scalar
is analogous to the dual description of fields in electrostatics.
The electric field \( \vec{E} \) (local, vectorial) corresponds to force, while the potential \( V \) (global, scalar) corresponds to energy.

Kinetic and Potential Energy

Mechanical energy appears primarily in two forms: kinetic energy and potential energy.

Kinetic energy \(K\) is associated with the motion of an object, while potential energy \(U\) is associated with the configuration of a system.

\[ K=\frac{1}{2}mv^2 \qquad U=\text{depends on configuration} \]

A moving car possesses kinetic energy because of its motion. A stretched spring or an elevated object possesses potential energy because of its configuration relative to other parts of the system.

Although kinetic and potential energy arise from different physical situations, both represent energy stored within a mechanical system. During motion, energy can continuously transform from one form to the other.

For conservative forces, potential energy can be uniquely defined and the total mechanical energy \[ E=K+U \] remains constant.

Work and the Work–Energy Theorem

Work quantifies the transfer of energy by a force. It is defined as the scalar product of force and displacement:

\[ W = \vec{F}\cdot\vec{s} = Fs\cos\theta \]

The concept of work forms the bridge between force and energy. It allows us to translate the force-based description of mechanics into an energy-based description.

The work–energy theorem is a direct consequence of Newton's second law expressed in scalar form.

It states that the net work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy:

\[ W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K \]

This theorem provides the foundation of the energy framework in mechanics.

Because it remains valid for both constant and variable forces, it applies to a much wider range of situations than the simple expression \(W = Fs\cos\theta\) might suggest.

Mechanical interactions can be described through two complementary frameworks:
Impulse \(\rightarrow\) Change in Momentum
Work \(\rightarrow\) Change in Energy
Just as impulse provides a bridge between force and momentum, work provides a bridge between force and energy.

Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces

A central question in mechanics is whether mechanical energy remains within the mechanical system or is transformed into other forms of energy.

This leads to a fundamental classification of forces into conservative and non-conservative forces.

Conservative Forces

Conservative forces transfer energy between kinetic and potential forms without changing the total mechanical energy of the system.

For such forces, the work done between two points is independent of the path taken. Equivalently, the work done around any closed path is zero.

Because of this property, a unique potential energy function can be defined.

\[ F=-\frac{dU}{dx} \]

Examples include gravitational and spring forces.

Non-Conservative Forces

Non-conservative forces convert mechanical energy into other forms such as heat, sound or internal energy.

For these forces, the work done depends on the path taken. As a result, a unique potential energy function cannot be defined.

Friction is the most common example.

The Key Distinction

  • Conservative forces allow potential energy to be defined.
  • When only conservative forces act, mechanical energy is conserved.
  • Non-conservative forces transform mechanical energy into other forms of energy.
  • Both types obey Newton's laws; the distinction lies in how they affect the energy description of a system.

Core Topics

  • Work — Definition via scalar product; positive, negative, and zero work cases.
  • Work by Variable Forces — Mathematical evaluation of work using integration and area under the \(F\text{–}x\) graph. This focuses on the calculation of work rather than the physical origin of the force.
  • Work Dynamics — Work done by common mechanical forces such as gravity, normal reaction, tension and friction.
  • Kinetic Energy — Energy due to motion; frame dependence.
  • Work–Energy Theorem — Net work equals change in kinetic energy.
  • Potential Energy — Gravitational and elastic; defined for conservative forces.
  • Conservative Forces — Path independence; relationship \( F = -dU/dx \).
  • Conservation of Mechanical Energy — \( K + U = \text{constant} \) when only conservative forces act.
  • Power — Rate of doing work; instantaneous power \( P = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{v} \).
