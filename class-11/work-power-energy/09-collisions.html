Collisions

Short-duration interactions where momentum is always conserved and kinetic energy may or may not be.

What is a Collision?

A collision is a brief, intense interaction between two or more objects during which they exert large forces on each other over a very short time. The key feature is that the impulsive forces during the collision are so large compared to external forces (gravity, friction) that the latter can be neglected during the event.

As a result, the total momentum of the system is conserved across any collision. The total kinetic energy, however, is conserved only in special (elastic) collisions.

Momentum is always conserved in a collision (as long as no significant external impulse acts). Kinetic energy conservation is an additional constraint that separates elastic from inelastic collisions.

Classification of Collisions

1. Elastic Collision

Both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved. No permanent deformation occurs; the objects bounce off each other without any energy loss.

\[\vec{p}_{\text{initial}} = \vec{p}_{\text{final}} \qquad K_{\text{initial}} = K_{\text{final}}\]

Examples: collisions between ideal billiard balls, atomic/molecular collisions in gases.

2. Inelastic Collision

Momentum is conserved, but kinetic energy is not — some is converted to heat, sound, or deformation energy. The objects may or may not stick together.

\[\vec{p}_{\text{initial}} = \vec{p}_{\text{final}} \qquad K_{\text{initial}} > K_{\text{final}}\]

3. Perfectly Inelastic Collision

The most extreme case: the objects stick together and move with a common final velocity. Maximum kinetic energy loss occurs (subject to momentum conservation).

\[(m_1 + m_2)\,v_f = m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2\]
Note: "perfectly inelastic" does not mean "zero final kinetic energy" — it means maximum possible kinetic energy loss while still conserving momentum.

Head-On Elastic Collision: Result Formulae

For a 1D elastic collision between mass \(m_1\) (velocity \(u_1\)) and \(m_2\) (velocity \(u_2\)), solving the two conservation equations simultaneously gives:

\[v_1 = \frac{(m_1 - m_2)u_1 + 2m_2 u_2}{m_1 + m_2}\] \[v_2 = \frac{(m_2 - m_1)u_2 + 2m_1 u_1}{m_1 + m_2}\]

Special Cases

  • Equal masses (\(m_1 = m_2\)): The velocities exchange — \(v_1 = u_2,\; v_2 = u_1\). A striking billiard ball stops and the struck one moves off at the original speed.
  • Very heavy projectile (\(m_1 \gg m_2\)): \(v_1 \approx u_1\) (barely slows down), \(v_2 \approx 2u_1\) (target shoots forward at twice the projectile speed).
  • Very light projectile (\(m_1 \ll m_2\)): \(v_1 \approx -u_1\) (bounces back), \(v_2 \approx 0\) (target barely moves). Example: a ball bouncing off a wall.

Coefficient of Restitution

The coefficient of restitution \(e\) characterises the "bounciness" of a collision. It is defined as the ratio of the relative speed of separation to the relative speed of approach along the line of collision:

\[e = \frac{v_2 - v_1}{u_1 - u_2}\]
  • \(e = 1\) → perfectly elastic collision
  • \(0 < e < 1\) → partially inelastic collision
  • \(e = 0\) → perfectly inelastic collision (objects stick)
The coefficient of restitution is an empirical property of the material pair. It is not derivable from Newton's laws alone — it encapsulates the microscopic physics of deformation and recovery in a single number.

Energy Lost in a Perfectly Inelastic Collision

For a perfectly inelastic collision where \(m_2\) is initially at rest:

\[v_f = \frac{m_1 u_1}{m_1 + m_2}\]

The kinetic energy lost is:

\[\Delta K = \frac{1}{2}m_1 u_1^2 - \frac{1}{2}(m_1+m_2)v_f^2 = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1+m_2} \cdot u_1^2\]

This energy appears as heat, sound, and deformation. It is always positive — you can never gain kinetic energy in a collision.
