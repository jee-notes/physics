Kinetic Energy

The energy possessed by an object by virtue of its motion.

What is Kinetic Energy?

Kinetic energy is the energy that an object possesses due to its state of motion. Any object with mass that is in motion has kinetic energy. It is always non-negative, because it depends on the square of the speed.

For an object of mass \(m\) moving with speed \(v\), the kinetic energy is:

\[K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2\]

The unit of kinetic energy is the Joule (J), the same as the unit of work — as it must be, since work can be converted into kinetic energy.

Kinetic energy depends on the square of the speed. Doubling the speed quadruples the kinetic energy. This is why high-speed collisions are so much more destructive than low-speed ones.

Derivation from Work

The kinetic energy formula is not postulated — it is derived directly from Newton's second law and the definition of work. Consider an object of mass \(m\) initially at rest, acted upon by a constant net force \(F\) over a displacement \(s\):

From Newton's second law: \(F = ma\). Using kinematics: \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\), so \(as = \frac{v^2 - u^2}{2}\).

The work done by the net force is:

\[W = Fs = (ma)s = m \cdot as = m \cdot \frac{v^2 - u^2}{2} = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - \frac{1}{2}mu^2\]

This is the work–energy theorem: the work done equals the change in kinetic energy. The kinetic energy at speed \(v\) is therefore \(K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2\).

This derivation assumed constant force for simplicity. The same result holds for variable forces when integration is used — the work–energy theorem is a general result, not limited to constant forces.

Kinetic Energy and Momentum

Kinetic energy and momentum \(p = mv\) are related. Expressing kinetic energy in terms of momentum:

\[K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{(mv)^2}{2m} = \frac{p^2}{2m}\]

This relation is extremely useful in collision problems and quantum mechanics. It shows that for two objects with the same momentum, the lighter one has more kinetic energy.

  • Same momentum, different mass → different kinetic energy: \(K \propto \frac{1}{m}\)
  • Same kinetic energy, different mass → different momentum: \(p \propto \sqrt{m}\)

Frame Dependence

Kinetic energy is a frame-dependent quantity. The same object can have different kinetic energies as observed from different inertial frames, because speed changes with the choice of reference frame.

For example, a ball thrown at \(10\,\text{m/s}\) in the forward direction from a train moving at \(20\,\text{m/s}\) has speed \(30\,\text{m/s}\) relative to the ground frame and speed \(10\,\text{m/s}\) relative to the train frame. The kinetic energies differ by a factor of 9.

Unlike charge in electrostatics, kinetic energy is not Lorentz-invariant. However, within a given inertial frame, the work–energy theorem holds exactly, making internal consistency guaranteed as long as we fix the reference frame.
