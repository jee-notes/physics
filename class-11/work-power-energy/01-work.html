Work

The bridge between force and energy — how a force produces a change in the state of motion.

What is Work?

In everyday language, "work" means any physical or mental effort. In physics, work has a precise, quantitative meaning: work is done by a force when it causes a displacement of the object it acts on, and the two are not perpendicular to each other.

Work is a scalar quantity — it has magnitude but no direction. It can be positive, negative, or zero depending on the angle between the force and the displacement.

Work is not a property of an object; it is a quantity associated with a force acting over a displacement. Different forces acting on the same object do different amounts of work.

Definition: Constant Force

When a constant force \(\vec{F}\) acts on a body that undergoes a displacement \(\vec{s}\), the work done by that force is defined as the scalar (dot) product of the force and the displacement:

\[W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{s} = Fs\cos\theta\]

Here, \(\theta\) is the angle between the direction of the force and the direction of the displacement. \(F\) and \(s\) are the magnitudes of \(\vec{F}\) and \(\vec{s}\) respectively.

The SI unit of work is the Joule (J), where \(1\,\text{J} = 1\,\text{N} \cdot \text{m}\).

Positive, Negative, and Zero Work

The sign of work conveys physical meaning:

  • Positive Work (\(\theta < 90°\)): The force has a component along the displacement. The force aids the motion and transfers energy to the object. Example: a person pushing a box in the direction it moves.
  • Negative Work (\(90° < \theta \leq 180°\)): The force has a component opposing the displacement. The force removes energy from the object. Example: friction acting on a sliding block.
  • Zero Work (\(\theta = 90°\)): The force is perpendicular to the displacement. No energy transfer occurs. Example: the normal reaction on a horizontally moving body, or the centripetal force on an object in uniform circular motion.
A force that does no work does not mean the force is absent. The centripetal force continuously acts on a satellite in circular orbit, yet it does zero work because it is always perpendicular to the velocity.

Work Done by Multiple Forces

When several forces act simultaneously on an object, the work done by each force is computed separately using \(W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{s}\). The total (net) work done on the object is the algebraic sum of works done by all individual forces:

\[W_{\text{net}} = W_1 + W_2 + \cdots + W_n\]

Alternatively, since superposition holds, we may first find the net force and then compute its work:

\[W_{\text{net}} = \vec{F}_{\text{net}} \cdot \vec{s}\]
Both approaches are equivalent when the displacement is the same for all force applications (i.e., a single rigid body). When computing individual force contributions, always use the full displacement vector, not just its component.

Work Along a Curved Path

The definition \(W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{s}\) applies only when both the force and the displacement are constant (in magnitude and direction). In general, along a curved path, we must break the path into infinitesimal segments \(d\vec{r}\) and sum the contributions:

\[W = \int_{\vec{r}_1}^{\vec{r}_2} \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r}\]

This is the general definition of work, of which the constant-force formula is a special case. The integral reduces to \(Fs\cos\theta\) when \(\vec{F}\) is constant throughout the displacement.
