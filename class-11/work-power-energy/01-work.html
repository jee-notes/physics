Work

The bridge between force and energy — how a force produces a change in the state of motion.

Understanding Work

In everyday language, "work" usually refers to physical or mental effort. However, Physics requires precise definitions for physical quantities such as work, pressure, stress, strain, wave and energy.

Science and common sense are not always the same.
For example, we commonly say that the Sun rises in the East.
Scientifically, the apparent motion of the Sun is caused by Earth's rotation from West to East.
The claim that the Sun rises in the East is perfectly valid as per common sense but is scientifically inaccurate.
Similarly, the scientific meaning of "work" is more precise than its everyday meaning.

Definition of Work

As per Physics, work is associated with a force that is acting on an object (particle body) while the object undergoes displacement.

If a force \(\vec{F}\) acts on an object and the object undergoes an infinitesimal displacement \(d\vec{s}\), then the infinitesimal work done by the force on the object is defined as:

\[ dW = \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{s} \]

Work is a scalar quantity. It can be positive, negative or zero depending on the angle between the force and the displacement.

Work is not a property of the object alone.
It is a quantity associated with a force acting over a displacement.
Different forces acting on the same object can do different amounts of work.

Why Study Work?

Defining a new physical quantity is not enough. We must also understand why that quantity is useful or "Why are we putting an effort to define Work?".

The importance of work becomes clear when we introduce another quantity called Energy.

The central result of this chapter is the Work-Energy Theorem, which establishes a relationship between work done on a body and the change in its kinetic energy.

Therefore, work is not studied in isolation. It is introduced because it provides a powerful way to understand and calculate changes in energy.

Just as successive displacements accumulate to determine the final position of a body, successive amounts of work accumulate to determine the change in its energy. This idea forms the foundation of the Work-Energy Theorem studied later in this chapter.

Position is a state quantity that changes through accumulated displacements. Similarly, energy is a state quantity that changes through accumulated work. This connection forms the foundation of the Work-Energy Theorem studied later in this chapter.

Measuring Work

Constant Force and Displacement

To understand work quantitatively, let us begin with the simplest case: a constant force acting on a body that undergoes displacement.

If a constant force \(\vec{F}\) acts on a body and the body undergoes a displacement \(\vec{s}\), then the work done by the force is defined as the dot product of the force and the displacement:

\[ W = \vec{F}\cdot\vec{s} \]

Using the definition of the dot product:

\[ W = Fs\cos\theta \]

where \(\theta\) is the angle between the force and the displacement.

For the special case where force and displacement are in the same direction:

\[ \theta = 0^\circ \] \[ W = Fs \]

Thus, work depends not only on the magnitudes of force and displacement, but also on the angle between them.

Units and Dimensions

From the relation \(W = Fs\), the SI unit of work is:

\[ 1\,\text{J} = 1\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m} \]

The SI unit of work is called the Joule (J).

The dimensions of work are:

\[ [M\,L^2\,T^{-2}] \]

A useful way to visualize one joule is to imagine lifting a 100 g object vertically through a height of 1 m at a steady speed. The work done against gravity is approximately 1 J.

Positive, Negative and Zero Work

The sign of work carries important physical meaning because work is related to energy transfer.

  • Positive Work (\(\theta \lt 90^\circ\)): The force has a component in the direction of displacement. Energy is transferred to the object.
  • Negative Work (\(90^\circ \lt \theta \le 180^\circ\)): The force has a component opposite to the displacement. Energy is removed from the object.
  • Zero Work (\(\theta = 90^\circ\)): The force is perpendicular to the displacement. No energy transfer occurs through that force.
The sign of (non-zero) work depends only on the angle between force and displacement.
Since \[ dW = \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{s} = |\vec{F}| \cdot |d\vec{s}| \cdot \cos{\theta} \] Work is positive when force and displacement point in similar directions, negative when they point in opposite directions, and zero when they are perpendicular.
Thus, a force doing zero work does not necessarily imply that the force is absent (\(F = 0\)) or displacement is absent (\(ds = 0\) or \(v = 0\)).
For example, the centripetal force acting on a satellite continuously changes the direction of its velocity, yet it does no work because it is always perpendicular to the displacement.
In this case, \(F \neq 0\) , \(v \neq 0\) but still \(W = 0\)

Work Done by Multiple Forces

When several forces act simultaneously on an object, the work done by each force is computed separately using \(W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{s}\). The total (net) work done on the object is the algebraic sum of works done by all individual forces:

\[ W_{\text{net}} = W_1 + W_2 + \cdots + W_n \]

Alternatively, since superposition holds, we may first find the net force and then compute its work:

\[ W_{\text{net}} = \vec{F}_{\text{net}} \cdot \vec{s} \]
Both approaches are equivalent when the displacement is the same for all force applications (i.e., a single rigid body). When computing individual force contributions, always use the full displacement vector, not just its component.

Work Along a Curved Path

The relation

\[ W=\vec{F}\cdot\vec{s} \]

is valid only when the force remains constant throughout the motion.

In many situations, the force may change in magnitude, direction or both. The object itself may also move along a curved path rather than a straight line.

To calculate work in such cases, we divide the motion into infinitesimally small displacements \(d\vec{r}\). Over each small segment, the work done is:

\[ dW=\vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} \]

The total work done is obtained by adding the contributions from all segments along the path:

\[ W=\int_{\vec r_1}^{\vec r_2}\vec F\cdot d\vec r \]
This is the most general definition of work.
Just as a curved path can be viewed as a collection of infinitesimal displacements \(d\vec r\), the total work done can be viewed as the accumulation of infinitesimal works \(dW\).

When the force remains constant throughout the displacement, the integral simplifies to:

\[ W=\vec{F}\cdot\vec{s} \]

Thus, the familiar constant-force formula is a special case of the more general integral definition.
