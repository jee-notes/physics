Work by a Variable Force

When force changes with position, integration replaces simple multiplication.

Why Integration is Required

The formula \(W = Fs\cos\theta\) assumes that the force is constant in both magnitude and direction throughout the displacement. In many physical situations — a spring being compressed, a rocket burning fuel, a charge moving through a non-uniform field — the force varies from point to point along the path.

In such cases, we divide the entire path into infinitesimally small segments. Over each segment \(dx\), the force \(F(x)\) is effectively constant, and the work done over that segment is \(dW = F(x)\,dx\). Summing over all segments gives:

\[W = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} F(x)\,dx\]
This integral is the general definition of work in one dimension. For motion in 3D along a curve, the analogous expression is \(W = \int \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r}\).

Graphical Interpretation: Area Under the F–x Graph

The integral \(\int_{x_1}^{x_2} F(x)\,dx\) represents the area enclosed between the \(F\text{–}x\) curve, the \(x\)-axis, and the vertical lines at \(x = x_1\) and \(x = x_2\).

  • If the curve lies above the \(x\)-axis (force in the direction of displacement), the work is positive.
  • If the curve lies below the \(x\)-axis (force opposing displacement), the work is negative.
  • The net work is the algebraic sum of all such areas.
Graphical methods are extremely useful in JEE problems where \(F(x)\) is given as a piecewise linear function or a simple shape. Count the area above the axis as positive and below as negative.

The Spring Force: A Key Example

A spring obeying Hooke's Law exerts a restoring force proportional to its extension or compression:

\[F = -kx\]

Here, \(k\) is the spring constant (stiffness) and \(x\) is the displacement from the natural (unstretched) length. The negative sign indicates the force is always directed back towards the equilibrium position.

The work done by the spring force when the spring is compressed or stretched by \(x\) from its natural length is:

\[W_{\text{spring}} = \int_0^x (-kx')\,dx' = -\frac{1}{2}kx^2\]

The work done by an external agent stretching the spring from 0 to \(x\) is:

\[W_{\text{external}} = +\frac{1}{2}kx^2\]
The work done by the spring force is always negative when the spring is being deformed from its natural length, because the spring force opposes the displacement. This energy is stored as elastic potential energy.

General Work by Spring Between Two Positions

When a spring moves from compression/extension \(x_1\) to \(x_2\), the work done by the spring force is:

\[W_{\text{spring}} = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} (-kx)\,dx = \frac{1}{2}kx_1^2 - \frac{1}{2}kx_2^2\]

This equals the decrease in the spring's potential energy, which is the defining property of a conservative force.

Important Observations

  • Work depends on the path and the force law — for non-conservative forces, the same starting and ending positions can yield different work for different paths.
  • For a linear force like the spring, the \(F\text{–}x\) graph is a straight line through the origin, and the area is a triangle: \(W = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}\).
  • When the displacement is in the same direction as the axis and the force is one-dimensional, the vector integral reduces to the scalar integral shown above.
