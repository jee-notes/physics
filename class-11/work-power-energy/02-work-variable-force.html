Dot Product Math for Work

Dot Product Integration.

Vector Algebra of Work

The expression

\[ W=\vec F\cdot\vec s \]

gives the work done when the force remains constant throughout the displacement.

In many physical situations, however, the force changes from point to point and this situation is called as variable force. Force is different at different locations in space.

Examples include a spring being compressed, a charge moving through a non-uniform field, or an object moving through a varying gravitational field.

For variable force situation, the displacement is divided into infinitesimally small segments \(dx\). Over each segment, the force can be treated as approximately constant, allowing the work done over that segment to be written as:

\[ dW = F(x)\,dx \]

The total work is obtained by summing the work done over all infinitesimal segments:

\[ W = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} F(x)\,dx \]

Thus, integration does not replace the dot product. The dot product continues to determine the work done on each infinitesimal segment, while integration merely adds the contributions from all segments along the path.

Work by a variable force is still fundamentally a dot-product calculation.
Dot Product \(\rightarrow\) Work on an infinitesimal segment
Integration \(\rightarrow\) Sum of all infinitesimal work contributions
Calculus provides the summation tool; the underlying physical operation remains the vector dot product.

Dot Product in One and Three Dimensions

The expression

\[ dW = F(x)\,dx \]

may appear different from the familiar dot-product form

\[ dW = \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} \]

However, they represent the same physical operation.

In one-dimensional motion, force and displacement can only be along the positive or negative direction of a single axis.

As a result, the directional information is carried by the signs of \(F(x)\) and \(dx\).

Only two relative orientations are possible:

  • Same direction \(\rightarrow\) positive work
  • Opposite direction \(\rightarrow\) negative work

In three dimensions, infinitely many angles from \(0^\circ\) to \(180^\circ\) are possible between \(\vec F\) and \(d\vec r\), so the dot product must be written explicitly:

\[ dW = \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} \]

Thus,

\[ dW = F(x)\,dx \]

is simply the one-dimensional form of the vector dot product.

Variable Force \(F(x)\)

When we write a force as a function of position, such as the spring force

\[ F(x)=-kx \]

many students interpret it as a scalar equation. However, it is actually a one-dimensional vector equation.

The displacement coordinate \(x\) is a signed quantity. Its magnitude gives the distance from equilibrium, while its sign specifies the direction.

Since the force depends on \(x\), both the magnitude and direction of the force are determined by the magnitude and sign of \(x\).

For example, if \(x=2\text{ m}\), the object is located 2 m on the positive side of the chosen axis (lets say upwards).

The spring force is then \(F = -2k\)

That is actually a vector equation and it means that the force has magnitude \(2k\) and acts in the opposite direction of displacement (that is downwards).

The sign carries the directional information.

To make the vector nature explicit, let the positive direction be represented by the unit vector \(\hat{i}\). The spring force can then be written as

\[ \vec{F}=-kx\,\hat{i} \text{ (same as: } F(x) = -kx \text{ )} \]

Consider a general infinitesimal displacement along the same axis:

\[ d\vec{x}=dx\,\hat{i}. \]

The general variable quantity \(dx\) is signed (its a 1D vector variable). A positive value represents displacement along \(+\hat{i}\), while a negative value represents displacement along \(-\hat{i}\).

The infinitesimal work done by the force is

\[ dW = \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{x} = -kx\hat{i} \cdot dx\hat{i} = -kxdx = F(x)dx \]

Thus,

\[ dW = F(x)\,dx \]

is simply the one-dimensional form of the vector dot product. The vector nature has not disappeared; it is encoded in the signs of \(F(x)\) and \(dx\).

The sign of \(dx\) determines the direction of an infinitesimal displacement. The overall direction of motion is reflected in the limits of integration, which specify the initial and final positions of the motion.

Graphical Interpretation: Area Under the F–x Graph

The integral \(\int_{x_1}^{x_2} F(x)\,dx\) represents the area enclosed between the \(F\text{–}x\) curve, the \(x\)-axis, and the vertical lines at \(x = x_1\) and \(x = x_2\).

  • If the curve lies above the \(x\)-axis (force in the direction of displacement), the work is positive.
  • If the curve lies below the \(x\)-axis (force opposing displacement), the work is negative.
  • The net work is the algebraic sum of all such areas.
Graphical methods are extremely useful in JEE problems where \(F(x)\) is given as a piecewise linear function or a simple shape. Count the area above the axis as positive and below as negative.

The Spring Force: A Key Example

A spring obeying Hooke's Law exerts a restoring force proportional to its extension or compression:

\[F = -kx\]

Here, \(k\) is the spring constant (stiffness) and \(x\) is the displacement from the natural (unstretched) length. The negative sign indicates the force is always directed back towards the equilibrium position.

The work done by the spring force when the spring is compressed or stretched by \(x\) from its natural length is:

\[W_{\text{spring}} = \int_0^x (-kx')\,dx' = -\frac{1}{2}kx^2\]

The work done by an external agent stretching the spring from 0 to \(x\) is:

\[W_{\text{external}} = +\frac{1}{2}kx^2\]
The work done by the spring force is always negative when the spring is being deformed from its natural length, because the spring force opposes the displacement. This energy is stored as elastic potential energy.

General Work by Spring Between Two Positions

When a spring moves from compression/extension \(x_1\) to \(x_2\), the work done by the spring force is:

\[W_{\text{spring}} = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} (-kx)\,dx = \frac{1}{2}kx_1^2 - \frac{1}{2}kx_2^2\]

This equals the decrease in the spring's potential energy, which is the defining property of a conservative force.

Important Observations

  • Work depends on the path and the force law — for non-conservative forces, the same starting and ending positions can yield different work for different paths.
  • For a linear force like the spring, the \(F\text{–}x\) graph is a straight line through the origin, and the area is a triangle: \(W = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}\).
  • When the displacement is in the same direction as the axis and the force is one-dimensional, the vector integral reduces to the scalar integral shown above.
