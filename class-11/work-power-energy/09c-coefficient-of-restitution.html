Coefficient of Restitution

A metric defining the "bounciness" of a collision, characterizing inelasticity.

Coefficient of Restitution (\(e\))

The coefficient of restitution \(e\) is a dimensionless quantity that characterizes the degree of elasticity in a collision. It is defined as the ratio of the relative speed of separation after collision to the relative speed of approach before collision along the line of impact:

\[e = \frac{v_2 - v_1}{u_1 - u_2}\]
  • \(e = 1\) → perfectly elastic collision (no kinetic energy loss).
  • \(0 < e < 1\) → partially inelastic collision.
  • \(e = 0\) → perfectly inelastic collision (objects stick together, relative speed of separation is zero).
The coefficient of restitution is an empirical property of the material pair interacting. It is not derivable from Newton's laws alone — it encapsulates the microscopic physics of structural deformation and recovery in a single number.

Fractional Loss of Kinetic Energy

In a collision characterized by coefficient of restitution \(e\), the kinetic energy lost can be computed effectively. For a head-on collision between a mass \(m_1\) and a stationary mass \(m_2\) (\(u_2 = 0\)), the fractional loss of kinetic energy is:

\[\frac{\Delta K}{K_i} = \frac{(1 - e^2)m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\]

Notice that for a perfectly elastic collision (\(e = 1\)), the fractional loss is exactly zero. For a completely inelastic collision (\(e = 0\)), the fraction lost simplifies to \(\frac{m_2}{m_1+m_2}\).

Application: Bouncing Ball

A very common application of the coefficient of restitution is determining the rebound height of a ball dropped onto a hard horizontal surface (like a floor, where the floor's mass \(M \to \infty\)).

If a ball is dropped from a height \(h\), its speed just before impact is \(v = \sqrt{2gh}\). The floor is stationary, so the relative speed of approach is \(v\).

After the collision, the ball rebounds with speed \(v' = ev\). It will rise to a new height \(h'\):

\[h' = \frac{(v')^2}{2g} = \frac{(ev)^2}{2g} = e^2 \frac{v^2}{2g} = e^2 h\]

After \(n\) bounces, the height reached by the ball is given by:

\[h_n = e^{2n} h\]
Limiting Case: Since \(e < 1\) for real balls, the sequence of heights \(h, e^2h, e^4h \dots\) forms a geometric progression that eventually tends to zero as the macroscopic energy dissipates into heat.
