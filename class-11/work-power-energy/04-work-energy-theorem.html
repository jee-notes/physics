Work–Energy Theorem

Newton's second law recast in scalar form: net work equals the change in kinetic energy.

Statement

The work–energy theorem states that the net work done on a body by all forces acting on it equals the change in the kinetic energy of the body:

\[W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K = K_f - K_i = \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2 - \frac{1}{2}mv_i^2\]

Here, \(W_{\text{net}}\) is the total work done by all forces (conservative, non-conservative, normal, tension, etc.) acting on the body. This is a result of Newton's second law expressed in the language of energy.

The theorem involves net work — work done by every force on the body. If you include all forces correctly, this relation is exact and requires no assumptions about the nature of the forces (constant or variable) or the path.

Proof for Variable Force

Starting from Newton's second law along the direction of motion:

\[F_{\text{net}} = ma = m\frac{dv}{dt}\]

Multiply both sides by \(v = \frac{dx}{dt}\):

\[F_{\text{net}} \cdot v = mv\frac{dv}{dt}\]

Integrating both sides with respect to time from \(t_1\) to \(t_2\):

\[\int_{t_1}^{t_2} F_{\text{net}} \cdot v\,dt = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} mv\frac{dv}{dt}\,dt\]

The left side is \(\int F_{\text{net}}\,dx = W_{\text{net}}\); the right side evaluates to \(\frac{1}{2}mv_2^2 - \frac{1}{2}mv_1^2\). Hence:

\[W_{\text{net}} = \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2 - \frac{1}{2}mv_i^2\]

Key Points of Application

  • All forces count: Include gravity, normal force, friction, tension, applied force — everything. Forces that do zero work (e.g., normal force on a horizontal surface) contribute 0 and can be identified and dropped without affecting the result.
  • Path independent from the theorem's perspective: The theorem relates the states at the beginning and end. You do not need to know the intermediate details of the path.
  • No direction involved: Because work and kinetic energy are scalars, this theorem involves no vector addition. This is its principal advantage over Newton's second law in vector form.
  • Valid for variable forces: The derivation above holds for any force, constant or not.
The work–energy theorem is Newton's second law in disguise. It contains exactly the same physics, but rearranged to relate two scalar quantities at two instants rather than a vector equation at every instant.

When to Use It

The work–energy theorem is the preferred approach when:

  • You need to find the speed at a certain position, given the forces acting.
  • Forces are variable (e.g., spring force, gravity on a curved path).
  • The path is complex, but only the endpoints matter.
  • You want to find the work done by one particular force, knowing the others.

It is not directly useful when you need the time of motion or the force at a specific instant — for those, Newton's second law in its original form is required.

Extension: Including Non-Conservative Work

When separating conservative and non-conservative work, the theorem can be rewritten as:

\[W_{\text{conservative}} + W_{\text{non-conservative}} = \Delta K\]

Since \(W_{\text{conservative}} = -\Delta U\) (work by a conservative force equals the decrease in potential energy), this gives:

\[W_{\text{non-conservative}} = \Delta K + \Delta U = \Delta E_{\text{mech}}\]

This is the generalised form of the energy conservation principle: non-conservative work (e.g., friction) equals the change in total mechanical energy.
