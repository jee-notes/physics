Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces

The fundamental classification of forces based on whether their work depends on path or only on endpoints.

The Central Question

When a force acts on an object moving from point A to point B, the work done can depend in general on the path taken. In some special cases, however, the work is the same regardless of the path. This distinction has profound physical consequences.

Forces for which the work done between two points is independent of the path are called conservative forces. All other forces are non-conservative.

Conservative Forces

A force is conservative if and only if:

  • The work done by it between any two points is independent of the path taken.
  • Equivalently, the work done by it along any closed path is zero: \(\oint \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r} = 0\)
  • Equivalently, the force can be expressed as the negative gradient of a scalar potential energy function: \(\vec{F} = -\nabla U\).

In one dimension, this reduces to \(F = -\dfrac{dU}{dx}\).

All three definitions are mathematically equivalent. The fundamental reason for conservative behaviour is that the force field is derivable from a scalar — there is an underlying potential energy landscape.

Examples of Conservative Forces

  • Gravitational force (near earth and universal)
  • Spring (elastic) force — Hooke's law
  • Electrostatic force (Coulomb's law)

Non-Conservative Forces

A force is non-conservative if the work done by it depends on the path taken, not just the endpoints. Equivalently, the work done around a closed path is non-zero:

\[\oint \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r} \neq 0\]

The "lost" mechanical energy is irreversibly converted into other forms (heat, sound, deformation), which cannot be recovered as mechanical energy. No potential energy function can be defined for such forces.

Examples of Non-Conservative Forces

  • Kinetic friction — always opposes relative motion, so more work is done for longer paths.
  • Air resistance (drag) — same reason as friction.
  • Normal force on a rough inclined plane (when friction is also present).
Friction always does negative work (or zero, if the surface is stationary and the object doesn't move). The magnitude of this negative work increases with path length — which is the hallmark that distinguishes it from conservative forces.

The F = −dU/dx Relation

For a conservative force in one dimension, the force at any point equals the negative rate of change of potential energy with position:

\[F = -\frac{dU}{dx}\]

This is the key link between force (a vector concept) and potential energy (a scalar function). It means:

  • Where \(U\) is steep (large \(|dU/dx|\)), the force is large.
  • Where \(U\) is flat (\(dU/dx = 0\)), the force is zero — this is an equilibrium point.
  • The force always points in the direction of decreasing \(U\).

In three dimensions: \(\vec{F} = -\nabla U = -\left(\dfrac{\partial U}{\partial x}\hat{i} + \dfrac{\partial U}{\partial y}\hat{j} + \dfrac{\partial U}{\partial z}\hat{k}\right)\).

Practical Summary

  • Conservative forces: work depends only on endpoints → potential energy definable → mechanical energy conserved.
  • Non-conservative forces: work depends on path → no potential energy → mechanical energy not conserved.
  • The work–energy theorem applies to both; only the energy conservation principle is restricted to conservative forces.
