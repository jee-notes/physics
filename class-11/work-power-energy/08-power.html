Power

The rate of doing work — how quickly energy is transferred or converted.

Definition

Power is the rate at which work is done, or equivalently, the rate at which energy is transferred or converted. A powerful machine does the same amount of work as a less powerful one, but in less time.

The SI unit of power is the Watt (W), where \(1\,\text{W} = 1\,\text{J/s} = 1\,\text{kg\,m}^2\,\text{s}^{-3}\).

Power describes how fast work is done, not how much. A human climbing stairs and an elevator doing the same job have the same total work, but the elevator delivers far greater power because it completes it faster.

Average Power

Average power over a time interval \(\Delta t\) is the total work \(W\) done divided by the elapsed time:

\[P_{\text{avg}} = \frac{W}{\Delta t}\]

This gives the average rate of energy transfer, smoothed out over the interval. It does not capture how the power varies within the interval.

Instantaneous Power

Instantaneous power is the limit of average power as \(\Delta t \to 0\):

\[P = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta W}{\Delta t} = \frac{dW}{dt}\]

Since \(dW = \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r}\), dividing by \(dt\):

\[P = \vec{F} \cdot \frac{d\vec{r}}{dt} = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{v}\]

This is the most useful form: instantaneous power equals the dot product of the force and velocity vectors at that instant.

In component form: \(P = Fv\cos\alpha\), where \(\alpha\) is the angle between the force and velocity. When force and velocity are parallel (e.g., a car accelerating on a straight road), \(P = Fv\).

Power and Efficiency

No machine is perfectly efficient. Some of the input power is always dissipated as heat or other losses. The efficiency \(\eta\) of a machine is:

\[\eta = \frac{P_{\text{output}}}{P_{\text{input}}} \times 100\%\]

An efficiency of 80% means that 80% of the input power is converted to useful output, and 20% is wasted.

Practical Units and Orders of Magnitude

  • 1 horsepower (hp) = 746 W ≈ 0.75 kW (unit used for engines)
  • 1 kWh (kilowatt-hour) = \(3.6 \times 10^6\,\text{J}\) — the commercial unit of electrical energy
  • Human body at rest: ~80 W; during intense exercise: ~500–1000 W
  • A 100 W bulb converts 100 J of electrical energy to light and heat every second
The kilowatt-hour is a unit of energy, not power — it equals 1000 W sustained for 3600 seconds. Confusing power and energy is a common error: power is the rate, energy is the total amount.

Key Distinctions

  • Large force does not imply large power — if the velocity is small, power is small.
  • Large displacement does not imply large power — if the time is long, average power is small.
  • Zero displacement in \(dt\) means zero work in \(dt\), so \(P = 0\) for a stationary force application (even if the force is huge, e.g., pushing a wall).
