Theorem vs Laws

Relating Mathematical terms in Physics.

Two New Physical Quantities

This chapter introduces two important physical quantities:

  • Work
  • Energy

Although this chapter also introduces a third quantity called Power, our primary focus will be on Work and Energy.

This is because these quantities play roles similar to quantities that we encountered earlier in Newton's Laws of Motion:

  • Work ↔ Impulse
  • Energy ↔ Momentum

In the previous chapter, the Impulse-Momentum Theorem allowed us to relate impulse with the change in momentum of a body.

Similarly, in this chapter, the Work-Energy Theorem will allow us to relate work done on a body with the change in its kinetic energy.

Just as a change in momentum indicates that an impulse has acted on a body, a change in kinetic energy indicates that work has been done on the body.

The goal of this chapter is to develop this relationship between Work and Energy, just as Newton's Laws helped us develop the relationship between Impulse and Momentum.

What is a Theorem?

In Mathematics, a theorem is a statement that can be derived from a set of axioms using logical reasoning.

For example, the Pythagoras Theorem and Binomial Theorem are derived from more fundamental mathematical principles.

Physics has a similar hierarchy. Instead of axioms, Physics begins with laws that are established through experiments and observations.

From these laws, we can derive many useful results called theorems.

Examples of theorems in Physics include:

  • Impulse-Momentum Theorem
  • Work-Energy Theorem
  • Parallel Axis Theorem
  • Perpendicular Axis Theorem
  • Shell Theorem
  • Gauss Theorem

In this chapter, we study the Work-Energy Theorem. However, its source law is the same as that of the Impulse-Momentum Theorem. Both are mathematical consequences of Newton's Second Law.

Newton's Second Law

Newton's Second Law relates force to the rate of change of momentum:

\[ \vec F = \frac{d\vec p}{dt} \]

For a particle of constant mass, this becomes:

\[ \vec F = m\vec a \]

In one-dimensional motion, we often write:

\[ F = ma \]

From Kinematics, acceleration can be expressed in two equivalent forms:

\[ a = \frac{dv}{dt} \] \[ a = v\frac{dv}{dx} \] Substituting these into Newton's Second Law gives: \[ F = m\frac{dv}{dt} \] or \[ F = mv\frac{dv}{dx} \]

Integrating the same law with different variables produces different theorems.

Impulse-Momentum Theorem

\[ F = m\frac{dv}{dt} \] \[ \int F\,dt = m\int dv \] \[ \int F\,dt = m(v_f-v_i) \]

The impulse delivered by a force equals the change in momentum.

Work-Energy Theorem

\[ F = mv\frac{dv}{dx} \] \[ \int F\,dx = m\int v\,dv \] \[ \int F\,dx = \frac12 m(v_f^2-v_i^2) \]

The work done by a force equals the change in kinetic energy.

Notice that both theorems originate from the same physical law. The difference arises only from the quantity chosen for integration.

Integrating with respect to time produces the Impulse-Momentum Theorem, while integrating with respect to displacement produces the Work-Energy Theorem.

Impact on Sign

Although Work-Energy and Impulse-Momentum are closely related, there is an important difference in how signs appear in the two frameworks.

The Impulse-Momentum Theorem is:

\[ J = \int F\,dt \] Notice that time always moves forward, so: \[ dt > 0 \]

As a result, the sign of an infinitesimal impulse \(dJ = F\,dt\) is determined entirely by the sign of the force.

The Work-Energy Theorem is: \[ W = \int F\,dx \] Unlike time, displacement does not have a preferred direction. \[ dx > 0 \] or \[ dx \lt 0 \] are both possible.

Therefore, the sign of an infinitesimal work \(dW = F\,dx\) depends on both the force and the displacement.

If \(u = x\,y\), then the sign of \(u\) depends on the sign of both \(x\) and \(y\).
Similarly, \(dW = F\,dx\), so the sign of work depends on both force and displacement.

When force and displacement are in the same direction, work is positive and kinetic energy increases.

When force and displacement are in opposite directions, work is negative and kinetic energy decreases.

This additional dependence on displacement is one of the major differences between the Impulse-Momentum and Work-Energy frameworks.
