Theorem vs Laws

Relating Mathematical terms in Physics.

Concept

In Mathematics, a theorem is a statement that can be derived (or proved) from a set of axioms, definitions and previously established results using logical reasoning.

For example, the Pythagoras Theorem and Binomial Theorem are derived from more fundamental mathematical principles.

As Physics adopts mathematics as a medium to express itself, it follows a similar hierarchy.

However, in Physics, the starting points for deriving results are not limited to mathematical axioms. They include physical laws and principles, definitions of physical quantities, and assumptions of the physical model being used.

Physical laws and principles are formulated on the basis of experiments and observations and are tested against them.

By applying these laws, principles and definitions through mathematical reasoning, we can derive many useful results called theorems.

Examples of theorems in Physics include:

  • Impulse-Momentum Theorem
  • Work-Energy Theorem
  • Parallel Axis Theorem
  • Perpendicular Axis Theorem
  • Shell Theorem

In this chapter, we study the Work-Energy Theorem. Its source law is the same as that of the Impulse-Momentum Theorem. Both are mathematical consequences of Newton's Second Law.

Two New Physical Quantities

Till last chapter, we learned about new physical quantities like Force, inertia, impulse and momentum.

We studied the impulse-momentum theorem as derived from Newton's Second Law of Motion:

The Impulse-Momentum Theorem allowed us to relate impulse on a body (\(\int_{t_i}^{t_f} \vec{F} \, dt\)) with the change in its momentum (\(\Delta \,\vec{p}\)).

This chapter introduces two important physical quantities:

  • Work
  • Energy

Although this chapter also introduces a third quantity called Power, our primary focus will be on Work and Energy.

This is because Work and Energy play a role mathematically analogous to Impulse and Momentum from Newton's Laws of Motion:

  • Work ↔ Impulse
  • Energy ↔ Momentum

The Work-Energy Theorem relates the net work done on a body (\(\int_{x_i}^{x_f} F_x \, dx\)) to the change in its kinetic energy (\(\Delta \,\text{KE}\)).

Newton's Second Law

Newton's Second Law relates force to the rate of change of momentum:

\[ \vec F = \frac{d\vec p}{dt} \]

For a particle of constant mass, this becomes \(\vec F = m\vec a\) .

From Kinematics, acceleration can be expressed in two equivalent forms:

\[ a = \frac{dv}{dt} \qquad a = v\frac{dv}{dx} \]

Substituting these into Newton's Second Law gives:

\[ F = m\frac{dv}{dt} \qquad F = mv\frac{dv}{dx} \]

Integrating the same law with different variables produces different theorems.

Impulse-Momentum Theorem

\[ F = m\frac{dv}{dt} \] \[ \int F\,dt = m\int dv \] \[ \int F\,dt = m(v_f-v_i) = \Delta p \]

The impulse delivered by a force equals the change in momentum.

Work-Energy Theorem

\[ F = mv\frac{dv}{dx} \] \[ \int F\,dx = m\int v\,dv \] \[ \int F\,dx = \frac12 m(v_f^2-v_i^2) = \Delta \text{KE} \]

The work done by a force equals the change in kinetic energy.

Notice that both theorems originate from the same physical law. The difference arises only from the quantity chosen for integration.

Integrating with respect to time produces the Impulse-Momentum Theorem, while integrating with respect to displacement produces the Work-Energy Theorem.

Impact of Sign

Although Work-Energy and Impulse-Momentum are closely related, there is an important difference in how signs appear in the two frameworks.

In one dimension, impulse is given by:

\[ J = \int F\,dt \]

Time always moves forward, so \(dt \ge 0\).

As a result, the sign of an infinitesimal impulse \(dJ = F\,dt\) is determined entirely by the sign of the force—it simply tells us the direction of the impulse.

Work is given by:

\[ W = \int F\,dx \]

Unlike time, displacement can take place in either direction, so \(dx \le 0\) and \(dx \ge 0\) are both possible.

Therefore, the sign of an infinitesimal work \(dW = F\,dx\) depends on both the force and the displacement.

If \(u = x\,y\), then the sign of \(u\) depends on the signs of both \(x\) and \(y\).
Similarly, since \(dW = F\,dx\), the sign of work depends on the relative directions of force and displacement—not on how you choose your coordinate axes.

When the net force and displacement are in the same direction, net work is positive and kinetic energy increases.

When the net force and displacement are in opposite directions, net work is negative and kinetic energy decreases.

This dependence on the direction of displacement—and the resulting shift from tracking vector direction to tracking energy gain or loss—is one of the key differences between Impulse-Momentum and Work-Energy.
