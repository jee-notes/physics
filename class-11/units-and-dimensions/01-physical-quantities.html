Physical Quantity

The measurable properties of the physical world.

Concept

Concepts like "physical quantity" lie at the intersection of the philosophy behind physics as a subject and what we actually do in physics.

In physics, our aim is to describe natural phenomena quantitatively, that is, using numbers. For example, instead of saying "it is cold outside", we say that the temperature is \(12^\circ \text{C}\).

A natural phenomenon is any event that occurs in the physical world (within shared space and time). To describe a phenomenon quantitatively, we need to identify its properties that can be measured and assigned numerical values.

For example, saying that "Virat Kohli is good because he makes me happy" might be true, but it is scientifically irrelevant. However, saying that "Virat Kohli is good because he has scored 80+ centuries" refers to an objectively verifiable and countable property, making it scientifically relevant.

"Virat Kohli makes you happy" is also a property of your personal experience of the event. However, it is not measurable in the same sense as the number of centuries he has scored, and therefore cannot be described quantitatively in the standardized sense required in physics.

The main doubt students often have is: "But I can experience when I am more or less happy. I can even compare my happiness today with my happiness yesterday. So why do you say that happiness cannot be measured?"

This is a reasonable doubt. We can certainly distinguish between different levels of happiness within our own experiences, and we can even assign numerical scores to them. However, the numerical value assigned by one individual does not necessarily represent the same magnitude of happiness for another individual.

For a measurement to be meaningful across different observers, we need a common reference (unit or scale) that has a uniform meaning for everyone. We do not have such a universally established unit for happiness.

Therefore, the issue is not whether we can experience or compare different levels of happiness. The issue is understanding what we actually mean when we say that a physical quantity is measurable. We will explore this distinction in the following discussion.

Physical Quantity

A Physical Quantity is a property of a physical system or a physical process that can be measured and represented by a numerical value together with a unit.

  • Examples: Length, Mass, Time, Temperature, Force, Electric Current.
  • Counter-examples: Love, Hate, Beauty, Happiness.

This is the main issue. Measurability does not simply mean that we can assign a number to something. It means that the property can be compared with a defined reference unit, allowing its magnitude to be expressed quantitatively.

For example, let's say your mom tells you to bring \(2.5 \text{ kg}\) of apples from the market. This mass is the same for everyone. If you take those apples home and measure their mass using a properly calibrated weighing scale, it should show approximately the same value, irrespective of who performs the measurement.

But this is not the case with emotions. We can certainly assess our emotions subjectively. You can say that, "I feel less happy when Virat Kohli scores 50 compared to when he scores 100". Even your friend, who is also a Virat Kohli fan, might say the same thing. However, the magnitude of happiness experienced by you and your friend need not be the same.

You can even assign numerical scores to your happiness based on your individual experience. But the same numerical score assigned by two different individuals does not necessarily represent the same magnitude of happiness.

This is why we say that emotions are not measurable uniformly across every individual. We do not have a universally established reference unit (scale) for emotions that guarantees the same numerical value represents the same magnitude of experience across individuals.

It does not mean that we cannot assess or compare our own happiness in the reference of our individual life experience. It means that such an assessment is different from measurement using a common, standardized reference.

Measurement

Measurement is the process through which we obtain quantitative information about physical quantities. In fact, an observer is the person or a physical arrangement that performs the measurement and obtains information about the physical world.

Measurement is the process of comparing a physical quantity with a chosen reference called a unit.

The comparison is mathematical in nature. We ask: How many times does the reference fit into the quantity being measured?

For example, suppose a student measures the length of a table using handspans. If the table is found to be 10 handspans long, then its length has been compared against the reference quantity "one handspan".

Measurement is the process that converts a physical observation into a quantitative statement.
It is the point where mathematics meets the physical world.
One may think of it as the smallest unit of the process of physics in which mathematics marries physics and gives birth to a number.
Mathematics deals with abstract numbers and relations, but measurement is the process that gives those numbers a physical meaning.
\(2.5\) is a number, but \(2.5 \text{ kg}\) of apples is a quantitative description of something in the physical world.
The measurement process was performed by the shopkeeper who weighed and sold those apples to you.

The Need for a Common Scale

Imagine two students measuring the length of the same table using their handspans. The first student obtains a reading of 10 handspans, while the second student obtains a reading of 9 handspans.

Both students have performed a measurement. However, their numerical readings cannot be directly compared because their handspans are different. The reference used by one student does not have the same magnitude as the reference used by the other.

Can we solve this problem? Yes. We can define a handspan as a fixed reference length and ask everyone to use that same reference. Now, irrespective of who performs the measurement, the numerical readings will be based on a common scale.

This brings us to an important requirement of scientific measurement. We need a common scale that allows measurements made by different observers to be meaningfully compared. The reference must have a fixed and commonly defined magnitude so that the numerical values obtained by different observers have the same meaning.

Now let us revisit our earlier example of happiness. You can certainly experience more or less happiness, compare your present happiness with your past experiences, and even assign numerical scores to those experiences. But can we establish a common scale of happiness that allows us to compare the actual magnitude of happiness experienced by two different individuals?

For example, if you assign a score of 8 to your happiness and your friend also assigns a score of 8, does it mean that both of you experienced the same magnitude of happiness? Not necessarily. The numerical values are based on your individual experiences, and we do not have a universally established reference scale that guarantees the same magnitude of happiness for the same numerical value across individuals.

This is the fundamental distinction. Being able to experience, compare or assign numbers to something does not automatically mean that we can measure it on a common scale.

In physics, we require such a common scale so that measurements made by different observers can be quantitatively compared, communicated and reproduced.

A reference allows us to measure a physical quantity.
A commonly defined reference allows different observers to use the same scale.
A common scale makes measurements quantitatively comparable.
Scientific measurement requires this common basis so that numerical results have a consistent meaning across observers.

Mathematical Representation

Every measurement produces two pieces of information:

\[ Q = n \times u \]

where:

  • \(Q\) : Physical Quantity
  • \(n\) : Numerical Value
  • \(u\) : Unit (Reference Quantity)

For example,

\[ \text{Length of Table} = 10 \times \text{handspan} \]

The measurement tells us that the table length contains 10 copies of the chosen reference unit.

A measurement never produces a pure number.
It always produces a number together with a unit.
Together, the numerical value and unit specify the magnitude of the physical quantity.

Unit and Numerical Value

The magnitude of the physical quantity itself remains unchanged even if we choose a different unit.

Only the numerical value changes.

\[ Q = n_1u_1 = n_2u_2 \]

Therefore,

\[ n \propto \frac{1}{u} \]

A larger unit produces a smaller numerical value, while a smaller unit produces a larger numerical value.

Consider a length of one metre.

\[ 1\,m=100\,cm \]

When the unit becomes 100 times smaller, the numerical value becomes 100 times larger.

The numerical value is not an intrinsic property of a quantity.
It depends on the choice of unit.
The magnitude of the physical quantity remains unchanged and is an intrinsic property of the quantity.

Why Physical Quantities, Measurement and Units Belong Together

Physics is often described as the study of the physical world using the language of mathematics.

Physics = Physical World + Maths

The physical world and mathematics are fundamentally different things. The physical world contains objects, events and phenomena. Mathematics contains abstract entities such as numbers, equations and relations.

The process that connects these two worlds is called measurement. Measurement is the act through which a physical observation is converted into a mathematical statement.

The result of every measurement is a quantitative description of a physical quantity expressed as:

\[ Q = n \times u \]

This equation contains the two ingredients required to describe reality quantitatively.

  • \(n\) : The numerical value, which comes from mathematics.
  • \(u\) : The unit, which provides the connection to the physical world.

A physical quantity is therefore the smallest building block in physics that contains both a mathematical component and a component of the physical world from unit.

Physical Quantities, Measurement and Units are not independent topics.
They are three aspects of a single idea.
Measurement is the process that joins mathematics and the physical world.
A Physical Quantity is the result of that process.
A Unit provides the physical meaning that allows a number to describe reality.
Together, these concepts form the foundation of quantitative physics.
