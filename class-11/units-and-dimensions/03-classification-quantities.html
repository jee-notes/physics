Classification of Physical Quantities

Organising the multitude of Physical Quantities.

Why Do We Need Classification?

The physical world contains an enormous number of measurable properties.

Length, Area, Volume, Velocity, Acceleration, Force, Pressure, Energy, Power, Electric Charge, Electric Field, Temperature and countless others are all physical quantities.

As physics develops, new physical quantities continue to be introduced to describe newly discovered phenomena and relationships in nature.

Consequently, the number of physical quantities grows continuously. Studying every quantity independently would make physics increasingly difficult to organise and understand.

A large collection of objects is difficult to understand without organisation.
Classification is the process of identifying patterns and grouping similar objects together.
Biology classifies living organisms so that newly discovered species can be placed within an existing framework.
Similarly, physics classifies physical quantities so that both existing and newly introduced quantities can be understood systematically.

To manage the multitude of physical quantities, physicists developed systems of classification.

Basis of Classification

Physical quantities can be classified in many ways.

Two of the most important classifications are:

  • Fundamental and Derived Quantities
  • Scalar and Vector Quantities

The first classification is based on how a quantity is defined.

The second classification is based on how a quantity behaves mathematically.

Fundamental and Derived Quantities

In the previous chapter, we saw that the SI system uses seven base quantities.

The reason for introducing base quantities is simple.

There are far too many physical quantities to define independent units for each one.

Nature presents us with a vast number of physical quantities.
Instead of treating every quantity as independent, physics chooses a small set of foundational quantities and expresses all others in terms of them.
This dramatically simplifies measurement and scientific communication.

Fundamental Quantities

Fundamental quantities are quantities that are treated as basic and independent within a chosen system of units.

They are not expressed in terms of other physical quantities within that system.

Fundamental quantities are not necessarily the most important quantities in nature.
They are quantities chosen as the starting point for measurement.
Their role is similar to the role of axioms in mathematics.

In the SI system, the seven fundamental quantities are:

  • Length
  • Mass
  • Time
  • Electric Current
  • Thermodynamic Temperature
  • Amount of Substance
  • Luminous Intensity

Derived Quantities

Once a small set of fundamental quantities has been chosen, many other quantities can be constructed from them.

Such quantities are called Derived Quantities.

A derived quantity is a quantity that can be expressed mathematically in terms of fundamental quantities.

Derived quantities are not assigned completely independent units.
Instead, their units are built from the units of fundamental quantities.
Thus derived quantities "borrow" their meaning from fundamental quantities.

Examples include:

  • Area = Length × Length
  • Volume = Length × Length × Length
  • Velocity = Length / Time
  • Acceleration = Velocity / Time
  • Force = Mass × Acceleration
  • Work = Force × Displacement

The Process of Derivation

When a quantity is derived from fundamental quantities, its unit can also be derived from the units of those quantities.

The derivation follows directly from the mathematical definition of the quantity.

Pressure is defined as:

\[ P=\frac{F}{A} \]

Substituting the SI units:

\[ \text{Unit of Pressure} = \frac{\text{kg m s}^{-2}} {\text{m}^{2}} = \text{kg m}^{-1}\text{s}^{-2} \]

This derived unit is called the Pascal (Pa).

Derivation is not merely a mathematical trick.
It reflects a deeper idea:
A derived quantity obtains its physical meaning from the fundamental quantities used to define it.

Why This Classification Matters

The distinction between fundamental and derived quantities allows physics to describe a vast number of measurable properties using only a small set of foundational standards.

Without this classification, every physical quantity would require its own independent unit and standard of measurement.

Fundamental quantities provide the building blocks.
Derived quantities provide the structure built from those blocks.
This is how physics manages an enormous number of physical quantities using only a small number of basic standards.
