Work, Heat, and Internal Energy

The triad of thermodynamic energy transfer and the state function nature of internal energy.

Concept of Work Done in Thermodynamics

In classical thermodynamics, work represents an organized transfer of energy across a system's boundary mediated by a macroscopic mechanical force displacing that boundary. Unlike thermal energy transfer (heat), which is random and molecular, mechanical work is coherent and directional.

Consider a gas confined in a cylinder of cross-sectional area \( A \) sealed by a frictionless, movable piston. The gas exerts an outward pressure \( P \) on the piston face. The normal force exerted by the gas is:

\[ F = P \cdot A \]

If the piston undergoes an infinitesimal quasi-static displacement \( dx \) outwards, the infinitesimal mechanical work done by the gas on the piston is:

\[ dW = F \, dx = (P \cdot A) \, dx = P \, (A \, dx) = P \, dV \] where \( dV = A \, dx \) is the change in the volume of the gas.

Expansion and Compression Work Formula

For a finite quasi-static process where the volume changes from an initial volume \( V_i \) to a final volume \( V_f \), the total work done by the system on its surroundings is obtained by integrating the differential work along the process path:

\[ W = \int_{V_i}^{V_f} P_{\text{ext}} \, dV \quad \xrightarrow{\text{quasi-static}} \quad W = \int_{V_i}^{V_f} P \, dV \] where:
  • \( W \): Mechanical work done (SI Unit: Joule (\( \text{J} \)) or \( \text{N}\cdot\text{m} \), Dimension: \( [M L^2 T^{-2}] \))
  • \( P \): Absolute pressure of the gas at that volume (SI Unit: \( \text{Pa} = \text{N/m}^2 \))
  • \( V \): Volume occupied by the gas (SI Unit: \( \text{m}^3 \))

Key Physical Consequences:

  • Expansion (\( V_f > V_i \implies dV > 0 \)): The gas pushes the boundary outward; \( W > 0 \) (work is done by the gas on the surroundings).
  • Compression (\( V_f < V_i \implies dV < 0 \)): External forces compress the gas; \( W < 0 \) (work is done on the gas by the surroundings).
  • Constant Volume (\( V_f = V_i \implies dV = 0 \)): Rigid container; \( W = 0 \) (zero boundary work).

Sign Conventions in Physics vs Chemistry

A frequent source of confusion in competitive examinations arises from differing conventions between Physics (IUPAP) and Chemistry (IUPAC). Both formulations represent the exact same physical reality.

Quantity Physics Convention (Standard in JEE Physics) Chemistry Convention (IUPAC)
Work Done by System (Expansion) \( W > 0 \) (Positive) \( W < 0 \) (Negative)
Work Done on System (Compression) \( W < 0 \) (Negative) \( W > 0 \) (Positive)
Heat Added to System (\( Q_{\text{in}} \)) \( Q > 0 \) (Positive) \( Q > 0 \) (Positive)
Heat Released by System (\( Q_{\text{out}} \)) \( Q < 0 \) (Negative) \( Q < 0 \) (Negative)
First Law Equation \[ \Delta U = Q - W \implies dQ = dU + dW \] \[ \Delta U = Q + W \implies dU = dQ + dW \]
In Physics, we view the system as an engine built to perform useful work for us, so work done by the system is counted as positive (\( +W \)). In Chemistry, the focus is on the internal energy ledger of the system, so work done on the system increases its energy (\( +W \)).

Heat as Energy in Transit and Path Dependency

Heat (\( Q \)) is defined strictly as energy in transit across a boundary as a direct consequence of a temperature difference between the system and its surroundings.

  • Heat is not a substance ("caloric") stored inside matter. Matter contains internal energy, not heat.
  • Heat appears only at the boundary while a process is taking place and ceases to exist once thermal equilibrium is reached.
  • Heat is a path function: the total heat transferred between two equilibrium states depends entirely on the path followed.
\[ dQ = n \, C \, dT \] where \( n \) is the number of moles, \( dT \) is the temperature increment, and \( C \) is the molar heat capacity for that particular process.

Internal Energy of an Ideal Gas

The Internal Energy (\( U \)) of a thermodynamic system is the total macroscopic kinetic and potential energy of all its constituent atoms, molecules, and subatomic particles relative to a reference frame attached to the center of mass of the system.

\[ U = E_{\text{kinetic, microscopic}} + E_{\text{potential, intermolecular}} \]

For an ideal gas, intermolecular forces are identically zero by definition. Therefore, the intermolecular potential energy vanishes (\( E_{\text{potential}} = 0 \)), and the internal energy consists entirely of the kinetic energy of molecular translation, rotation, and vibration.

For \( n \) moles of an ideal gas with \( f \) degrees of freedom: \[ U = n \left( \frac{f}{2} R T \right) = n \, C_v \, T \] where:
  • \( U \): Internal Energy (SI Unit: Joule (\( \text{J} \)), Dimension: \( [M L^2 T^{-2}] \))
  • \( f \): Degrees of freedom of the gas molecules (\( f=3 \) for monoatomic, \( f=5 \) for diatomic at room temperature)
  • \( C_v = \frac{f}{2}R \): Molar heat capacity at constant volume
  • \( T \): Absolute temperature (in Kelvin)

Microscopic Interpretation and Joule's Law of Internal Energy

The dependency of the internal energy of an ideal gas exclusively on temperature is formalized by Joule's Law of Internal Energy.

Joule's Law: The internal energy of a given mass of an ideal gas depends solely on its absolute temperature \( T \), and is completely independent of its pressure \( P \) and volume \( V \): \[ \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T = 0, \quad \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial P}\right)_T = 0 \]

Consequently, for any process whatsoever (isochoric, isobaric, isothermal, adiabatic, or arbitrary polytropic) undergone by an ideal gas:

\[ \Delta U = n \, C_v \, \Delta T = n \, \left(\frac{f}{2} R\right) (T_f - T_i) = \frac{P_f V_f - P_i V_i}{\gamma - 1} \]

Solved Example: Internal Energy Change Across Different Paths

An ideal diatomic gas (\( \gamma = 7/5 \)) expands from state \( A \, (P_0, V_0) \) to state \( B \, (2P_0, 2V_0) \) via two different paths:
Path 1: A straight line on the \( P \)-\( V \) diagram.
Path 2: Isochoric heating to \( (2P_0, V_0) \) followed by isobaric expansion to \( (2P_0, 2V_0) \).
Find \( \Delta U \) for both paths.

Solution:

Since \( U \) is a state function, \( \Delta U \) depends only on the initial and final states, not the path:

\[ T_A = \frac{P_0 V_0}{nR}, \quad T_B = \frac{(2P_0)(2V_0)}{nR} = \frac{4 P_0 V_0}{nR} \] \[ \Delta U_1 = \Delta U_2 = n C_v (T_B - T_A) = n \left(\frac{5}{2} R\right) \left(\frac{4 P_0 V_0 - P_0 V_0}{nR}\right) = \frac{15}{2} P_0 V_0 \]

The internal energy change is identical for both paths (\( 7.5 P_0 V_0 \)), while the work done and heat exchanged differ significantly.
