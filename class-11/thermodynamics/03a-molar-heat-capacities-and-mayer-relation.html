Molar Heat Capacities & Mayer's Relation

Kinetic origins of specific heats, the isobaric-isochoric disparity, and gas mixture thermodynamics.

Molar Heat Capacity at Constant Volume (Cv)

The Molar Heat Capacity at Constant Volume (\( C_v \)) is the quantity of heat required to raise the temperature of one mole of a gas by one Kelvin (or \( 1^\circ\text{C} \)) while maintaining its volume strictly constant.

\[ C_v = \left( \frac{dQ}{n \, dT} \right)_V = \frac{1}{n} \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial T} \right)_V \] where:
  • \( C_v \): Molar heat capacity at constant volume (SI Unit: \( \text{J}/(\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}) \), Dimension: \( [M L^2 T^{-2} K^{-1} N^{-1}] \))
  • \( dQ \): Heat supplied under isochoric conditions (\( dV = 0 \))
  • \( n \): Number of moles of gas
  • \( dT \): Temperature increment

Because no external work is performed in an isochoric process (\( dW = P\,dV = 0 \)), all thermal energy supplied goes entirely into increasing the microscopic internal kinetic energy of the gas:

\[ dQ = dU \implies dU = n \, C_v \, dT \]

Molar Heat Capacity at Constant Pressure (Cp)

The Molar Heat Capacity at Constant Pressure (\( C_p \)) is the quantity of heat required to raise the temperature of one mole of a gas by one Kelvin while the pressure is maintained strictly constant.

\[ C_p = \left( \frac{dQ}{n \, dT} \right)_P \] where:
  • \( C_p \): Molar heat capacity at constant pressure (SI Unit: \( \text{J}/(\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}) \))

In an isobaric process, heating causes the gas to expand. Consequently, the heat supplied must accomplish two tasks simultaneously:

  1. Increase the internal energy (\( dU \)) to raise the temperature by \( dT \).
  2. Perform external mechanical work (\( dW = P\,dV \)) against the constant external pressure as the volume expands.

Therefore, more heat is required to achieve the same temperature rise at constant pressure than at constant volume:

\[ C_p > C_v \quad \text{always for any gas} \]

Mayer's Relation: Cp - Cv = R

The quantitative relationship between \( C_p \) and \( C_v \) for an ideal gas is known as Mayer's Relation.

Rigorous Derivation:

Consider 1 mole of an ideal gas (\( n = 1 \)).

1. Under constant volume, supplying heat \( dQ_V \) raises temperature by \( dT \): \[ dQ_V = dU = C_v \, dT \] 2. Under constant pressure, supplying heat \( dQ_P \) raises temperature by the same \( dT \): \[ dQ_P = C_p \, dT \] 3. From the First Law of Thermodynamics for the isobaric process: \[ dQ_P = dU + dW \] \[ C_p \, dT = dU + P \, dV \] 4. Since \( U \) depends only on \( T \) for an ideal gas, \( dU = C_v \, dT \) in both processes: \[ C_p \, dT = C_v \, dT + P \, dV \] 5. From the ideal gas equation for 1 mole (\( PV = RT \)), differentiating at constant pressure yields: \[ P \, dV = R \, dT \] 6. Substituting \( P\,dV = R\,dT \) into the energy balance: \[ C_p \, dT = C_v \, dT + R \, dT \] Dividing throughout by \( dT \):

\[ C_p - C_v = R \] where \( R \approx 8.314 \, \text{J}/(\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}) \) is the Universal Gas Constant.
For specific heat capacities per unit mass (\( c_p \) and \( c_v \) in \( \text{J}/(\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}) \)): \[ c_p - c_v = \frac{R}{M} = r \] where \( M \) is the molar mass of the gas (in \( \text{kg/mol} \)) and \( r \) is the characteristic gas constant.

Ratio of Specific Heats (Gamma)

The ratio of molar heat capacities is denoted by the dimensionless parameter \( \gamma \) (Adiabatic Index or Poisson's Ratio):

\[ \gamma = \frac{C_p}{C_v} = 1 + \frac{R}{C_v} = 1 + \frac{2}{f} \] where \( f \) is the active degrees of freedom of the gas molecules.

From \( C_p - C_v = R \) and \( \gamma = C_p / C_v \), we can express \( C_v \) and \( C_p \) directly in terms of \( \gamma \):

\[ C_v = \frac{R}{\gamma - 1}, \qquad C_p = \frac{\gamma R}{\gamma - 1} \]

Degrees of Freedom and Heat Capacities for Different Gas Types

The Equipartition Theorem of Energy states that each active quadratic degree of freedom of a gas molecule in thermal equilibrium contributes an average thermal energy of \( \frac{1}{2} k_B T \) per molecule, or \( \frac{1}{2} R T \) per mole.

Gas Atomicity / Structure Degrees of Freedom (\( f \)) \( C_v = \frac{f}{2}R \) \( C_p = \left(\frac{f}{2}+1\right)R \) \( \gamma = \frac{C_p}{C_v} \) Typical Physical Examples
Monoatomic Gas 3 Translational (\( f = 3 \)) \( \frac{3}{2}R \) \( \frac{5}{2}R \) \( \frac{5}{3} \approx 1.67 \) \( \text{He}, \text{Ne}, \text{Ar}, \text{Kr} \)
Diatomic Gas (Rigid, Room Temp) 3 Trans + 2 Rotational (\( f = 5 \)) \( \frac{5}{2}R \) \( \frac{7}{2}R \) \( \frac{7}{5} = 1.40 \) \( \text{H}_2, \text{N}_2, \text{O}_2, \text{CO} \)
Diatomic Gas (High Temp, Vibrating) 3 Trans + 2 Rot + 2 Vib (\( f = 7 \)) \( \frac{7}{2}R \) \( \frac{9}{2}R \) \( \frac{9}{7} \approx 1.29 \) \( \text{Cl}_2, \text{Br}_2 \) (at elevated \( T \))
Non-linear Triatomic / Polyatomic 3 Trans + 3 Rotational (\( f = 6 \)) \( 3R \) \( 4R \) \( \frac{4}{3} \approx 1.33 \) \( \text{H}_2\text{O}, \text{NH}_3, \text{CH}_4, \text{SO}_2 \)
Linear Polyatomic Gas 3 Trans + 2 Rotational (\( f = 5 \)) \( \frac{5}{2}R \) \( \frac{7}{2}R \) \( \frac{7}{5} = 1.40 \) \( \text{CO}_2, \text{C}_2\text{H}_2 \)

Specific Heat Capacity of Gas Mixtures

Consider a non-reacting mixture of \( k \) ideal gases containing \( n_1, n_2, \dots, n_k \) moles with molar heat capacities \( C_{v1}, C_{v2}, \dots, C_{vk} \).

1. Equivalent \( C_{v,\text{mix}} \):

Since internal energy is extensive (\( U_{\text{mix}} = U_1 + U_2 + \dots \)):

\[ C_{v,\text{mix}} = \frac{n_1 C_{v1} + n_2 C_{v2} + \dots + n_k C_{vk}}{n_1 + n_2 + \dots + n_k} = \frac{\sum n_i C_{vi}}{\sum n_i} \]

2. Equivalent \( C_{p,\text{mix}} \):

\[ C_{p,\text{mix}} = \frac{n_1 C_{p1} + n_2 C_{p2} + \dots + n_k C_{pk}}{n_1 + n_2 + \dots + n_k} = C_{v,\text{mix}} + R \]

3. Equivalent \( \gamma_{\text{mix}} \):

\[ \gamma_{\text{mix}} = \frac{C_{p,\text{mix}}}{C_{v,\text{mix}}} = \frac{n_1 C_{p1} + n_2 C_{p2} + \dots}{n_1 C_{v1} + n_2 C_{v2} + \dots} = \frac{\sum n_i \left(\frac{\gamma_i}{\gamma_i - 1}\right)}{\sum n_i \left(\frac{1}{\gamma_i - 1}\right)} \]

Solved Example: Mixture of Helium and Oxygen

A gas mixture consists of \( 2 \, \text{moles} \) of Helium (monoatomic, \( C_{v1} = 1.5R \)) and \( 3 \, \text{moles} \) of Oxygen (diatomic, \( C_{v2} = 2.5R \)). Calculate \( C_{v,\text{mix}} \), \( C_{p,\text{mix}} \), and \( \gamma_{\text{mix}} \).

Solution:

\[ C_{v,\text{mix}} = \frac{2(1.5R) + 3(2.5R)}{2 + 3} = \frac{3R + 7.5R}{5} = \frac{10.5R}{5} = 2.1 \, R \] \[ C_{p,\text{mix}} = C_{v,\text{mix}} + R = 2.1R + R = 3.1 \, R \] \[ \gamma_{\text{mix}} = \frac{C_{p,\text{mix}}}{C_{v,\text{mix}}} = \frac{3.1R}{2.1R} = \frac{31}{21} \approx 1.476 \]
