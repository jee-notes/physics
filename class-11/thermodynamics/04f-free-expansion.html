Free Expansion & Joule Expansion

Thermodynamics of unrestrained vacuum expansion: zero work, zero heat, and absolute irreversibility.

Concept of Free Expansion (Joule Expansion into Vacuum)

Free Expansion (historically investigated in Joule's famous porous-plug and double-chamber experiments) occurs when a gas initially confined in an insulated compartment is allowed to suddenly rush into an adjacent evacuated chamber through a valve or ruptured membrane.

Conditions: W = 0, Q = 0 implies Delta U = 0

Analyzing the energy ledger of free expansion:

  1. Work Done (\( W = 0 \)): Because the gas expands into an absolute vacuum, there is no opposing external pressure (\( P_{\text{ext}} = 0 \)). The gas pushes against nothing: \[ W = \int P_{\text{ext}} \, dV = \int (0) \, dV = 0 \]
  2. Heat Exchanged (\( Q = 0 \)): The outer chamber walls are thermally insulated: \[ Q = 0 \]
  3. Internal Energy Change (\( \Delta U = 0 \)): Applying the First Law of Thermodynamics:
    \[ \Delta U = Q - W = 0 - 0 = 0 \implies U_{\text{final}} = U_{\text{initial}} \]

Free expansion is an isoenergetic process (\( U = \text{constant} \)).

Irreversibility and Non-Quasistatic Nature

Free expansion is the archetypal example of an intrinsically irreversible, non-quasistatic process:

  • During the violent expansion, pressure, density, and temperature are highly non-uniform throughout the chamber; macroscopic state variables are undefined while the gas is rushing.
  • The process cannot be represented as a continuous line on a \( P \)-\( V \) diagram. Only the initial equilibrium state \( (P_1, V_1) \) and final equilibrium state \( (P_2, V_2) \) can be plotted as isolated points.
  • The gas will never spontaneously compress itself back into the left chamber without external mechanical intervention, proving it generates entropy in the universe (\( \Delta S > 0 \)).

Temperature Behavior of Ideal Gas vs Real Gas in Free Expansion

The effect of free expansion on temperature reveals the presence or absence of intermolecular forces:

1. Ideal Gas:

For an ideal gas, internal energy depends solely on temperature (\( U = n C_v T \)). Since \( \Delta U = 0 \):

\[ \Delta U = 0 \implies \Delta T = 0 \implies T_{\text{final}} = T_{\text{initial}} \] An ideal gas undergoes zero temperature change during free expansion.

Final pressure for an ideal gas expanding from \( V_1 \) to \( V_2 = V_1 + V_{\text{vacuum}} \): \[ P_2 = \frac{n R T}{V_2} = P_1 \left(\frac{V_1}{V_2}\right) \]

2. Real Gas (van der Waals Gas):

For a real gas, attractive intermolecular forces exist (described by the van der Waals parameter \( a \)). The internal energy includes intermolecular potential energy: \[ U(T, V) = n C_v T - \frac{a n^2}{V} \] As volume increases from \( V_1 \) to \( V_2 \), molecules are pulled apart against mutual attractive forces, increasing intermolecular potential energy. Since total \( U \) is conserved (\( \Delta U = 0 \)): \[ n C_v \Delta T = \Delta U_{\text{pot}} = -a n^2 \left( \frac{1}{V_1} - \frac{1}{V_2} \right) < 0 \implies \Delta T < 0 \]

A real gas cools down (\( T_f < T_i \)) during free expansion into a vacuum because it must expend kinetic energy to overcome internal molecular attractive forces.
