Thermodynamic Processes on Ideal Gases

Standard Thermo-Mechanical thermodynamic processes on gaseous bulk matter.

Concept

When we study any process in physics, the process that we study mathematically is not an exact description of the process that happens in real life.

A real physical process involves many causes and effects and including everything would make the model chaotic and extremely complicated to analyze. Therefore, we deliberately make certain idealizing assumptions and construct a simpler model that captures the essential behaviour.

For example, in mechanics, when we study projectile motion, we neglect air resistance. We model a sufficiently small object as a point mass. The actual projectile is neither moving through a vacuum nor a point, but these assumptions allow us to isolate and understand the essential mechanics of the motion.

The same idea is followed in Thermodynamics. When we talk about a thermodynamic process, we are not claiming that the real physical process occurs exactly according to the simplified equations that we write. Rather, we construct an idealized model of the process by imposing suitable assumptions and constraints.

For example, many standard thermodynamic processes are studied in the quasi-static limit, in which the system passes through a sequence of states arbitrarily close to thermodynamic equilibrium. This allows quantities such as \(P\), \(V\) and \(T\) to remain well-defined throughout the process and allows us to represent the process as a path through thermodynamic state space.

We also introduce the idealization of reversibility. A reversible process is an ideal limiting process in which the system can be brought back through the same sequence of equilibrium states without leaving a finite net change in the system and surroundings.

Real processes always involve effects such as finite temperature or pressure differences, friction, turbulence and other dissipative effects, so a perfectly reversible process is an idealization.

Thus, the standard thermodynamic processes that we study — such as isochoric, isobaric, isothermal, adiabatic and polytropic processes — are to be understood as ideal models of thermodynamic processes.

Physics does not always study a real process in all of its complexity.
We construct an idealized model by deliberately neglecting effects that are not essential to the phenomenon under consideration and imposing suitable assumptions.
The purpose of these idealizations is not to claim that the real world is exactly ideal, but to isolate the essential physics and make the dynamics mathematically calculable.

Classification of Thermodynamic Processes

A thermodynamic process describes the evolution of a system from an initial thermodynamic state \( (P_i, V_i, T_i) \) to a final thermodynamic state \( (P_f, V_f, T_f) \) along a particular path. The path specifies how the system evolves between the two states.

In syllabus (Physics), we only study ideal thermodynamic processes of ideal gases. These processes are classified according to the state variable that is constrained to remain constant, the nature of the energy exchange, or some other physical constraint imposed during the evolution.

The following processes are covered here only as a comparative summary. Each process is studied in greater detail in its corresponding sub-topic.

  • Isochoric Process: Volume remains constant (\( V = \text{const}\), \(dV = 0\)). No work done by gas (or on the gas). (\( W = 0 \)).
  • Isobaric Process: Pressure remains constant (\( P = \text{const}\), \(dP = 0\)). The gas does mechanical work (positive or negative but non-zero) against constant external load.
  • Isothermal Process: Absolute temperature remains constant (\( T = \text{const}\), \(dT = 0\)). Internal energy remains invariant for ideal gases (\( \Delta U = 0 \)).
  • Adiabatic Process: System is thermally isolated from surroundings (\( Q = 0 \)). Work is done entirely at the expense of internal energy.
  • Polytropic Process: General process following the path equation \( P V^m = \text{constant} \), unifying all classical processes.
  • Free Expansion: Sudden, non-quasistatic expansion of gas into a vacuum without external work or heat transfer.

The standard thermodynamic processes can also be grouped according to which energy interaction is externally controlled and which interaction is determined as the response of the system.

In isochoric ,isobaric processes, we externally supply or remove heat from the gas. Thus, the thermal energy interaction \(Q\) is the one we control externally. The mechanical condition is imposed through the external setup. The resulting mechanical work \(W\) is determined by how the thermodynamic state changes.

In isothermal ,adiabatic processes, we instead externally drive the gas mechanically through expansion or compression. Thus, the work \(W\) is the externally imposed energy interaction. The heat transfer \(Q\) is then determined by how the internal energy must change under the thermal condition of the process.

We use the First Law of Thermodynamics equation \(\Delta U = Q - W\) . In case of isochoric and isobaric, we externally control \(Q\) and let \(W,\Delta U\) be determined by constraints and state.
In case of isothermal and adiabatic, we externally control \(W\) and let \(Q,\Delta U\) be determined by constraints and state.

These classifications are majorly about first law usage: \(\Delta U = Q - W\)

  • Isochoric Process: \( dV = 0\) , \( W = 0\) , \(\Delta U = Q\)
  • Isothermal Process: \( dT = 0\) , \( \Delta U = 0 \) , \( Q = W\)
  • Adiabatic Process: \( Q = 0 \) , \(\Delta U = - W\)
  • Isobaric Process: \( dP = 0\) , \(\Delta U = Q - W\) - All 3 are non-zero.

Governing Equations and Constraints

Each fundamental process satisfies a characteristic constraint equation linking the macroscopic state variables \( P, V, T \):

Process Constraint Condition P-V Relation T-V Relation P-T Relation
Isochoric \( V = \text{const} \) or \( \rho = \text{const} \) \( V = C \) \( T \propto P \) \( \frac{P}{T} = \text{const} \)
Isobaric \( P = \text{const} \) \( P = C \) \( \frac{V}{T} = \text{const} \) or \( \rho T = \text{const} \) \( T \propto V \)
Isothermal \( T = \text{const} \) \( PV = \text{const} \) or \( \frac{P}{\rho} = \text{const} \) \( T = C \) \( T = C \)
Adiabatic \( dQ = 0 \) \( P V^\gamma = \text{const} \) \( T V^{\gamma-1} = \text{const} \) \( T^\gamma P^{1-\gamma} = \text{const} \)
Polytropic \( PV^m = \text{const} \) \( P V^m = \text{const} \) \( T V^{m-1} = \text{const} \) \( T^x P^{1-m} = \text{const} \)

Comparative Slopes on P-V Diagrams

The slope of a process curve at any point on the \( P \)-\( V \) diagram represents the rate of change of pressure with respect to volume, \( \left( \frac{dP}{dV} \right) \).

1. Isothermal Slope:

Differentiating \( PV = \text{constant} \) with respect to \( V \): \[ P + V \frac{dP}{dV} = 0 \implies \left( \frac{dP}{dV} \right)_{\text{iso}} = -\frac{P}{V} \]

2. Adiabatic Slope:

Differentiating \( PV^\gamma = \text{constant} \) with respect to \( V \): \[ \gamma P V^{\gamma-1} + V^\gamma \frac{dP}{dV} = 0 \implies \left( \frac{dP}{dV} \right)_{\text{adia}} = -\gamma \frac{P}{V} \]

\[ \left( \frac{dP}{dV} \right)_{\text{adiabatic}} = \gamma \times \left( \frac{dP}{dV} \right)_{\text{isothermal}} \]

Since \( \gamma > 1 \), the adiabatic curve on a \( P \)-\( V \) diagram is strictly steeper (has a greater negative slope) than the isothermal curve passing through the same point.

Expansion vs Compression Comparisons from \( (P_0, V_0) \):

  • During Expansion (\( V > V_0 \)): \[ P_{\text{isobaric}} > P_{\text{isothermal}} > P_{\text{adiabatic}} \] \[ W_{\text{isobaric}} > W_{\text{isothermal}} > W_{\text{adiabatic}} \]
  • During Compression (\( V \lt V_0 \)): \[ P_{\text{adiabatic}} > P_{\text{isothermal}} > P_{\text{isobaric}} \] \[ |W_{\text{adiabatic}}| > |W_{\text{isothermal}}| > |W_{\text{isobaric}}| \]
