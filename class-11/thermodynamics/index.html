Thermodynamics

The science of energy exchange and dynamic equilibrium and direction.

Concept

To understand Thermodynamics, first understand what is continuously happening in this universe. Matter does not exist in complete isolation with respect to interaction with its surrounding. Everything (bulk matter) is continuously involved in interactions and exchanges with its surroundings, whether we intentionally participate in them or not.

In heat transfer mechanism chapter we learned that - as long as a body is above absolute zero, it continuously exchanges energy with its surroundings through thermal radiation. Thus, its not like thermal exchange happens only when we deliberately heat or cool something. It is a continuously occurring phenomenon that occurs simply because bodies exist at a temperature above absolute zero.

Humans, for example, are warm-blooded creatures and need to burn calories to maintain their body temperature at around \(98.4^\circ F\). Its because, our bodies continuously exchange energy with the surroundings, through thermal radiation.

Absolute zero is an ideal limiting condition and has not been achieved as an actual temperature. Therefore, in the physical world, bulk matter is continuously involved in thermal interaction and hence in continuous energy exchange.

Also, thermal exchange is not the only kind of exchange! Bulk matter also participates in mechanical interaction with its surroundings. For example, everything that exists within Earth's atmosphere is continuously subjected to atmospheric pressure. And we know that bulk matter is elastic and not perfectly rigid. Thus, the surroundings interact with a system not only thermally, but mechanically as well and the shape and size of bulk matter also depends on surrounding!

Therefore, all bulk matter is involved in continuous exchange of mechanical and thermal energy with its surroundings. These exchanges are about transfer of thermal energy and perform mechanical work and change the condition of the matter.

This continuous exchange of energy through work and heat between bulk matter and its surroundings is what we study about in this chapter.

Note that here we talk about heat and work in general.
We are intentionally talking about only thermal and mechanical energy exchanges as of now due to syllabus.
But the bigger definition of thermodynamics involves heat and work and work includes mechanical, electromagnetic etc.

Why so? Because our intention is to make machines like engines and refrigerators. Refrigerator and steam engine - both are machines in which we use this study of relation between heat and mechanical work, and control it to our advantage.

However, to understand a system (bulk matter) involved in any dynamics, we first need a way to describe what the system is like at any particular instant. That description is what we call its state.

State is thus a description created for the purpose of understanding the dynamics. A physical system possesses an enormous number of properties (like smell etc.), but not every property is a thermodynamic state variable. We select those macroscopic, measurable properties (physical quantities) that are relevant to the thermodynamic behaviour we are trying to understand.

Thus, pressure \(P\), volume \(V\), temperature \(T\), amount of substance \(n\), internal energy \(U\), etc. become thermodynamic state variables. They provide us with a mathematical description for expressing the condition of matter as it undergoes thermodynamic processes.

Thus, Thermodynamics is the study of thermal and mechanical energy interactions of bulk matter with its surroundings. This study is through investigation of the relations between heat, mechanical work, temperature, internal energy and also the transformations of energy that occur when a system (bulk matter) interacts with its surroundings.

The foundations of Thermodynamics rest on empirical laws of nature that cannot be derived from purely mechanical principles alone. These laws tell us what happens during thermodynamic change.

The First Law establishes the conservation and accounting of energy, while the Second Law places fundamental limits on energy transformations and determines the preferred direction of spontaneous thermodynamic processes.

Thus, Thermodynamics gives us a framework to understand a universe in which matter is continuously interacting, exchanging energy and undergoing change. We define the state to understand the dynamics.

State Variables

The structure of our study of dynamics is based on how we define the system, state and process. The same idea was followed in mechanics.

We first define the system as the entity that we choose to study and which is involved in the dynamics. In mechanics, our systems were point masses, rigid bodies and systems of particles. In thermodynamics, we study bulk matter — matter for which a thermal state can be defined (temperature).

Once the system is defined, we need a way to describe its condition while it is involved in the dynamics. For this, we define state variables. In mechanics, quantities such as position and velocity, which we studied in kinematics, were used to describe the state of the system. In thermodynamics, we use macroscopic properties of bulk matter such as pressure \(P\), volume \(V\), temperature \(T\) and amount of substance \(n\).

A thermodynamic system is a clearly defined quantity of matter or a region in space enclosed by a boundary across which energy or matter may be exchanged with the surrounding environment. The boundary is part of how we define the system and its external conditions. For example, in a rigid container the boundary fixes the volume, whereas in a container with a movable piston the volume can change and an external mechanical condition such as \(P_{\rm ext}\) becomes relevant.

  • Open System: Can exchange both matter and energy with its surroundings (e.g., an open beaker of boiling water).
  • Closed System: Can exchange energy, through heat and work, but no matter across its boundary (e.g., gas sealed inside a cylinder with a movable piston).
  • Isolated System: Cannot exchange either matter or energy with its surroundings (e.g., gas inside a perfectly rigid, insulated container).

In any study of dynamics, equilibrium is a special condition that can be attained when relevant opposing interactions or rates of exchange balance each other. The exchanges themselves need not stop. For example, in a steady-state flow (dynamic equilibrium), matter or energy continues to flow through a system, while the relevant macroscopic state variables remain constant with time.

In thermodynamics, the equilibrium condition is especially important because the system is continuously involved in exchanges with its surroundings.
For example, a gas at temperature \(T\) continuously exchanges thermal radiation with its surroundings.
If the incoming and outgoing energy are not balanced, the internal energy and hence the temperature of the gas will change.
At thermal equilibrium, the gas and its surroundings have the same temperature: \[ T_{\rm gas}=T_{\rm surroundings} \] and there is no net heat transfer due to a temperature difference.
A sufficient equilibrium condition is therefore essential for macroscopic thermodynamic quantities such as temperature and pressure to be well-defined for the system.

The equilibrium condition of a thermodynamic system is described by its state variables, such as \(P\), \(V\), \(T\), \(n\) and \(U\). These quantities characterize the state and do not depend on the history by which that state was reached. They are therefore distinct from path variables, such as heat \(Q\) and work \(W\), whose values depend on the particular process followed between two states.

A thermodynamic process describes the change (evolution) of a system from one thermodynamic state to another. Therefore, in studying a process, we use both the state description of the system and the path-dependent quantities associated with the process. State variables describe the states involved in the dynamics, whereas path variables describe the exchanges that occur along the particular path of the process.

\[ \boxed{\text{Thermodynamic Process}:\quad \text{State}_{i} \; \xrightarrow{\;\text{Path}\;} \; \text{State}_{f}} \]

First Law of Thermodynamics

The Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics has already been discussed at the beginning of the thermodynamics section and is not repeated here. The Zeroth Law is important because it gives temperature its operational meaning. Without the Zeroth Law, we may conceptually define temperature as a property describing the thermal state of matter, but we would not have the principle that allows different bodies to be compared through thermal equilibrium and hence allows temperature to be measured using a thermometer. In this sense, the Zeroth Law is what makes temperature practically usable in the physical world.

The First Law of Thermodynamics is a more general formulation of the principle of conservation of energy than the work-energy theorem that we studied in mechanics. In mechanics, we mainly followed the transfer and transformation of mechanical energy. In thermodynamics, we extend this energy accounting to bulk matter in which energy can be transferred through thermal interaction as well as mechanical interaction. Thus, the First Law gives us a more generic framework for accounting for energy during the dynamics of a system.

At the present stage of our study, we concern ourselves primarily with two kinds of energy interaction: thermal and mechanical. Thermal energy interaction is represented by heat, while mechanical energy interaction is represented by work. Later, when other physical interactions are introduced, the general idea of energy interaction can be extended further. For example, electrical interaction can also transfer energy to or from a system. However, at this stage we restrict ourselves to thermal and mechanical interactions as required by the syllabus.

The First Law tells us how the energy supplied to a system is accounted for. When an amount of heat \( \delta Q \) is supplied to a system, that energy may increase the internal energy of the system and/or be transferred out of the system as mechanical work done on the surroundings. Thus:

\[ \delta Q=dU+\delta W \]

where \(dU\) represents the change in the internal energy of the system and \(\delta W\) represents the mechanical work done by the system on the surroundings. For a finite process, this becomes

\[ Q=\Delta U+W \qquad\text{or}\qquad \Delta U=Q-W \]

Notice something fundamental about the notation. We write \( \Delta U \), but we do not write \( \Delta Q \) or \( \Delta W \). This is because \(U\) is a state function: the change in internal energy between two states has a definite value independent of the path followed. Heat \(Q\) and work \(W\), on the other hand, are path-dependent quantities. They describe energy transfer occurring during a particular process and are not properties possessed by the system in a particular state. Therefore, there is no unique \( \Delta Q \) or \( \Delta W \) associated with two states alone.

This is also why the First Law connects quantities of fundamentally different kinds: \(U\) belongs to the state description of the system, whereas \(Q\) and \(W\) describe energy interactions occurring during the process. The First Law tells us that although the individual heat and work transfers depend on the particular path, their net effect on the system's internal energy is fixed once the initial and final states are specified.

We now need to understand what exactly is meant by internal energy. In the Kinetic Theory of Gases (KTG) chapter, we study the microscopic origin of internal energy and learn how it can be calculated for an ideal gas. Here, however, we first need the thermodynamic meaning of the concept.

Internal energy is the energy associated with the microscopic realm of the bulk matter. A macroscopic body can possess mechanical energy that we can directly describe and control through quantities such as its position, velocity, height, deformation, etc. But the matter making up that body also contains energy associated with the microscopic motion, interaction, deformation and other microscopic degrees of freedom of its constituents. We cannot generally access and control these microscopic degrees of freedom individually in the same way that we control the macroscopic mechanical state of the body. The total energy associated with this microscopic realm is what we call its internal energy \(U\).

A useful way to appreciate this is through a collision. When two bodies collide, their macroscopic mechanical energies can change. For example, after an inelastic collision, the total translational kinetic energy of the bodies is less than it was before the collision. The natural question is: Where did that energy go?

The energy has not disappeared. Part of the mechanical energy that was available in the macroscopic motion has been transferred into microscopic forms within the bodies. During a collision, the bodies may undergo microscopic deformation, heating, vibration, sound production and other microscopic changes. Energy associated with these microscopic processes contributes to the internal energy of the matter.

Thus, what appears in mechanics as a loss of accessible mechanical energy can, from the thermodynamic point of view, be understood as a transfer of energy into the microscopic realm of the matter. The First Law allows us to account for this energy even when it is no longer available in the form of macroscopic mechanical energy that we can directly track.

Key idea:
Mechanical energy can be transferred into the microscopic realm of matter.
Thermodynamics gives us a macroscopic way of accounting for that energy through the internal energy \(U\).
Thus, energy that appears to be "lost" from the mechanical description has not disappeared; it has been transferred into microscopic forms that are collectively represented by the internal energy of the system.

Therefore, the First Law connects the macroscopic energy interactions of a system with its change in internal energy:

\[ \boxed{\text{Energy supplied}}\;=\;\boxed{\text{Increase in internal energy}}\;+\;\boxed{\text{Energy transferred as work}} \]

This is the thermodynamic extension of the conservation of energy that we already encountered in mechanics.

Thermodynamic Processes and Indicator Diagrams

A thermodynamic process represents the physical trajectory along which a system transitions from an initial equilibrium state to a final equilibrium state. The geometric representation of these quasi-static transitions on coordinate planes (such as the \( P \)-\( V \), \( T \)-\( S \), or \( P \)-\( T \) diagrams) is known as an indicator diagram.

The area under any curve in a \( P \)-\( V \) indicator diagram represents the total mechanical boundary work done during that quasi-static process: \( W = \int_{V_i}^{V_f} P\,dV \).

Four fundamental ideal gas processes form the backbone of thermal analysis:

  • Isochoric (\( V = \text{const} \)): No boundary work is done (\( W = 0 \)); all supplied heat directly changes internal energy (\( Q = \Delta U \)).
  • Isobaric (\( P = \text{const} \)): Pressure remains fixed as the gas expands, performing boundary work (\( W = P\Delta V = nR\Delta T \)) and absorbing heat (\( Q = n C_p \Delta T \)).
  • Isothermal (\( T = \text{const} \)): Temperature is strictly clamped; internal energy remains unchanged (\( \Delta U = 0 \)), so \( Q = W = nRT \ln(V_f / V_i) \).
  • Adiabatic (\( Q = 0 \)): Perfectly insulated system where expansion occurs solely at the expense of internal energy (\( \Delta U = -W \)), following \( P V^\gamma = \text{constant} \).

Heat Engines, Refrigerators, and Cyclic Processes

A cyclic process is a sequence of thermodynamic operations that repeatedly returns the working substance to its exact initial thermodynamic state (\( \Delta U_{\text{cycle}} = 0 \)).

In a Heat Engine, the system executes a clockwise cycle on a \( P \)-\( V \) diagram, absorbing heat \( Q_{\text{in}} \) from a high-temperature reservoir, performing net external work \( W_{\text{net}} = \text{Area of loop} \), and rejecting waste heat \( Q_{\text{out}} \) to a low-temperature sink. The thermal efficiency is defined as:

\[ \eta = \frac{W_{\text{net}}}{Q_{\text{in}}} = 1 - \frac{Q_{\text{out}}}{Q_{\text{in}}} \]

Reversing the cycle operates as a Refrigerator or Heat Pump (counter-clockwise cycle), utilizing external work to pump heat out of a cold space and reject it into a hotter ambient environment.

Second Law of Thermodynamics and Entropy

While the First Law guarantees energy balance, it does not explain why heat flows spontaneously only from hot to cold bodies, or why 100% of heat cannot be continuously converted into mechanical work. The Second Law of Thermodynamics formalizes this fundamental irreversibility through the Kelvin-Planck and Clausius statements.

The Second Law introduces the universal state function Entropy (\( S \)), defined differentially for a reversible process as:

\[ dS = \frac{dQ_{\text{rev}}}{T} \]

For any real, spontaneous process occurring in an isolated system (or the entire universe), the total entropy always increases: \( \Delta S_{\text{universe}} \ge 0 \). This defines the thermodynamic arrow of time.
