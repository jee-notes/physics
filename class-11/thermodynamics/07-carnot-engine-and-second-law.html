Carnot Engine & Second Law of Thermodynamics

The ultimate theoretical ceiling on thermal efficiency, reversible cycles, and the directional laws of nature.

The Ideal Carnot Engine Concept

In 1824, French engineer Nicolas Léonard Sadi Carnot devised a theoretical idealized heat engine operating completely reversibly between two constant-temperature thermal reservoirs.

The Carnot Engine establishes the absolute upper thermodynamic limit on the efficiency that any heat engine can possibly achieve when operating between a high-temperature heat source at absolute temperature \( T_H \) (or \( T_1 \)) and a low-temperature heat sink at absolute temperature \( T_C \) (or \( T_2 \)).

Because all four processes in a Carnot cycle are strictly reversible and frictionless, there is zero dissipation of mechanical energy into unrecoverable heat, and all heat transfer occurs across infinitesimal temperature differences.

The Four Stages of the Carnot Cycle

The working substance (typically an ideal gas) undergoes four alternating reversible transformations in sequence:

  1. \( 1 \to 2 \): Reversible Isothermal Expansion at \( T_H \):
    The cylinder base is in contact with the hot source at \( T_H \). The gas absorbs heat \( Q_H \) and expands from \( V_1 \) to \( V_2 \): \[ Q_H = W_{1\to 2} = n R T_H \ln\left(\frac{V_2}{V_1}\right) \]
  2. \( 2 \to 3 \): Reversible Adiabatic Expansion (\( T_H \to T_C \)):
    The cylinder is placed on an insulating stand (\( Q = 0 \)). The gas expands from \( V_2 \) to \( V_3 \), cooling down to \( T_C \): \[ T_H V_2^{\gamma - 1} = T_C V_3^{\gamma - 1} \implies \frac{T_H}{T_C} = \left(\frac{V_3}{V_2}\right)^{\gamma - 1} \]
  3. \( 3 \to 4 \): Reversible Isothermal Compression at \( T_C \):
    The cylinder is placed in contact with the cold sink at \( T_C \). The gas is compressed from \( V_3 \) to \( V_4 \), rejecting heat \( Q_C \): \[ Q_C = |W_{3\to 4}| = n R T_C \ln\left(\frac{V_3}{V_4}\right) \]
  4. \( 4 \to 1 \): Reversible Adiabatic Compression (\( T_C \to T_H \)):
    The cylinder is placed back on the insulating stand. The gas is compressed from \( V_4 \) back to \( V_1 \), heating back up to \( T_H \): \[ T_C V_4^{\gamma - 1} = T_H V_1^{\gamma - 1} \implies \frac{T_H}{T_C} = \left(\frac{V_4}{V_1}\right)^{\gamma - 1} \]

Derivation of Carnot Efficiency Formula

Equating the temperature-volume ratios from the two adiabatic legs:

\[ \left(\frac{V_3}{V_2}\right)^{\gamma - 1} = \left(\frac{V_4}{V_1}\right)^{\gamma - 1} \implies \frac{V_3}{V_2} = \frac{V_4}{V_1} \implies \frac{V_2}{V_1} = \frac{V_3}{V_4} \]

Now taking the ratio of heats exchanged:

\[ \frac{Q_C}{Q_H} = \frac{n R T_C \ln(V_3 / V_4)}{n R T_H \ln(V_2 / V_1)} = \frac{T_C}{T_H} \]

Substituting this ratio into the general engine efficiency formula:

\[ \eta_{\text{Carnot}} = 1 - \frac{Q_C}{Q_H} = 1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H} = \frac{T_H - T_C}{T_H} \] where:
  • \( \eta_{\text{Carnot}} \): Carnot thermal efficiency (Dimensionless, \( 0 < \eta < 1 \))
  • \( T_H \): Absolute temperature of the hot source (in Kelvin)
  • \( T_C \): Absolute temperature of the cold sink (in Kelvin)
Critical Takeaways for Carnot Efficiency:
  • \( \eta \) depends only on the temperatures of the source and sink; it is completely independent of the working substance (air, helium, steam, etc.).
  • \( \eta = 1 \) (\( 100\% \)) is achievable only if the sink temperature \( T_C = 0 \, \text{K} \) (Absolute Zero), which is physically unattainable by the Third Law of Thermodynamics.
  • To increase efficiency, one must either increase \( T_H \) or decrease \( T_C \).

Reversed Carnot Cycle: Ideal Refrigerator and Heat Pump

Because every stage of the Carnot cycle is completely reversible, operating the cycle in reverse (counter-clockwise on \( P \)-\( V \)) yields the ideal refrigerator and heat pump.

1. Carnot Refrigerator COP:

\[ \beta_{\text{Carnot}} = \frac{Q_C}{W} = \frac{Q_C}{Q_H - Q_C} = \frac{T_C}{T_H - T_C} \]

2. Carnot Heat Pump COP:

\[ \text{COP}_{\text{hp, Carnot}} = \frac{Q_H}{W} = \frac{T_H}{T_H - T_C} = \beta_{\text{Carnot}} + 1 \]

Carnot's Theorem and Corollaries

Carnot formalized the theoretical supremacy of reversible cycles in two fundamental theorems:

Carnot's Theorem:
  1. No heat engine operating between two given constant-temperature thermal reservoirs can ever be more efficient than a reversible Carnot engine operating between the exact same reservoirs: \[ \eta_{\text{irreversible}} \le \eta_{\text{reversible}} = 1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H} \]
  2. All reversible heat engines operating between the same two thermal reservoirs have identical efficiency, regardless of the nature or properties of the working substance.

Statements of the Second Law of Thermodynamics

While the First Law guarantees that energy must balance, the Second Law of Thermodynamics dictates the permissible direction of spontaneous natural processes.

1. Kelvin-Planck Statement (Engine Formulation):

It is impossible to construct a cyclic device that operates in a cycle and produces no other effect than the absorption of heat from a single thermal reservoir and the complete conversion of this heat into an equivalent amount of mechanical work.

\[ \eta < 100\% \quad \text{always} \]

A 100% efficient heat engine (a Perpetual Motion Machine of the Second Kind, PMM-2) is fundamentally impossible. Every engine must reject some waste heat to a colder sink.

2. Clausius Statement (Refrigerator Formulation):

It is impossible to construct a device that operates in a cycle and produces no other effect than the transfer of heat from a cooler body to a hotter body without requiring external work input.

\[ W_{\text{input}} > 0 \quad \text{for any refrigerator} \]

Heat cannot spontaneously flow uphill from cold to hot without external work.

Proof of Equivalence Between Kelvin-Planck and Clausius Statements

Although phrased differently, the Kelvin-Planck and Clausius statements are logically equivalent: a violation of either statement leads directly to a violation of the other.

Proof 1: Violation of Clausius implies Violation of Kelvin-Planck

  1. Suppose a hypothetical Clausius-violating refrigerator transfers heat \( Q \) from a cold reservoir at \( T_C \) to a hot reservoir at \( T_H \) with zero external work input (\( W = 0 \)).
  2. Now run a regular heat engine between the same reservoirs, absorbing heat \( Q_H \) from \( T_H \), delivering useful work \( W = Q_H - Q_C \), and rejecting heat \( Q \) to the cold sink at \( T_C \).
  3. Couple the two devices into a single combined composite system:
    • The cold reservoir receives heat \( Q \) from the engine and loses heat \( Q \) to the refrigerator: net heat exchange at \( T_C \) is \( Q - Q = 0 \).
    • The hot reservoir provides \( Q_H \) and receives \( Q \): net heat drawn from \( T_H \) is \( Q_H - Q \).
    • Net work produced by the composite engine: \( W_{\text{net}} = Q_H - Q \).
  4. The composite machine extracts heat from a single reservoir at \( T_H \) and converts \( 100\% \) of it into mechanical work with zero heat rejected anywhere else, directly violating the Kelvin-Planck Statement.
Hence, the Kelvin-Planck statement and Clausius statement are two complementary perspectives of the same underlying physical truth: spontaneous thermal irreversibility.
