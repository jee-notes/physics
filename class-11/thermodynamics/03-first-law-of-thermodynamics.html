First Law of Thermodynamics

The universal law of conservation of energy applied to thermodynamic systems.

Statement of the First Law of Thermodynamics

The First Law of Thermodynamics is the generalization of the universal law of conservation of energy to include thermal energy transfers (heat) alongside mechanical work.

Formal Statement:

Energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be transformed from one form into another. The net amount of energy transferred to a thermodynamic system in the form of heat is equal to the increase in the internal energy of the system plus the external work done by the system on its surroundings.

The First Law asserts that a perpetual motion machine of the first kind (PMM-1) — an engine that could continuously produce mechanical work without consuming an equivalent amount of heat or chemical energy — is physically impossible.

Mathematical Formulation: dQ = dU + dW

For an infinitesimal quasi-static thermodynamic change, the First Law is expressed differentially as:

\[ dQ = dU + dW \] where:
  • \( dQ \): Infinitesimal heat supplied to the system (Inexact differential, SI Unit: Joule (\( \text{J} \)))
  • \( dU \): Infinitesimal change in internal energy (Exact differential of a state function, SI Unit: Joule (\( \text{J} \)))
  • \( dW = P\,dV \): Infinitesimal boundary work done by the system (Inexact differential, SI Unit: Joule (\( \text{J} \)))

For a finite process transitioning between equilibrium state 1 and equilibrium state 2:

\[ Q = \Delta U + W \quad \implies \quad \Delta U = Q - W \] where \( \Delta U = U_2 - U_1 \).

Sign Conventions for Heat, Work, and Internal Energy

Strict adherence to the algebraic sign conventions of the First Law is essential for accurate calculations in physics:

Thermodynamic Quantity Algebraic Condition Sign in First Law Physical Meaning
Heat (\( Q \)) Heat enters the system \( Q > 0 \) (Positive) System absorbs thermal energy from surroundings.
Heat leaves the system \( Q < 0 \) (Negative) System rejects thermal energy to surroundings.
Work (\( W \)) System expands (\( dV > 0 \)) \( W > 0 \) (Positive) Gas does work on external surroundings.
System compresses (\( dV < 0 \)) \( W < 0 \) (Negative) External surroundings do work on the gas.
Internal Energy (\( \Delta U \)) Temperature rises (\( \Delta T > 0 \)) \( \Delta U > 0 \) (Positive) Internal microscopic kinetic energy increases.
Temperature drops (\( \Delta T < 0 \)) \( \Delta U < 0 \) (Negative) Internal microscopic kinetic energy decreases.

Differential and Integrated Forms of First Law

Substituting the ideal gas relations into the differential First Law gives its most versatile operational formulas:

\[ dQ = n \, C_v \, dT + P \, dV \] Using the ideal gas equation of state in differential form (\( P\,dV + V\,dP = nR\,dT \)): \[ dQ = n \, C_p \, dT - V \, dP \]

Specialized Formulations for Standard Processes:

  • Isochoric Process (\( dV = 0 \)): \[ dW = 0 \implies Q = \Delta U = n C_v \Delta T \]
  • Isobaric Process (\( dP = 0 \)): \[ W = P\Delta V = nR\Delta T \implies Q = \Delta U + W = n C_v \Delta T + n R \Delta T = n C_p \Delta T \]
  • Isothermal Process (\( dT = 0 \)): \[ \Delta U = 0 \implies Q = W = nRT \ln\left(\frac{V_f}{V_i}\right) \]
  • Adiabatic Process (\( dQ = 0 \)): \[ Q = 0 \implies \Delta U = -W \implies W = -\Delta U = \frac{nR(T_i - T_f)}{\gamma - 1} \]
  • Cyclic Process (Returns to initial state): \[ \Delta U_{\text{net}} = 0 \implies Q_{\text{net}} = W_{\text{net}} \]

Physical Consequences and Energy Conservation

The First Law reveals several fundamental characteristics of macroscopic physical systems:

  1. Equivalence of Mechanical Work and Thermal Energy: Mechanical energy can be completely converted into heat (as established in Joule's mechanical equivalent of heat experiments).
  2. Internal Energy as an Integrating Factor: Although neither \( dQ \) nor \( dW \) is an exact differential individually, their difference \( dQ - dW = dU \) is an exact differential of a true state function.
  3. Limitation of the First Law: The First Law is strictly a balance sheet of energy. It asserts that energy must be conserved in any transformation, but it makes no prediction about whether a given process can occur spontaneously or which direction heat will flow. (This directional necessity requires the Second Law).
The First Law does not say which processes are physically possible in nature — it only states how energy must balance if a process does occur.

Solved Example: Application of the First Law

A sample of ideal gas expands from volume \( V_1 \) to \( V_2 \) while absorbing \( 500 \, \text{J} \) of heat. If the work done by the gas during this expansion is \( 320 \, \text{J} \), calculate the change in the internal energy of the gas and state whether its temperature increased or decreased.

Solution:

From the First Law of Thermodynamics:

\[ \Delta U = Q - W = (+500 \, \text{J}) - (+320 \, \text{J}) = +180 \, \text{J} \]

Since \( \Delta U > 0 \), and for an ideal gas \( \Delta U = n C_v \Delta T \), the positive internal energy change implies that \( \Delta T > 0 \). The temperature of the gas increases.
