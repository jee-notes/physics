Entropy, Clausius Inequality, & Irreversibility

The quantitative measure of disorder, the Clausius theorem, and the thermodynamic arrow of time.

Clausius Theorem and Clausius Inequality

Rudolf Clausius generalized the Second Law of Thermodynamics to arbitrary cyclic processes, arriving at the fundamental Clausius Inequality:

\[ \oint \frac{dQ}{T} \le 0 \] where:
  • \( \oint \frac{dQ}{T} = 0 \): Strictly for a Reversible Cycle.
  • \( \oint \frac{dQ}{T} < 0 \): For any real Irreversible Cycle.
  • \( \oint \frac{dQ}{T} > 0 \): Physically Impossible Cycle (violates Second Law).

For a Carnot cycle operating reversibly between \( T_H \) and \( T_C \): \[ \oint \frac{dQ}{T} = \frac{Q_H}{T_H} - \frac{Q_C}{T_C} = 0 \quad \left(\text{since } \frac{Q_H}{T_H} = \frac{Q_C}{T_C}\right) \]

Definition of Entropy as a State Variable

Since \( \oint \frac{dQ_{\text{rev}}}{T} = 0 \) for any reversible closed loop, the line integral of \( \frac{dQ_{\text{rev}}}{T} \) between two equilibrium states \( A \) and \( B \) is completely independent of the reversible path chosen.

This mathematical property proves that there exists an exact macroscopic state function called Entropy (\( S \)):

\[ dS = \left( \frac{dQ_{\text{rev}}}{T} \right) \] \[ \Delta S = S_B - S_A = \int_A^B \frac{dQ_{\text{rev}}}{T} \] where:
  • \( S \): Entropy (Extensive State Function, SI Unit: \( \text{J/K} \) (Joules per Kelvin), Dimension: \( [M L^2 T^{-2} K^{-1}] \))
  • \( dQ_{\text{rev}} \): Heat exchanged along any arbitrary reversible path connecting state \( A \) to state \( B \)
  • \( T \): Absolute temperature at which the heat exchange occurs (in Kelvin)
Even if a process is irreversible, the entropy change \( \Delta S = S_{\text{final}} - S_{\text{initial}} \) of the system is calculated by devising an imaginary reversible path connecting the same initial and final equilibrium states and integrating \( \int \frac{dQ_{\text{rev}}}{T} \).

Entropy Change of an Ideal Gas in Various Thermodynamic Processes

From the differential First Law: \( dQ_{\text{rev}} = n C_v dT + P dV \). Dividing by \( T \): \[ dS = \frac{dQ_{\text{rev}}}{T} = n C_v \frac{dT}{T} + \frac{P}{T} dV = n C_v \frac{dT}{T} + n R \frac{dV}{V} \] Integrating between state \( 1 \, (P_1, V_1, T_1) \) and state \( 2 \, (P_2, V_2, T_2) \):

Master Formulas for Entropy Change of an Ideal Gas: \[ \Delta S = n \, C_v \ln\left(\frac{T_2}{T_1}\right) + n \, R \ln\left(\frac{V_2}{V_1}\right) \] Using \( C_p = C_v + R \) and \( PV = nRT \), in terms of \( T \) and \( P \): \[ \Delta S = n \, C_p \ln\left(\frac{T_2}{T_1}\right) - n \, R \ln\left(\frac{P_2}{P_1}\right) \] In terms of \( P \) and \( V \): \[ \Delta S = n \, C_v \ln\left(\frac{P_2}{P_1}\right) + n \, C_p \ln\left(\frac{V_2}{V_1}\right) \]

Standard Process Specializations:

  • Isochoric Process (\( V_1 = V_2 \)): \[ \Delta S = n \, C_v \ln\left(\frac{T_2}{T_1}\right) = n \, C_v \ln\left(\frac{P_2}{P_1}\right) \]
  • Isobaric Process (\( P_1 = P_2 \)): \[ \Delta S = n \, C_p \ln\left(\frac{T_2}{T_1}\right) = n \, C_p \ln\left(\frac{V_2}{V_1}\right) \]
  • Isothermal Process (\( T_1 = T_2 \)): \[ \Delta S = n \, R \ln\left(\frac{V_2}{V_1}\right) = n \, R \ln\left(\frac{P_1}{P_2}\right) \]
  • Reversible Adiabatic Process (\( dQ_{\text{rev}} = 0 \)): \[ dS = 0 \implies \Delta S = 0 \quad \implies S = \text{constant} \quad \text{(Isentropic Process)} \]
  • Free Expansion of Ideal Gas into Vacuum (\( V_1 \to V_2 \)): \[ \Delta T = 0 \implies \Delta S = n \, R \ln\left(\frac{V_2}{V_1}\right) > 0 \]

Principle of Increase of Entropy for Isolated Systems

The Principle of Increase of Entropy is the most universal formulation of the Second Law of Thermodynamics:

\[ \Delta S_{\text{universe}} = \Delta S_{\text{system}} + \Delta S_{\text{surroundings}} \ge 0 \]
  • \( \Delta S_{\text{universe}} = 0 \): For an idealized, completely reversible process.
  • \( \Delta S_{\text{universe}} > 0 \): For any real, spontaneous, natural irreversible process.
  • \( \Delta S_{\text{universe}} < 0 \): Physically impossible.

The entropy of an isolated system (or the entire cosmos) can never decrease; it continually increases towards a maximum equilibrium state.

Microscopic Interpretation of Entropy

Ludwig Boltzmann established the statistical-mechanical foundation of entropy, linking macroscopic thermodynamics to microscopic molecular disorder:

\[ S = k_B \ln \Omega \] where:
  • \( S \): Macroscopic entropy
  • \( k_B \approx 1.38 \times 10^{-23} \, \text{J/K} \): Boltzmann Constant
  • \( \Omega \): Thermodynamic probability (the number of microscopic quantum states, or microstates, corresponding to the observed macroscopic state)

Spontaneous processes progress from states of lower probability (ordered microstates) to states of maximum probability (disordered microstates).

Degradation of Energy and the Thermodynamic Arrow of Time

Although the total quantity of energy in the universe is strictly conserved (First Law), the quality (availability) of that energy continuously degrades over time:

  • High-grade energy (organized mechanical work, electrical energy, chemical bonds) is relentlessly dissipated through friction, electrical resistance, and heat transfer into low-grade thermal energy distributed uniformly at ambient temperature.
  • Once thermalized, this low-temperature heat cannot be fully reclaimed into useful mechanical work without generating even greater entropy elsewhere.
  • The relentless increase of entropy defines the unique, non-reversible Arrow of Time in the physical universe.
