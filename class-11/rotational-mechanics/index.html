Rotational Mechanics

The complete dynamics of rigid bodies — torque, moment of inertia, angular momentum, and rolling.

Conceptual Overview

Rotational mechanics extends Newton's laws from point particles to rigid bodies — objects with a definite shape that can both translate and rotate. While translational motion is described by force, mass, and linear velocity, rotational motion introduces their angular counterparts: torque, moment of inertia, and angular velocity.

The chapter builds a complete parallel structure: just as \(\vec{F} = m\vec{a}\) governs translation, \(\vec{\tau} = I\vec{\alpha}\) governs rotation. Just as linear momentum \(\vec{p} = m\vec{v}\) is conserved when no external force acts, angular momentum \(\vec{L} = I\vec{\omega}\) is conserved when no external torque acts.

The Translation–Rotation Analogy

Every concept in translational mechanics has a rotational analogue. This structural parallel is the key organising principle of the chapter:

  • Displacement \(s\) ↔ Angular displacement \(\theta\)
  • Velocity \(v\) ↔ Angular velocity \(\omega\)
  • Acceleration \(a\) ↔ Angular acceleration \(\alpha\)
  • Mass \(m\) ↔ Moment of inertia \(I\)
  • Force \(\vec{F}\) ↔ Torque \(\vec{\tau}\)
  • Momentum \(\vec{p} = m\vec{v}\) ↔ Angular momentum \(\vec{L} = I\vec{\omega}\)
  • Kinetic energy \(\frac{1}{2}mv^2\) ↔ Rotational KE \(\frac{1}{2}I\omega^2\)
  • Work \(W = Fs\) ↔ Rotational work \(W = \tau\theta\)

The analogy is not superficial — it arises because the underlying mathematics (Newton's second law applied to each particle) generates both sets of equations.

Moment of Inertia: The Rotational Mass

Moment of inertia \(I\) plays the role of mass in rotational dynamics. It measures how resistant a body is to angular acceleration, and depends not only on the total mass but on how that mass is distributed relative to the axis of rotation:

\[ I = \sum m_i r_i^2 \qquad \text{(discrete)} \qquad I = \int r^2\,dm \qquad \text{(continuous)} \]

Two key theorems — the parallel axis theorem and the perpendicular axis theorem — allow the moment of inertia about any axis to be calculated from a known result about a simpler axis.

Torque and the Law of Rotation

Torque is the rotational analogue of force. It measures the tendency of a force to cause rotation about an axis:

\[ \vec{\tau} = \vec{r} \times \vec{F} \]

Newton's second law for rotation about a fixed axis states:

\[ \tau_{\text{net}} = I\alpha \]

This single equation governs all fixed-axis rotation problems — from simple pulleys to rolling bodies and compound pendulums.

Angular Momentum and Its Conservation

Angular momentum is the rotational analogue of linear momentum:

\[ \vec{L} = \vec{r} \times \vec{p} \qquad \text{(particle)} \qquad L = I\omega \qquad \text{(rigid body, fixed axis)} \]

When no net external torque acts on a system, angular momentum is conserved. This principle explains the spinning skater who speeds up by pulling in her arms, the stability of gyroscopes, and the orbital mechanics of planets.
