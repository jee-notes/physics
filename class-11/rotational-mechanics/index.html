Rotational Mechanics

Rigid body dynamics — torque, moment of inertia, angular momentum.

Conceptual Overview

Rotational mechanics extends Newton's laws from point particles to rigid bodies.

Rigid bodies are a special case of system of particles that are idealized such that their shape and size is fixed and finite and so, they can undergo both translation and rotation.

NLM is dynamics of particle body and Rotation is dynamics of rigid body.

Unlike a particle, which is completely described by its position \(\vec{r}\), a rigid body possesses both a position \(\vec{r}\) and an orientation \(\theta\). Together, these define the body's spatial state—the complete description of how the body occupies space, or equivalently, its spatial configuration at a given instant.

Because a rigid body possesses an additional degree of freedom—its orientation—describing its motion requires new physical quantities.

Along with the familiar translational quantities such as force, mass and linear velocity, we therefore introduce their rotational counterparts: angular displacement, angular velocity, angular acceleration, torque and moment of inertia.

Building the Rotational Framework

The physical quantities of rotational mechanics are defined so that the mathematical framework of translational mechanics can be reused to describe rotational motion. Consequently, many fundamental quantities in translation have natural rotational counterparts:

  • Displacement \(s\) ↔ Angular displacement \(\theta\)
  • Velocity \(v\) ↔ Angular velocity \(\omega\)
  • Acceleration \(a\) ↔ Angular acceleration \(\alpha\)
  • Mass \(m\) ↔ Moment of inertia \(I\)
  • Force \(\vec{F}\) ↔ Torque \(\vec{\tau}\)
  • Momentum \(\vec{p}=m\vec{v}\) ↔ Angular momentum \(\vec{L}=I\vec{\omega}\)
  • Kinetic Energy \(\frac12mv^2\) ↔ Rotational Kinetic Energy \(\frac12I\omega^2\)
  • Work \(W=Fs\) ↔ Rotational Work \(W=\tau\theta\)

This parallel is not merely an analogy. A rigid body is ultimately a collection of particles obeying Newton's laws. The rotational quantities are therefore defined so that the equations governing rotational motion retain the same mathematical structure as those governing translational motion. For example,

\[ \vec{F}=m\vec{a} \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad \vec{\tau}=I\vec{\alpha} \]

This parallel structure allows many concepts, derivations and problem-solving techniques developed for particle dynamics to be carried over naturally to rigid-body dynamics.

Moment of Inertia: The Rotational Mass

Moment of inertia \(I\) plays the role of mass in rotational dynamics. It measures how resistant a body is to angular acceleration, and depends not only on the total mass but on how that mass is distributed relative to the axis of rotation:

\[ I = \sum m_i r_i^2 \qquad \text{(discrete)} \qquad I = \int r^2\,dm \qquad \text{(continuous)} \]

Two key theorems — the parallel axis theorem and the perpendicular axis theorem — allow the moment of inertia about any axis to be calculated from a known result about a simpler axis.

Torque and the Law of Rotation

Torque is the rotational analogue of force. It measures the tendency of a force to cause rotation about an axis:

\[ \vec{\tau} = \vec{r} \times \vec{F} \]

Newton's second law for rotation about a fixed axis states:

\[ \tau_{\text{net}} = I\alpha \]

This single equation governs all fixed-axis rotation problems — from simple pulleys to rolling bodies and compound pendulums.

Angular Momentum and Its Conservation

Angular momentum is the rotational analogue of linear momentum:

\[ \vec{L} = \vec{r} \times \vec{p} \qquad \text{(particle)} \qquad L = I\omega \qquad \text{(rigid body, fixed axis)} \]

When no net external torque acts on a system, angular momentum is conserved. This principle explains the spinning skater who speeds up by pulling in her arms, the stability of gyroscopes, and the orbital mechanics of planets.
