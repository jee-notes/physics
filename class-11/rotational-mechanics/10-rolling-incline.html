Rolling on Inclines and Friction

Dynamics of rolling bodies on slopes — acceleration, friction requirements, and why shape matters.

Rolling Down an Incline

A body of mass \(M\), radius \(R\), and moment of inertia \(I_{\text{CM}} = Mk^2\) rolls without slipping down a rough incline of angle \(\theta\). Two equations govern the motion:

Translation (along the incline):

\[Mg\sin\theta - f = Ma\]

Rotation (about the CM):

\[fR = I_{\text{CM}}\alpha = Mk^2 \cdot \frac{a}{R}\]

Solving these simultaneously:

\[a = \frac{g\sin\theta}{1 + k^2/R^2}\]

And the friction force:

\[f = \frac{Mg\sin\theta \cdot k^2/R^2}{1 + k^2/R^2}\]
The acceleration depends on the shape of the body (through \(k^2/R^2\)) but not on its mass or radius. A solid sphere and a tiny solid marble accelerate equally — the larger mass is exactly compensated by the larger inertia.

Race of Rolling Bodies

Since acceleration \(a = \frac{g\sin\theta}{1 + k^2/R^2}\), bodies with smaller \(k^2/R^2\) accelerate faster. The ranking from fastest to slowest down the same incline:

  • Solid sphere (\(k^2/R^2 = 2/5\)) — fastest
  • Solid cylinder / disc (\(k^2/R^2 = 1/2\))
  • Hollow sphere (\(k^2/R^2 = 2/3\))
  • Thin ring / hollow cylinder (\(k^2/R^2 = 1\)) — slowest

A sliding block (no rotation, \(k^2/R^2 = 0\) effectively) has \(a = g\sin\theta\) and always beats all rolling bodies.

Minimum Friction for Pure Rolling

For the body to roll without slipping, the required friction must not exceed the maximum available static friction:

\[f \leq \mu_s N = \mu_s Mg\cos\theta\]

Substituting the expression for \(f\):

\[\frac{Mg\sin\theta \cdot k^2/R^2}{1 + k^2/R^2} \leq \mu_s Mg\cos\theta\] \[\mu_s \geq \frac{\tan\theta \cdot k^2/R^2}{1 + k^2/R^2}\]

If \(\mu_s\) is less than this critical value, the body slips and slides down the incline (kinetic friction applies, and the problem becomes more complex).

Energy Method for Rolling on Inclines

Since static friction does no work in pure rolling, energy conservation provides a clean alternative:

\[Mgh = \frac{1}{2}Mv^2 + \frac{1}{2}I_{\text{CM}}\omega^2 = \frac{1}{2}Mv^2\left(1 + \frac{k^2}{R^2}\right)\] \[v = \sqrt{\frac{2gh}{1 + k^2/R^2}}\]

where \(h\) is the vertical height descended. This gives the speed at the bottom without needing to find friction or acceleration explicitly.

Rolling Up an Incline

A body projected up a rough incline decelerates. Friction now acts up the incline (to maintain the rolling condition as gravity decelerates both translation and rotation). The deceleration is the same magnitude as the acceleration going down:

\[a = \frac{g\sin\theta}{1 + k^2/R^2}\]

The body comes to a momentary stop and then rolls back down with the same acceleration. No energy is lost if rolling is maintained throughout.

Transition from Sliding to Rolling

A sphere placed on a rough horizontal surface with initial velocity \(v_0\) (no spin) will slide and spin up due to kinetic friction. The sliding decelerates (friction reduces \(v\)) while spinning accelerates (friction provides torque to increase \(\omega\)). Eventually:

\[v = \omega R \qquad \text{(pure rolling achieved)}\]

After this point, friction becomes static and does no further work. The final rolling speed for a solid sphere:

\[v_f = \frac{5}{7}v_0\]

The lost kinetic energy is dissipated as heat during the sliding phase.

The transition problem is solved using kinetic friction for both translation (deceleration) and rotation (angular acceleration) until the rolling condition is met. After that, dynamics change fundamentally — kinetic friction becomes static friction.
