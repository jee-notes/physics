Conservation of Angular Momentum

When the net external torque vanishes, angular momentum is preserved — with dramatic consequences.

Statement

If the net external torque on a system about a point (or axis) is zero, the total angular momentum about that point (or axis) remains constant:

\[\vec{\tau}_{\text{ext}} = 0 \implies \vec{L} = \text{constant}\]

For a rigid body about a fixed axis:

\[I_1 \omega_1 = I_2 \omega_2\]

If the moment of inertia decreases, the angular velocity must increase to keep \(L\) constant, and vice versa.

Classic Applications

1. Spinning Figure Skater

A skater spins slowly with arms extended (\(I\) large, \(\omega\) small). Pulling arms in reduces \(I\); angular velocity \(\omega\) increases dramatically to conserve \(L\). The skater spins faster without any external torque.

2. Diver's Tuck

A diver leaves the board with a certain angular momentum. By tucking (reducing \(I\)), the diver spins faster to complete somersaults, then extends to slow the rotation before entering the water.

3. Collapsing Star

A rotating star that collapses under gravity shrinks to a fraction of its original size. Its moment of inertia decreases enormously (\(I \propto R^2\)), so its angular velocity increases by the same factor. Neutron stars spin at hundreds of revolutions per second.

4. Rotating Platform

A person stands on a freely rotating turntable holding a spinning wheel. Flipping the wheel reverses its angular momentum; the platform must rotate in the opposite direction to compensate, keeping total \(L\) constant.

Angular Momentum in Two-Body Problems

When two bodies interact (e.g., a disc and a ring dropped onto it), their internal torques are equal and opposite. The total angular momentum is conserved:

\[I_1\omega_1 + I_2\omega_2 = (I_1 + I_2)\omega_f\]

This is the rotational analogue of a perfectly inelastic collision. Energy is not conserved — friction between surfaces converts kinetic energy to heat until both rotate at the same \(\omega_f\).

Energy loss in such problems can be found by comparing initial and final rotational KE: \[\Delta K = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{I_1 I_2}{I_1 + I_2} \cdot (\omega_1 - \omega_2)^2\] This is entirely analogous to the kinetic energy loss in a linear perfectly inelastic collision.

When Torque is Zero About One Point but Not Another

Angular momentum conservation applies about the specific point or axis where the net torque is zero. A common situation: gravity exerts no torque about the centre of the Earth, but it does exert torque about other points.

In collision problems, if impulsive forces act at a hinge, angular momentum is conserved about the hinge (impulsive force exerts no torque about the hinge because its lever arm is zero), but not about other points.
