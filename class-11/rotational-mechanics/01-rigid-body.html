Rigid Body Motion

The Kinematic constraint characterising Rigid Body Motion.

What is a Rigid Body?

A rigid body is an idealized system of particles in which the distance between every pair of particles remains constant throughout the motion. This condition is known as the rigid body constraint.

No real object is perfectly rigid — every material undergoes some deformation when subjected to forces. However, in many situations these deformations are negligible, making the rigid-body approximation an excellent model.

The rigid body constraint dramatically simplifies the description of motion. Instead of tracking the positions of a very large number of particles individually, the motion of the entire body can be described using only a few variables representing its position and orientation.

Instead of tracking the positions of every particle individually, we introduce a new quantity called the orientation of a rigid body. It provides a compact description of how the entire body is rotated relative to a chosen reference.

A change in orientation is called rotational motion. This change is measured by an angular displacement. However, an angle is always measured between two rays. Therefore, every particle of the rigid body requires its own initial and final reference rays to measure its angular displacement.

The reference rays are different for different particles because they originate from different points of the body. Nevertheless, all these rays are associated with the same axis of rotation. The axis therefore provides the common geometric reference for the entire rigid body. Although every particle has its own pair of reference rays, the angular displacement measured between those rays is identical for every particle. Thus, the orientation of the entire rigid body can be described using a single angular coordinate together with the axis of rotation.

The rigid body constraint is a geometric property of the body and is therefore independent of the reference frame. Every observer agrees that the distances between particles remain constant. Just as your height does not change when observed from a different frame, the rigidity of a body is also frame - independent and the distances among the particles are relative to each other.

In this chapter, we restrict ourselves to rotation about a single axis, which is the case encountered in exam syllabus. For a planar (2D) body, every particle moves in a circle about a common centre. For a three-dimensional body, every particle moves in a circle whose plane is perpendicular to a common axis of rotation. These planes are parallel to one another, and their centres lie on a straight line called the axis of rotation. The axis itself may translate from one position to another (as in a rolling wheel), but its orientation remains unchanged throughout the motion considered in this chapter.

General rigid-body motion in three dimensions requires three independent angular coordinates to describe the body's orientation. In this chapter, however, we study only one-dimensional rotational motion, where the orientation is constrained so that a single angular coordinate \(\theta\) completely specifies the body's orientation.

Degrees of Freedom and Types of Rotation

A free particle in three-dimensional space has 3 degrees of freedom, corresponding to its three position coordinates \((x,y,z)\).

A free rigid body in three-dimensional space has 6 degrees of freedom:

  • 3 translational — the position of its centre of mass \((x,y,z)\).
  • 3 rotational — the orientation of the body (described, for example, by Euler angles).

If the translational motion of the centre of mass is confined to a plane, the translational degrees of freedom reduce from three to two. However, this translational constraint alone does not reduce the rotational degrees of freedom. The body may still possess all three rotational degrees of freedom if the orientation of its axis of rotation is allowed to change.

To reduce the rotational degrees of freedom to one, an additional rotational constraint is required. Throughout this chapter, we study only motions in which the orientation of the axis of rotation remains fixed. The axis itself may either remain fixed in space or translate, but its orientation does not change. Under this constraint, the body's orientation is completely specified by a single angular coordinate \(\theta\).

Thus, in the rotational motions studied in this chapter, the body has only 1 rotational degree of freedom.

Examples include a rotating door, a ceiling fan, a wheel mounted on a fixed axle, a rolling cylinder moving in a straight line, and a movable pulley in an Atwood machine. The turning wheel of a vehicle taking a turn or a sphere rolling along an arbitrary curved path do not satisfy this constraint because the orientation of the rotation axis changes during the motion.

Accordingly, the one-dimensional rotational motion studied in the syllabus may occur in two ways:

  • Fixed-axis rotation: The axis of rotation remains fixed in space. The body has 1 rotational degree of freedom and 0 translational degrees of freedom. Its orientation is completely described by the angular coordinate \(\theta(t)\). Examples: a door about its hinge, a ceiling fan, or a wheel mounted on a fixed axle.
  • Translating-axis rotation: The axis of rotation translates while its orientation remains unchanged. The body therefore undergoes combined translation and rotation. The rotational motion still has only 1 rotational degree of freedom, while the body may simultaneously possess translational degrees of freedom depending on the problem constraints. For example, a rolling cylinder moving in a straight line has 2 translational and 1 rotational degree of freedom, whereas a movable pulley may possess only 1 translational degree of freedom in a constrained system.
The defining feature of all rotational motion studied in this chapter is not whether the axis is fixed in space, but whether the orientation of the axis of rotation remains fixed. Under this rotational constraint, a single angular coordinate \(\theta\) completely specifies the body's orientation. General three-dimensional rotational motion, in which the orientation of the axis itself changes and additional angular coordinates are required, lies beyond the scope of this chapter.

Types of Motion

For a point mass, we classified motion as rectilinear or curvilinear because the state of a particle is completely described by its position. A rigid body, however, possesses both position and orientation. Accordingly, the motion of a rigid body is classified based on changes in these two quantities.

1. Pure Translation

In pure translation, the position of the body changes while its orientation remains unchanged. Every particle of the body has the same instantaneous velocity, and every line drawn in the body remains parallel to its original direction.

A common source of confusion is translatory circular motion. Students often associate a circular path with rotation, but this is not necessarily true. A rigid body can move along a circular path while maintaining the same orientation throughout its motion. In such cases, the motion is still pure translation. Every particle follows a circular path of the same radius but with different centres, and all particles have the same instantaneous velocity.

Examples: a box sliding on a frictionless floor, a car moving approximately in a straight line, and a puja thali moved in a vertical circular path during aarti while being kept horizontal so that the burning oil lamp and other objects placed on it do not fall.

2. Pure Rotation

In pure rotation, the orientation of the body changes while the axis of rotation remains fixed in space. The body has no translational motion. Every particle moves in a circle about the axis and has the same angular velocity \(\omega\). A particle at perpendicular distance \(r\) from the axis has linear speed \(v=r\omega\).

This is the simplest rotational motion studied in this chapter and has 0 translational and 1 rotational degree of freedom.

Examples: a door about its hinge, a ceiling fan, a wheel mounted on a fixed axle, and a fixed pulley.

3. Combined Translation and Rotation

In combined motion, both the position and the orientation of the rigid body change simultaneously. The axis of rotation translates through space while its orientation remains unchanged. The body therefore possesses translational degrees of freedom together with a single rotational degree of freedom.

Depending on the constraints imposed on the body, the translational motion may have one, two or three degrees of freedom.

  • 0T + 1R: Door, ceiling fan, fixed pulley.
  • 1T + 1R: Movable pulley in an Atwood machine.
  • 2T + 1R: A drone propeller while the drone maintains a constant altitude.
  • 3T + 1R: A drone propeller while the drone flies freely in space.
Throughout this chapter, every rotational motion has only one rotational degree of freedom. The difference between problems lies only in the number of translational degrees of freedom permitted by the constraints. General three-dimensional rotation, in which the orientation of the rotation axis itself changes, lies beyond the scope of this chapter.
