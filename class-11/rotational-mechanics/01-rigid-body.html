Rigid Body and Types of Motion

The idealisation that bridges particle dynamics and the real world of extended objects.

What is a Rigid Body?

A rigid body is a system of particles in which the distance between every pair of particles remains constant, regardless of what forces act on it. No real object is perfectly rigid — all materials deform under stress — but many objects deform so little that the rigid-body approximation is excellent.

The rigid body constraint dramatically simplifies analysis: instead of tracking the positions and velocities of \(10^{23}\) molecules, we need only a few variables to describe the entire body's motion.

Types of Motion

1. Pure Translation

Every particle of the body has the same velocity at every instant. The body moves without rotating — all lines drawn in the body remain parallel to their original directions.

Examples: a box sliding on a frictionless floor, a car moving in a straight line (to a first approximation).

2. Pure Rotation

The body rotates about a fixed axis. Every particle moves in a circle centred on the axis, and every particle has the same angular velocity \(\omega\). A particle at distance \(r\) from the axis has linear speed \(v = r\omega\).

Examples: a spinning top about its tip (approximately), a ceiling fan, the Earth about its axis.

3. Combined Translation and Rotation

The most general motion of a rigid body in a plane is a combination of translation of the centre of mass and rotation about the centre of mass. Every possible planar motion can be decomposed this way.

Example: a ball rolling on the ground, a cylinder rolling down an incline.

Any planar rigid-body motion can also be viewed as pure rotation about an instantaneous axis. This axis — called the instantaneous centre of rotation — may lie outside the body and changes from instant to instant.

Degrees of Freedom

A free particle in 3D has 3 degrees of freedom (coordinates \(x, y, z\)). A rigid body in 3D has 6 degrees of freedom:

  • 3 translational — position of the centre of mass \((x, y, z)\).
  • 3 rotational — orientation of the body (Euler angles or equivalent).

For planar motion (motion confined to a plane), this reduces to 3: two translational (\(x, y\) of COM) and one rotational (\(\theta\)). Adding constraints (like a fixed axis) reduces the count further.

Fixed-Axis vs Free Rotation

  • Fixed-axis rotation: The axis of rotation is fixed in space and does not move. The body's motion is fully described by a single variable \(\theta(t)\). Examples: a door hinged at one edge, a wheel on a fixed axle.
  • Free rotation: The axis of rotation can change direction. This is far more complex and includes phenomena like precession and nutation. For JEE purposes, most problems involve fixed-axis or constrained-axis rotation.
