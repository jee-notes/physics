Toppling

Toppling and Shifting of the Contact Force.

Statement

Toppling is one of the most important applications of rotational equilibrium involving contact forces.

Consider a rigid body resting on a supporting surface and initially in complete translational and rotational equilibrium. The supporting surface exerts a contact force on the body, which can be resolved into two mutually perpendicular components:

  • Normal Reaction, acting perpendicular to the surface.
  • Frictional Force, acting tangentially along the surface.

Unlike a particle, the contact force acting on a rigid body is not restricted to a fixed point. As external forces or torques acting on the body change, the point of application of the resultant contact force shifts over the contact surface so as to maintain rotational equilibrium. Consequently, the points of application of both the normal reaction and friction shift together.

This ability of the contact force to adjust its point of application is another self-adjusting characteristic of contact forces, in addition to the adjustment of their magnitudes.

The phenomenon of toppling is a direct consequence of this behaviour. As long as the resultant contact force can shift over the contact surface, rotational equilibrium can be maintained. Toppling occurs only when no further shift of the contact force is possible.

Unlike a particle, a rigid body is in contact with the supporting surface over a finite region rather than at a single point. This contact imposes the constraint that the body cannot penetrate or rotate into the supporting surface.

Whenever external forces or torques tend to violate this constraint, the distributed contact forces automatically rearrange themselves over the contact region. As a result, the point of application of the resultant contact force shifts over the contact surface, producing the counter-torque required to maintain rotational equilibrium. Consequently, the points of application of both the normal reaction and friction shift together.

This ability of the contact force to adjust its point of application is another self-adjusting characteristic of contact forces, in addition to the adjustment of their magnitudes.

Toppling occurs when the required point of application of the resultant contact force lies outside the available contact region. Beyond this stage, the contact force can no longer satisfy the contact constraint, and the body begins to rotate about the edge of the support.

Model

Consider a rectangular block of base b and height h resting on a horizontal surface. The thickness of the block is irrelevant, just as a disc and a solid cylinder are treated identically when only their cross-section matters. The centre of mass is located at the geometric centre of the block, i.e. at a height h/2 above the ground and at a horizontal distance b/2 from either edge.

Now apply a small horizontal force F at the same height h/2. The force is sufficiently small that the block remains in complete translational and rotational equilibrium.

The horizontal force is balanced by the frictional component of the contact force, while the weight mg is balanced by its normal component. Thus, translational equilibrium is satisfied.

Now suppose that the normal reaction still passes through the centre of mass. Then the forces F, mg, and the normal reaction all pass through the centre of mass and therefore produce zero torque about it. However, the frictional force produces a non-zero torque about the centre of mass.

This would imply a non-zero angular acceleration, contradicting the fact that the block remains in rotational equilibrium. Therefore, our assumption must be incorrect.

The resultant contact force must therefore shift its point of application over the contact surface. Consequently, the line of action of the normal reaction also shifts. Notice that, in this example, the magnitude of the normal reaction remains unchanged because it continues to balance the weight. The required balancing torque is produced not by increasing the normal reaction, but by shifting the point at which the contact force acts.

Vertical Stack

The point of application of the contact force may shift for reasons other than the application of an external horizontal force. A classic example is a vertical stack of identical slabs.

Consider identical slabs, each of length l, stacked one above another. Each successive slab is shifted slightly to the right with respect to the slab immediately below it. As more slabs are added, the centre of mass of the upper part of the stack moves progressively towards the edge of the supporting slab below.

For every slab to remain in rotational equilibrium, the resultant contact force between any two adjacent slabs must pass through the supporting region. This is achieved by continuously shifting the point of application of the contact force. The maximum possible height of the stack is reached when the contact force at every interface acts through the edge of the supporting slab.

Toppling and Rolling

The invention of the wheel may be viewed as a remarkable application of the principle of toppling. Instead of allowing a body to topple through a finite angle about a fixed edge, the wheel continuously changes its point of contact with the ground.

From this viewpoint, rolling may be regarded as an infinite sequence of infinitesimal toppling motions. At every instant, the contact point acts as the instantaneous pivot, and as the wheel rolls, this pivot continuously migrates along the rim. The contact force continuously shifts to the new point of contact, preventing penetration of the wheel into the ground while allowing smooth rotational motion.

Thus, a block advances by repeatedly toppling about successive edges, whereas a wheel advances by continuously changing its edge of support. The wheel can therefore be regarded as the limiting case of toppling, in which the angle of each individual toppling tends to zero while their number tends to infinity.

A wheel eliminates the need for the contact force to migrate within a contact region by continuously creating a new contact point.
