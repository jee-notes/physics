Newton's Second Law for Rotation

\(\tau = I\alpha\) — the equation that governs all fixed-axis rotation dynamics.

The Law

For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis, the net external torque about that axis equals the moment of inertia times the angular acceleration:

\[\tau_{\text{net}} = I\alpha\]

This is the rotational analogue of \(F = ma\). It is derived by applying \(F = ma\) to every particle in the body and summing the torques (internal torques cancel by Newton's third law).

Like \(F = ma\), this equation links cause (torque) to effect (angular acceleration) through an inertia parameter (\(I\)). It is valid only about a fixed axis (or about the centre of mass, even if the CM accelerates).

Applying the Law: Method

The standard approach for fixed-axis dynamics problems:

  • Step 1: Identify the axis of rotation and draw a free-body diagram showing all forces on the body.
  • Step 2: Calculate the moment of inertia \(I\) about the axis.
  • Step 3: Calculate the net torque \(\tau_{\text{net}}\) about the axis (using \(\tau = r_\perp F\) or \(\tau = rF\sin\theta\)).
  • Step 4: Apply \(\tau_{\text{net}} = I\alpha\) to find \(\alpha\).
  • Step 5: If needed, use kinematics (\(\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t\), etc.) to find velocities and displacements.

Example: Pulley with Mass

A pulley of mass \(M\), radius \(R\), treated as a uniform disc (\(I = \frac{1}{2}MR^2\)), has a block of mass \(m\) hanging from a string wrapped around it. For the block:

\[mg - T = ma\]

For the pulley (torque about its axis):

\[TR = I\alpha = \tfrac{1}{2}MR^2 \cdot \frac{a}{R}\] \[T = \tfrac{1}{2}Ma\]

Solving these simultaneously:

\[a = \frac{mg}{m + \frac{M}{2}}\]

The pulley's inertia reduces the acceleration compared to a massless pulley (\(a = g\)).

About the Centre of Mass

A powerful generalisation: \(\tau = I\alpha\) also holds about the centre of mass, even if the CM is accelerating. This is used extensively in combined translation–rotation problems (like rolling):

\[\tau_{\text{CM}} = I_{\text{CM}}\alpha\]

Together with Newton's second law for the COM (\(\vec{F}_{\text{ext}} = M\vec{a}_{\text{CM}}\)), these two equations fully determine the motion of a rigid body in a plane.

Rotational Equilibrium Revisited

If \(\alpha = 0\), then \(\tau_{\text{net}} = 0\). This is the condition for rotational equilibrium. The body either does not rotate or rotates at constant angular velocity.

In static problems (body at rest), both \(\sum \vec{F} = 0\) and \(\sum \vec{\tau} = 0\) must hold simultaneously. Classic applications include:

  • Ladders leaning against walls
  • Balanced beams and seesaws
  • Hanging signs supported by cables and hinges
