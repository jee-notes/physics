Angular Kinematics

Describing rotational motion with angular displacement, velocity, and acceleration — the rotational counterparts of \(s, v, a\).

Angular Variables

For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis, every particle traces a circle. The rotation is described by:

  • Angular displacement \(\theta\) — the angle (in radians) swept by a reference line in the body.
  • Angular velocity \(\omega\) — the rate of change of angular displacement: \[\omega = \frac{d\theta}{dt}\] SI unit: rad/s. Related to frequency: \(\omega = 2\pi f\) and \(\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T}\).
  • Angular acceleration \(\alpha\) — the rate of change of angular velocity: \[\alpha = \frac{d\omega}{dt} = \frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}\] SI unit: rad/s².
All particles of a rigid body share the same \(\omega\) and \(\alpha\), but their linear velocities and accelerations depend on their distance from the axis.

Equations of Rotational Kinematics

For constant angular acceleration, the equations mirror the linear kinematics equations exactly:

\[\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t\] \[\theta = \omega_0 t + \tfrac{1}{2}\alpha t^2\] \[\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha\theta\] \[\theta = \tfrac{1}{2}(\omega_0 + \omega)t\]

These are used in exactly the same way as their linear counterparts. Simply replace \(s \to \theta\), \(u \to \omega_0\), \(v \to \omega\), and \(a \to \alpha\).

Linear–Angular Relations

A particle at distance \(r\) from the axis has:

  • Linear speed: \(v = r\omega\)
  • Tangential acceleration: \(a_t = r\alpha\)
  • Centripetal (radial) acceleration: \(a_c = r\omega^2 = \frac{v^2}{r}\)
  • Net acceleration: \(a = \sqrt{a_t^2 + a_c^2}\)

The direction of \(\vec{v}\) is tangent to the circle; \(\vec{a}_t\) is tangent (in the direction of increasing or decreasing speed); \(\vec{a}_c\) is radially inward.

Angular Velocity as a Vector

Angular velocity is a pseudovector directed along the axis of rotation. Its direction is given by the right-hand rule: curl the fingers of the right hand in the direction of rotation, and the thumb points along \(\vec{\omega}\).

The velocity of any particle is then:

\[\vec{v} = \vec{\omega} \times \vec{r}\]

This cross-product formulation is essential for problems involving three-dimensional geometry and for understanding the vector nature of torque and angular momentum.

Only for fixed-axis rotation is \(\omega\) simply a scalar (positive or negative). In general 3D rotation, the full vector nature of \(\vec{\omega}\) must be used.

Non-Uniform Rotation

When angular acceleration varies with time, the kinematic equations above do not apply. Instead, use calculus:

\[\omega = \int \alpha\,dt + \omega_0\] \[\theta = \int \omega\,dt + \theta_0\]

If \(\alpha\) is given as a function of \(\theta\) (common in dynamics problems), use:

\[\alpha = \omega\frac{d\omega}{d\theta}\]
