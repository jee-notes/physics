Moment of Inertia

The rotational analogue of mass — measuring a body's resistance to angular acceleration.

Definition

The moment of inertia of a system about a given axis is the sum of the products of each particle's mass and the square of its perpendicular distance from the axis:

\[I = \sum m_i r_i^2 \qquad \text{(discrete)}\] \[I = \int r^2\,dm \qquad \text{(continuous)}\]

SI unit: kg·m². Moment of inertia depends on:

  • Total mass of the body
  • Distribution of mass relative to the axis
  • Choice of axis
Mass resists changes in linear velocity; moment of inertia resists changes in angular velocity. A body with the same mass can have very different moments of inertia depending on how its mass is distributed.

Radius of Gyration

The radius of gyration \(k\) is the distance from the axis at which the entire mass of the body could be concentrated to give the same moment of inertia:

\[I = Mk^2 \implies k = \sqrt{\frac{I}{M}}\]

It provides a single length that characterises how "spread out" the mass is about the axis.

Standard Results

Common Bodies (about natural symmetry axis)

  • Thin ring / hollow cylinder (about central axis): \(I = MR^2\)
  • Solid disc / solid cylinder (about central axis): \(I = \frac{1}{2}MR^2\)
  • Solid sphere (about any diameter): \(I = \frac{2}{5}MR^2\)
  • Hollow sphere (about any diameter): \(I = \frac{2}{3}MR^2\)
  • Thin rod (about centre, perpendicular): \(I = \frac{1}{12}ML^2\)
  • Thin rod (about one end, perpendicular): \(I = \frac{1}{3}ML^2\)
  • Rectangular plate (about centre, perp. to plane): \(I = \frac{M(a^2 + b^2)}{12}\)
These results must be memorised. Every rolling and rotation problem starts by identifying the moment of inertia, and incorrect values will cascade through the entire solution.

Parallel Axis Theorem

If the moment of inertia about an axis through the centre of mass is \(I_{\text{CM}}\), then the moment of inertia about any parallel axis a distance \(d\) away is:

\[I = I_{\text{CM}} + Md^2\]

The parallel axis theorem always increases the moment of inertia — the CM axis gives the minimum value among all parallel axes.

Perpendicular Axis Theorem

For a planar (2D) body lying in the \(xy\)-plane, the moment of inertia about the \(z\)-axis (perpendicular to the plane) equals the sum of the moments about the \(x\)- and \(y\)-axes:

\[I_z = I_x + I_y\]

This theorem applies only to flat laminae — not to three-dimensional bodies like spheres or cylinders. x-axis, y-axis and z-axis are mutually perpendicular.

The x-axis and y-axis should lie in the plane of the 2D body and z-axis should be perpendicular to the plane of the body. This is because \(dI_x = dm y^2\) and \(dI_y = dm x^2\) and \(dI_z = dm (x^2 + y^2)\).

The z-axis is taken from the point of intersection of x-axis and y-axis. It is not necessary for this point to be within the boundary of the body.

Example: For a uniform ring, \(I_x = I_y\) by symmetry, and \(I_z = MR^2\). Therefore \(I_x = I_y = \frac{1}{2}MR^2\) — the moment about any diameter.

Composite Bodies

For a body made of multiple parts, the total moment of inertia is the sum of the moments of the individual parts (about the same axis):

\[I_{\text{total}} = I_1 + I_2 + I_3 + \cdots\]

For a body with a cavity, subtract the moment of the removed part:

\[I_{\text{with cavity}} = I_{\text{full}} - I_{\text{removed}}\]
