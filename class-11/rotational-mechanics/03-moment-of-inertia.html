Moment of Inertia

The rotational analogue of mass — measuring a body's resistance to angular acceleration.

Definition

The moment of inertia of a system about a given axis is the sum of the products of each particle's mass and the square of its perpendicular distance from the axis:

\[ I = \sum m_i r_i^2 \qquad \text{(discrete)} \] \[ I = \int r^2\,dm \qquad \text{(continuous)} \]

SI unit: \(kg \cdot m^2\). Moment of inertia depends on:

  • Total mass of the body
  • Distribution of mass relative to the axis
  • Choice of axis
Mass resists changes in linear velocity; moment of inertia resists changes in angular velocity.
A body with the same mass can have very different moments of inertia depending on how its mass is distributed.

Radius of Gyration

The radius of gyration \(k\) is the distance from the axis at which the entire mass of the body could be concentrated to give the same moment of inertia:

\[I = Mk^2 \implies k = \sqrt{\frac{I}{M}}\]

It provides a single length that characterises how "spread out" the mass is about the axis.

Standard Results

Common Bodies (about natural symmetry axis)

  • Thin ring / hollow cylinder (about central axis): \(I = MR^2\)
  • Solid disc / solid cylinder (about central axis): \(I = \frac{1}{2}MR^2\)
  • Solid sphere (about any diameter): \(I = \frac{2}{5}MR^2\)
  • Hollow sphere (about any diameter): \(I = \frac{2}{3}MR^2\)
  • Thin rod (about centre, perpendicular): \(I = \frac{1}{12}ML^2\)
  • Thin rod (about one end, perpendicular): \(I = \frac{1}{3}ML^2\)
  • Rectangular plate (about centre, perp. to plane): \(I = \frac{M(a^2 + b^2)}{12}\)
These results must be memorised. Every rolling and rotation problem starts by identifying the moment of inertia, and incorrect values will cascade through the entire solution.

Point Mass about Coordinate Axes

Consider a point mass \(m\) located at the point \((x,y,z)\). Determine its moment of inertia about the coordinate axes.

The moment of inertia of a point mass about an axis is given by \[ I=mr_\perp^2, \] where \(r_\perp\) is the perpendicular distance of the point from the axis.

The Cartesian coordinates themselves provide these perpendicular distances. For example, let \(P(x,y,z)\) be projected perpendicularly onto the \(x\)-axis. By the definition of Cartesian coordinates, the foot of the perpendicular is \(A(x,0,0)\). Therefore, \[ PA=\sqrt{y^2+z^2}, \] giving \[ I_x=m(y^2+z^2). \] Similarly, \[ I_y=m(x^2+z^2), \] and \[ I_z=m(x^2+y^2). \]

This result serves as a mathematical tool for proving both the Parallel Axis Theorem and the Perpendicular Axis Theorem. Although a general formula exists for the perpendicular distance of a point from a line in three-dimensional geometry, it is considerably more complicated and lies outside the JEE syllabus. By choosing the axes of rotation as coordinate axes, the required perpendicular distances follow directly from the Cartesian coordinates of each mass element.

Perpendicular Axis Theorem

For a planar (2D) body lying in the \(xy\)-plane, the moment of inertia about the \(z\)-axis (perpendicular to the plane) equals the sum of the moments of inertia about the mutually perpendicular \(x\)- and \(y\)-axes:

\[ I_z=I_x+I_y \]

The theorem applies only to planar bodies (laminae). The \(x\)- and \(y\)-axes must lie in the plane of the body, while the \(z\)-axis must be perpendicular to the plane. These three axes intersect at a common point, which need not lie inside the body or coincide with its centre of mass.

Using the results obtained for a point mass about the coordinate axes, every mass element \(dm\) of the lamina has \(z=0\). Hence, \[ dI_x=dmy^2, \] \[ dI_y=dmx^2, \] and \[ dI_z=dm(x^2+y^2). \] Therefore, \[ dI_z=dI_x+dI_y. \] Integrating over the entire body gives \[ I_z=I_x+I_y. \]

Example: For a uniform ring, \[ I_x=I_y \] by symmetry, and \[ I_z=MR^2. \] Hence, \[ I_x=I_y=\frac12MR^2, \] which is the moment of inertia about any diameter of the ring.

Parallel Axis Theorem

If the moment of inertia of a rigid body about an axis passing through its centre of mass is \(I_{\rm CM}\), then the moment of inertia about any parallel axis at a perpendicular distance \(d\) is

\[ I=I_{\rm CM}+Md^2 \]

The theorem always increases the moment of inertia. Consequently, among all mutually parallel axes, the axis passing through the centre of mass has the minimum moment of inertia.

The theorem can be proved elegantly by choosing the coordinate axes appropriately. Consider an arbitrary rigid body. Let its centre of mass be at the origin \((0,0,0)\). Choose the \(y\)-axis to coincide with the axis passing through the centre of mass so that \[ I_{\rm CM}=I_y. \] Let the required parallel axis \(I_g\) also be parallel to the \(y\)-axis and pass through the point \((d,0,0)\). Thus, the \(x\)-axis lies along the common perpendicular between the two axes.

Now consider an arbitrary mass element \(dm\) located at \((x,y,z)\). Using the result for a point mass about a coordinate axis, \[ dI_{\rm CM}=dm(x^2+z^2) \] whereas its moment of inertia about the parallel axis is \[ dI_g=dm\left[(x-d)^2+z^2\right] \] Expanding, \[ dI_g=dI_{\rm CM}-2d\,x\,dm+d^2\,dm \] Integrating over the entire body, \[ I_g = I_{\rm CM} -2d\int x\,dm +d^2\int dm \] Since the origin is the centre of mass, \[ \int x\,dm=0 \] and \[ \int dm=M \] Hence, \[ I_g=I_{\rm CM}+Md^2 \]

Composite Bodies

For a body made of multiple parts, the total moment of inertia is the sum of the moments of the individual parts (about the same axis):

\[I_{\text{total}} = I_1 + I_2 + I_3 + \cdots\]

For a body with a cavity, subtract the moment of the removed part:

\[I_{\text{with cavity}} = I_{\text{full}} - I_{\text{removed}}\]

Slanting Axis of a Rod

The rod is the simplest one-dimensional rigid body and is therefore an excellent model for understanding the geometry of moment of inertia. Many common misconceptions become apparent when studying a rod about a slanting axis.

If a uniform rod of length \(L\) makes an angle \(\theta\) with an axis of rotation passing through one of its ends, then its moment of inertia about the axis is

\[ I=\frac13ML^2\sin^2\theta. \]

A common misconception is that the rod's length is being resolved into components \(L\sin\theta\) and \(L\cos\theta\), similar to vector resolution. This is incorrect. The factor \(\sin\theta\) appears because the perpendicular distance of each mass element from the axis changes with the inclination of the rod. The length of the rod itself is never resolved.

Another common doubt arises during the derivation. Should the differential mass element \(dm\) be taken along the length of the rod or along the perpendicular distance from the axis? Since mass is distributed along the rod,

\[ dm=\lambda\,dl, \]

where \(dl\) is an element of the rod's length. The perpendicular distance is used only while evaluating

\[ dI=r_\perp^2\,dm. \]

Notice that \(dm\) is always associated with an element of the rod's length through \[ dm=\lambda\,dl. \] If we instead choose a coordinate along the perpendicular radius, then the mass element is no longer distributed along that coordinate. The element \(dm\) lies at an angle to the chosen coordinate, so \(dm=\lambda\,dx\) is no longer valid.

A third misconception occurs when the rod lies in the plane of the paper while the axis of rotation is perpendicular to the plane. Tilting the rod within the plane changes its orientation but does not change the angle between the rod and the axis, which remains \(90^\circ\). Consequently, the moment of inertia remains unchanged.

A fourth misconception concerns the Parallel Axis Theorem. The perpendicular distance \(d\) between two parallel axes is always the shortest distance between them. For a slanting rod,

\[ d=\frac{L}{2}\sin\theta, \]

not \(\frac{L}{2}\). Therefore,

\[ I_{\rm CM} = I_{\rm end} - M\left(\frac{L\sin\theta}{2}\right)^2 = \frac13ML^2\sin^2\theta - \frac14ML^2\sin^2\theta = \frac1{12}ML^2\sin^2\theta. \]

Radius of Gyration for Discrete Systems

Since most standard moment of inertia formulae are for uniform continuous bodies, students often use the shortcut:

  • Replace the total mass \(M\) by 1.
  • Take the square root of the remaining expression.

For example, for a uniform rod, \[ I=\frac{ML^2}{12} \] Putting \(M=1\) gives \[ k=\sqrt{\frac{L^2}{12}}=\frac{L}{\sqrt{12}} \] This shortcut works because the formula is already expressed in the form \[ I=Mk^2 \]

However, this shortcut becomes a trap for discrete systems (and even for continuous bodies with non-uniform mass distributions). In such cases, the coefficient of the expression for \(I\) is not necessarily the total mass.

Consider three point masses:

  • \(m_1\) at \((0,0)\)
  • \(m_2\) at \((2,0)\)
  • \(m_3\) at \((-2,0)\)

The moment of inertia about the \(z\)-axis is

\[ I_z = m_1(0)^2 + m_2(2)^2 + m_3(2)^2 = 4(m_2+m_3) \]

If we incorrectly set \[ m_2+m_3=1 \] we obtain \[ k=2 \] which is generally incorrect because the radius of gyration is defined using the total mass of the system.

The correct relation is

\[ (m_1+m_2+m_3)k^2 = 4(m_2+m_3) \]

which gives

\[ k = \sqrt{\frac{4(m_2+m_3)} {m_1+m_2+m_3}} \]
The shortcut "put \(M=1\) and take the square root" works only when the moment of inertia formula is already written in the form \[ I=Mk^2 \] Always identify the total mass of the system before calculating the radius of gyration.
