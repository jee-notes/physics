Trajectory

Trajectory Path and its Equation.

What is Trajectory

The trajectory of a moving particle is the path traced by the particle during its motion.

For example, if a ball is thrown vertically upward, the particle moves along a straight line: it rises, reaches a maximum height, and then comes back down along the same line. Therefore, the trajectory of this motion is a straight line.

In 2D projectile motion, the particle is projected at an angle and its trajectory is a curved path. Under the ideal projectile assumptions of constant gravitational acceleration and negligible air resistance, this path is a parabola.

We can derive this parabola algebraically by finding a direct relation between the horizontal coordinate \(x\) and the vertical coordinate \(y\).

Equation of Trajectory

From the previous analysis of projectile motion, \(x\) and \(y\) are both functions of the same time parameter \(t\):

\[ x=u\cos\theta\,t \] \[ y=u\sin\theta\,t-\frac12gt^2 \]

What are \(x\) and \(y\) here? They are the co-ordinates of the particle in \(XY\) plane at different values of \(t\) when the launch-point is taken as origin.

The trajectory is the relation between \(x\) and \(y\) that describes the path independently of time. Therefore, we eliminate the common parameter \(t\).

From the horizontal equation,

\[ t=\frac{x}{u\cos\theta} \qquad t = \frac{x}{u_x} \]

Substituting this into the vertical equation,

\[ y=(u\sin\theta)\left(\frac{x}{u\cos\theta}\right)-\frac12g\left(\frac{x}{u\cos\theta}\right)^2 \]

and simplifying gives The Equation of Trajectory

\[ y=x\tan\theta-\frac{gx^2}{2u^2\cos^2\theta} \qquad y=x\tan\theta-\frac{g}{2}\frac{x^2}{u_x^2} \]

This has the form

\[ y=Ax-Bx^2 \]

where \(A\) and \(B\) are constants for a given projectile. Therefore, the trajectory is a parabola. Note that \(u\) and \(\theta\) are both constants. They are initial velocity and initial angle.

Other Forms of the Equation

Consider the form:

\[ y=x\tan\theta-\frac{g}{2}\frac{x^2}{u_x^2} \]

Put \(u_x = u_y / \tan \theta \) ,

\[ y=x\tan\theta-\frac{g}{2}\frac{x^2\tan\theta}{u_xu_y} = x\tan\theta\left( 1 - \frac{g}{2u_xu_y}x\right) \]

We get,

\[ y = \tan\theta \, x \left( 1 - \frac{x}{R}\right) \]

When \(x = R/2\) , \(y = H\)

\[ H = \tan\theta \, \frac{R}{2} \left( 1 - \frac{1}{2}\right) \qquad H = \tan\theta \, \frac{R}{4} \qquad R \tan \theta = 4H \]

Solved Examples

Question

Consider a projectile whose equation of trajectory is \(2y=4x-x^2\) . Find

  • the maximum height \(H_{\max}\),
  • the horizontal range \(R\),
  • the angle \(\alpha\) made by the instantaneous velocity with the horizontal at \(x=1\,\mathrm m\).

Solution

Given that

\[ 2y=4x-x^2 \]

To compare with the useful form, first we make the co-efficient of \(y\) as 1. \[ y=2x-\frac{x^2}{2} \] Now, we compare this with the useful form \(y=x\tan\theta\left(1-\frac{x}{R}\right)\) . Writing the given equation in the same form,

\[ y=2x\left(1-\frac{x}{4}\right) \]

we can immediately identify

\[ \boxed{\tan\theta=2\qquad R=4\,\mathrm m} \]

Maximum Height

The trajectory is symmetric about its highest point, which occurs halfway between the two points where it meets the ground. Since the range is \(4\,\mathrm m\),

\[ x=\frac R2=2\,\mathrm m \]

Therefore,

\[ H=y(2)=2(2)-\frac{2^2}{2}=2\,\mathrm m \] \[ \boxed{H=2\,\mathrm m} \]

This also agrees with the relation \(R\tan\theta=4H_\text{max}\)

\[ H_\text{max} = \frac{R \tan \theta}{4} = \frac{4(2)}{4} \]

Direction of Velocity at \(x=1\,\mathrm m\)

The instantaneous velocity is tangent to the trajectory. Therefore, its angle \(\alpha\) with the horizontal is determined by the slope of the trajectory:

\[ \tan\alpha=\frac{dy}{dx} \] \[ \frac{dy}{dx}=2-x \]

At \(x=1\,\mathrm m\),

\[ \tan\alpha=2-1=1 \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{\alpha=45^\circ} \]
The trajectory equation itself contains a large amount of information. From \[ y=x\tan\theta\left(1-\frac{x}{R}\right) \] we can extract the range \(R\) and launch angle \(\theta\).
The maximum height follows from the point \(x=R/2\), while the instantaneous direction of velocity follows from the slope \[ \tan\alpha=\frac{dy}{dx} \]
