Relative Motion in 2D

Extending the concept of moving frames of reference into two distinct dimensions.

Concept of Relative Velocity in a Plane

Velocity is not absolute; it is fundamentally determined by the observer's frame of reference. When we say a car travels at 60 km/h, we implicitly mean 60 km/h relative to the stationary ground.

However, if an observer is themselves moving, what they measure as the velocity of another object will systematically differ. In 1D, we simply add or subtract scalars depending on whether objects are approaching or receding. In 2D, the physical shift requires strict geometrical Vector subtraction.

General Vector Formulation

Let \(\vec{r}_A\) be the position vector of object A relative to the ground (origin O). Let \(\vec{r}_B\) be the position vector of object B relative to that same ground.

The position of A as seen entirely from the perspective of B is the relative position vector: \[ \vec{r}_{AB} = \vec{r}_A - \vec{r}_B \]

Relative Velocity Vector:

Taking the time derivative of the relative position yields the fundamental equation of relative velocity: \[ \frac{d\vec{r}_{AB}}{dt} = \frac{d\vec{r}_A}{dt} - \frac{d\vec{r}_B}{dt} \] \[ \vec{v}_{AB} = \vec{v}_A - \vec{v}_B \]

To physically construct \(\vec{v}_{AB}\), you systematically take the velocity vector of A, physically reverse the mathematical direction of B's velocity vector (creating \(-\vec{v}_B\)), and perform tail-to-head geometric vector addition.
