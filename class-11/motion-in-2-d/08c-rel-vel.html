Relative position and Relative velocity

If position of particle A wrt ground is \(\vec{r}_A\) and position of particle B wrt ground is \(\vec{r}_B\), then relative position of B wrt A is:

\[ \vec{r}_{B/A} = \vec{r}_B - \vec{r}_A \]

This vector \(\vec{r}_{B/A}\) represents the position of particle B as seen from particle A. It directly connects A to B and therefore fully describes their geometric separation at any instant.

If velocity of particle A wrt ground is \(\vec{v}_A\) and velocity of particle B wrt ground is \(\vec{v}_B\), then relative velocity of B wrt A is:

\[ \vec{v}_{B/A} = \vec{v}_B - \vec{v}_A \]

This gives the rate of change of the relative position vector \(\vec{r}_{B/A}\). In other words, \(\vec{v}_{B/A}\) tells us how the separation between the two particles is evolving with time.

Mathematically, \(\vec{v}_{B/A}\) appears as a combination of \(\vec{v}_B\) and \(-\vec{v}_A\), both defined with respect to the ground frame. This decomposition is algebraically correct and often useful for calculation.

However, this form does not directly reveal the nature of the relative motion between the particles.

In most physical situations, the important questions are:

  • Is the distance between the particles increasing or decreasing?
  • Is the line joining the particles rotating?

These questions are not naturally answered by resolving \(\vec{v}_{B/A}\) into horizontal and vertical components or into \(\vec{v}_B\) and \(-\vec{v}_A\).

Instead, we must analyze \(\vec{v}_{B/A}\) with respect to \(\vec{r}_{B/A}\) itself, since \(\vec{r}_{B/A}\) defines the instantaneous configuration of the system.

Key Idea: To understand relative motion, resolve relative velocity along and perpendicular to the relative position vector.

This leads to a more physically meaningful decomposition of motion.

Radial and Transverse Components of Relative Velocity

At any instant, the relative position vector \(\vec{r}\) connects the two particles. This vector carries two independent pieces of information:

  • Magnitude → the distance between the particles
  • Direction → the orientation of the line joining them

As the particles move, both of these quantities may change with time.

The relative velocity \(\vec{v}_{rel}\), being the time derivative of \(\vec{r}\), must therefore account for both types of change:

  • Change in magnitude of \(\vec{r}\)
  • Change in direction of \(\vec{r}\)

This naturally leads to the decomposition of \(\vec{v}_{rel}\) into two independent components.

Fundamental Decomposition:

\[ \vec{v}_{rel} = \underbrace{\frac{dr}{dt}\hat{r}}_{\text{radial component}} + \underbrace{r\omega \hat{\theta}}_{\text{transverse component}} \]

Radial Component (Distance Changing)

The radial component lies along the direction of \(\vec{r}\) (i.e., along \(\hat{r}\)). It represents the rate at which the magnitude of \(\vec{r}\) changes.

Physically, this component tells us whether the particles are moving closer together or farther apart.

  • Magnitude: \( \frac{dr}{dt} \)
  • Direction: along \(\hat{r}\)
  • Effect: changes distance between particles

\[ v_r = \vec{v}_{rel} \cdot \hat{r} \]

  • \(v_r \lt 0\) → distance decreases (approach)
  • \(v_r > 0\) → distance increases (separation)

Thus, the radial component completely governs how the separation between the particles evolves.

Transverse Component (Orientation Changing)

The transverse component lies perpendicular to \(\vec{r}\) (along \(\hat{\theta}\)). It represents the rate at which the direction of \(\vec{r}\) changes.

This component does not contribute to any change in distance, but instead causes the line joining the particles to rotate.

  • Magnitude: \( r\omega \)
  • Direction: perpendicular to \(\hat{r}\)
  • Effect: changes orientation of the line joining particles

\[ v_t = r\omega \quad \Rightarrow \quad \omega = \frac{v_t}{r} \]

  • Does not change distance
  • Represents angular motion of one particle relative to the other

Thus, the transverse component completely governs how the direction of the relative position vector evolves.

Combined Interpretation

Any relative motion in two dimensions can always be understood as a combination of two independent effects:

  • Change in distance (radial motion)
  • Change in direction (transverse motion)

Relative motion in 2D = distance change + orientation change

This decomposition provides a complete and physically meaningful description of relative motion and serves as a primary tool in solving problems involving closest approach, collision, and angular motion.

The cat and mouse chase problem and particles on regular polygon chase problem are associated with this theory.
