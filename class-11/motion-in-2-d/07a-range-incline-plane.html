Range Formula

Different Range Formula for Inclined Plane Projectile

Range Formula

The direct method of finding the range along an inclined plane using the inclined resolution is long and tedious and hence prone to manual error.

We can obtain a simpler expression by using the fact that, in the usual horizontal-vertical resolution, the horizontal velocity remains constant because gravity has no horizontal component.

Consider a projectile projected up the incline. The incline make an angle \(\alpha\) with the horizontal, and the projectile be launched with velocity \(u\) and at an angle \(\theta\) with respect to the incline.

If \(R\) is the range measured along the incline, then its horizontal projection is

\[ R\cos\alpha \]

Since gravity has no horizontal component, the horizontal velocity remains constant. The projectile is launched at an angle \((\theta+\alpha)\) with the horizontal, so its horizontal velocity is

\[ u_x=u\cos(\theta+\alpha) \]

Therefore, the horizontal displacement during the time of flight \(T\) is simply

\[ R\cos\alpha=u\cos(\theta+\alpha)\,T \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ R=\frac{u\cos(\theta+\alpha)}{\cos\alpha}\,T} \]

From the inclined resolution, the time of flight is already known:

\[ T=\frac{2u\sin\theta}{g\cos\alpha} \]

Substituting this gives

\[ R=\frac{u\cos(\theta+\alpha)}{\cos\alpha}\left(\frac{2u\sin\theta}{g\cos\alpha}\right) \]

so the range up the incline is

\[ \boxed{R=\frac{2u^2\sin\theta\cos(\theta+\alpha)}{g\cos^2\alpha}} \]
We use two different resolutions for two different purposes.
The direction perpendicular to the incline is used to determine the time of flight, because the projectile starts and ends on the incline.
The horizontal direction is then used to determine the range, because horizontal velocity remains constant under gravity.

Sign of \(\alpha\)

The difference between projection up the incline and projection down the incline can be incorporated simply by assigning a sign to \(\alpha\).

For projection up the incline, we take \(\alpha\) as positive. The angle of the initial velocity with the horizontal is then

\[ \theta+\alpha \]

so the horizontal component of the initial velocity is

\[ u_x=u\cos(\theta+\alpha) \]

For projection down the incline, we can equivalently take

\[ \alpha\rightarrow-\alpha \]

The angle of the initial velocity with the horizontal then becomes

\[ \theta-\alpha \]

giving

\[ u_x=u\cos(\theta-\alpha) \]

Thus, instead of writing separate formulas for the two cases, we can use the same range formula and simply change the sign of \(\alpha\). For the general expression,

\[ R=\frac{u\cos(\theta+\alpha)}{\cos\alpha}\left(\frac{2u\sin\theta}{g\cos\alpha}\right) \]

putting \(\alpha\rightarrow-\alpha\) gives the down-the-incline case:

\[ R=\frac{u\cos(\theta+(-\alpha))}{\cos(-\alpha)}\left(\frac{2u\sin\theta}{g\cos(-\alpha)}\right) \]

Since

\[ \cos(-\alpha)=\cos\alpha \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{R=\frac{2u^2\sin\theta\cos(\theta-\alpha)}{g\cos^2\alpha}} \]
The up-the-incline and down-the-incline cases are not fundamentally different formulas.
They can be represented by the same formula with the sign of \(\alpha\) chosen according to the orientation of the incline.

Maximum Range : \(R_{\text{max}}\)

When we talk about maximum range \(R_{\text{max}}\) we are talking about maintaining the same speed \(u\) and changing \(\theta\) .

The common brute force method is to write range \(R\) as a function of \(\theta\) and differentiate. But, we can make use of trigonometry directly.

We first use:

\[ 2 \, \sin A \,\cos B = \sin (A+B) + \sin (A-B) \]

For projection up the incline, the range is

\[ R=\frac{2u^2\sin\theta\cos(\theta+\alpha)}{g\cos^2\alpha} \]

Using

\[ 2\sin A\cos B=\sin(A+B)+\sin(A-B) \]

with \[ A=\theta,\qquad B=\theta+\alpha \] we get

\[ 2\sin\theta\cos(\theta+\alpha)=\sin(2\theta+\alpha)-\sin\alpha \]

Therefore,

\[ R=\frac{u^2}{g\cos^2\alpha}\left[\sin(2\theta+\alpha)-\sin\alpha\right] \]

Here \(u\) and \(\alpha\) are fixed, so the only quantity that can vary is \(\theta\). The term \(-\sin\alpha\) is therefore constant. Hence, the range is maximum when

\[ \sin(2\theta+\alpha)=1 \]

Therefore,

\[ 2\theta+\alpha=90^\circ \] \[ \boxed{\theta_{\max}=\frac{90^\circ-\alpha}{2}} \]

At this angle,

\[ \boxed{R_{\max}=\frac{u^2}{g\cos^2\alpha}\left(1-\sin\alpha\right)} \]

Using \[ 1-\sin\alpha=\frac{\cos^2\alpha}{1+\sin\alpha} \] this can also be written as

\[ \boxed{R_{\max}=\frac{u^2}{g(1+\sin\alpha)}} \]

Down the Incline

For projection down the incline, the signed-angle form gives

\[ R=\frac{2u^2\sin\theta\cos(\theta-\alpha)}{g\cos^2\alpha} \]

Using the same identity, with \[ A=\theta,\qquad B=\theta-\alpha \] we get

\[ 2\sin\theta\cos(\theta-\alpha)=\sin(2\theta-\alpha)+\sin\alpha \]

Hence,

\[ R=\frac{u^2}{g\cos^2\alpha}\left[\sin(2\theta-\alpha)+\sin\alpha\right] \]

Again, \(u\) and \(\alpha\) are fixed, so the range is maximum when the sine term is \(1\):

\[ \sin(2\theta-\alpha)=1 \]

Therefore,

\[ 2\theta-\alpha=90^\circ \] \[ \boxed{\theta_{\max}=\frac{90^\circ+\alpha}{2}} \]

The corresponding maximum range is

\[ \boxed{R_{\max}=\frac{u^2}{g\cos^2\alpha}\left(1+\sin\alpha\right)} \] or, using \[ 1+\sin\alpha=\frac{\cos^2\alpha}{1-\sin\alpha} \] \[ \boxed{R_{\max}=\frac{u^2}{g(1-\sin\alpha)}} \]
Once the range is written as a sine of a linear function of \(\theta\) plus a constant, the maximum is obtained immediately by setting that sine term equal to \(1\).
For projection up the incline: \[ \boxed{\theta_{\max}=\frac{90^\circ-\alpha}{2}} \] and for projection down the incline: \[ \boxed{\theta_{\max}=\frac{90^\circ+\alpha}{2}} \] The maximum range is greater for projection down the incline because the component of gravitational acceleration along the incline, \(g\sin\alpha\), acts in the same direction as the initial velocity along the incline.
