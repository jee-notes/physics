River-Boat Problems

Navigating cross-currents using vector velocity addition.

Setup

Consider a patch of still water of width \(L\) and a motorboat capable of speed \(v_b\) attempts to cross the patch. It will head straight towards the opposite side and reach the other saide in time \(L/v_b\) .

Now the same motorboat is in a river of uniform width \(L\) flowing horizontally with a constant velocity \(\vec{v}_r\). The motorboat attempts to cross it by heading straight towards the opposite side.

In this case, the motorboat is headed towards the opposite side. Velocity of boat relative to river \(=\vec{v}_{br} = v_{\text{br}}\hat j\) . This is also the headed velocity of the boat.

However, the boat will not reach the opposite side at the point exactly opposite to the starting point. This is because the river is dragging the boat horizontally.

If we want to determine the location of boat on ground, we need velocity of boat in the ground frame \(=\vec{v}_{b}\) .

Following our fundamental relative velocity equation \(\vec{v}_{br} = \vec{v}_b - \vec{v}_r\), resolving for the actual ground velocity yields a vector addition: \[ \vec{v}_b = \vec{v}_{br} + \vec{v}_r \]

The boat structurally possesses entirely autonomous physical control over its orientation relative to the water (\(\vec{v}_{br}\)), but it has absolutely zero control over the unyielding downstream flow of the river (\(\vec{v}_r\)). The resultant path is their geometric sum.

For building a generic formula structure, we take velocity of river along X axis \(=\vec{v}_{r} = v_{\text{r}}\hat i\) . And we take the width of the river \(=\vec{s}_{y} = L\hat j\) .

Time and Drift

We need to determine the time taken by the boat to cross the river \(T\). This is same as saying that the displacement along Y axis in the ground frame is given as \(L\) - the width of the river.

Thus, we need the Y component of the velocity of boat in ground frame.

Let:

\[ \vec{v}_{br} = v_{br/x}\hat i + v_{br/y}\hat j \]

Going by

\[ \vec{v}_b = \vec{v}_{br} + \vec{v}_r = (v_{br/x} + v_r)\hat i + v_{br/y}\hat j \]

We get,

\[ v_{b/x} = (v_{br/x} + v_r) \qquad v_{b/y} = v_{br/y} \]

For time to cross the river \(T\) , we evaluate motion in Y axis:

\[ T = \frac{s_y}{v_y} = \frac{L}{v_{b/y}} = \frac{L}{v_{br/y}} \]

Drift \(D\) is give by the horizontal distance from the starting point.

\[ D = v_{b/x}T = v_{b/x}\frac{L}{v_{b/y}} = (v_{br/x} + v_r)\frac{L}{v_{br/y}} \]

The cause of the drift is not relevant. If line joining the starting point and ending point is perpendicular, drift = 0. Drift can be downstream (along the river flow) or upstream (opposite to the river flow).

Time and Drift are the 2 key parameters here because Time depends only on the Y component and Drift depends on Time as well as X component.
This is similar to Time of flight and Range.

Drift related to path

Drift parameter is also related to the length of the path travelled by the boat in the river.

This is because, the displacement of the boat will be unidirectional and so we can equate distance and displacement directly.

The width of the river (\(L\)) is the vertical displacement and the drift (\(D\)) is the horizontal displacement. Total displacement (= distance) is \(\sqrt{L^2 + D^2}\)

Optimization Cases

The boatman cannot control river velocity \(\vec{v}_r\) and speed of his boat in still water \(|\vec{v}_{br}|\) .

He can only control the heading angle. Based on heading angle, we solve few optimization cases.

Crossing the River in Shortest Time

The boat needs to traverse a vertical displacement distance \(L\) -i.e. the width of the river.

As already established, horizontal flow cannot physically influence the vertical state.

Therefore, to cross in the absolute shortest time \(T_{min}\), the boat must maximize its vertical velocity component. This strictly means pointing the nose of the boat exactly structurally perpendicular straight across the river flow.

  • Set the heading angle perpendicular to the current (river velocity).
  • The vertical velocity is fully effectively \(v_{br}\) .
  • \(T_{min} = \frac{L}{v_{br}}\)

Thus, for shortest time:

\[ v_{br/x} = 0 \qquad \vec{v}_{br} = v_{br}\hat j \qquad \vec{v}_b = \vec{v}_{br} + \vec{v}_r = v_r \,\hat i + v_{br/y}\,\hat j \]

However, because it did not counteract the river, upon reaching the opposite bank, the boat will have drifted downstream by a distance \(D\): \[ D = v_r \times T_{min} = v_r \times\frac{L}{v_{br}} \]

Crossing the River along Shortest Path (Zero Drift)

Shortest path is the path that boat needs to take to reach the other side. This is along the perpendicular distance and so, it is also the path with zero drift (\(D=0\)).

If the boat intends to land exactly opposite to its starting point, its resultant velocity \(\vec{v}_b\) in the ground frame, must point perpendicularly straight across without any horizontal component.

Thus, the boat must make a heading angle \(\theta\) . This angle \(\theta\) is measured with the vertical as opposed to the usual convention to avoid angles \(\gt 90^\circ\) .

  • Set the heading angle \(\theta\) such that the river velocity is nullified by the boat speed.
  • The net velocity of the boat in ground frame is fully along the vertical \(v_{b/x} = 0\) .
  • \(T = \frac{L}{v_{br}\cos\theta}\)

Thus, for shortest path:

\[ \vec{v}_{br} = -v_{br}\sin\theta \,\hat i + v_{br}\cos\theta \,\hat j \] \[ \vec{v}_{b} = (v_{r} - v_{br}\sin\theta) \,\hat i + v_{br}\cos\theta \,\hat j \qquad \vec{v}_b = \vec{v}_{br} + \vec{v}_r \] \[ v_{b/x} = 0 \qquad (v_{r} - v_{br}\sin\theta) = 0 \] \[ \boxed{\sin\theta = \frac{v_{r}}{v_{br}}} \]

Few critical points:

  • \(\sin \theta \lt 1\) and so \(\frac{v_{r}}{v_{br}} \lt 1\) or \(v_{r} \lt v_{br} \) . This is possible only if the boat is faster than the river (\(v_{br} \gt v_r\)).
  • We know \(\sin \theta =\frac{v_{r}}{v_{br}}\) . So, \(v_{br}\cos \theta = \sqrt{v_{br}^2 - v_{r}^2}\) . We can find the time taken to reach the other side.
  • For shortest path: \(T = \frac{L}{v_{br}\cos\theta}\) . For shortest time: \(T = \frac{L}{v_{br}}\) . \(\cos \theta \lt 1\)
The entire river-boat problem framework is essentially about how the sum of two vectors depends on the angle between them.
The magnitudes \(|\vec v_r|\) and \(|\vec v_{br}|\) are fixed by the physical setup.
The boatman can control only the heading angle of \(\vec v_{br}\), and therefore controls the direction and components of the resultant ground velocity \(\vec v_b\).

Minimum Drift Case

Depending on whether \(v_{br}\gt v_r\) or \(v_{br}\le v_r\) , there are 2 cases related to drift.

We already studied for \(v_{br}\gt v_r\) , the shortest path (zero drift) is attained when \(\sin\theta = \frac{v_{r}}{v_{br}}\) where \(\theta\) is the heading angle measured from the perpendicular.

At this angle, the boat completely cancels the river velocity.

If the boat tilts more horizontally, then it will create non-zero drift in the opposite direction.

But when \(v_{br}\le v_r\) , the boat cannot cancel the river velocity completely and cross the river as well.

For \(v_{br}\le v_r\), there is no heading that can both completely cancel the river velocity and retain a non-zero velocity component across the river.

As zero drift is impossible and so we find the heading angle that gives the minimum possible drift.

This is the key realisation. Minimum drift is not achieved by keeping \(\theta \to 90^\circ\) as much as possible. This is because the time period will exceedingly increase and the boat would have drifted more than the minimum drift possible.

To find the angle of minimum drift, we write the drift as a function of the heading angle:

\[ D=\frac{L(v_r-v_{br}\sin\theta)}{v_{br}\cos\theta} \]

To minimize \(D\), differentiate with respect to \(\theta\):

\[ \frac{dD}{d\theta}=L\frac{\dfrac{v_r}{v_{br}}\sin\theta-1}{\cos^2\theta} \]

At the minimum,

\[ \frac{dD}{d\theta}=0 \]

so

\[ \frac{v_r}{v_{br}}\sin\theta=1 \]

Hence, the heading angle for minimum drift is

\[ \boxed{\sin\theta=\frac{v_{br}}{v_r}} \]

This is possible because \(v_{br} \lt v_r\). Using this condition,

\[ \cos\theta=\sqrt{1-\frac{v_{br}^2}{v_r^2}}=\frac{\sqrt{v_r^2-v_{br}^2}}{v_r} \]

Substituting into the drift expression gives

\[ \boxed{D_{\min}=\frac{L}{v_{br}}\sqrt{v_r^2-v_{br}^2}} \]

Minimum drift condition is also mentioned as shortest path condition as displacement is unidirectional.

Drift, like horizontal range in projectile motion, depends on both the transverse component and the time available for that component to act: \[ D=v_{b/x}T=\frac{Lv_{b/x}}{v_{b/y}} \] Thus, changing the heading changes drift in two ways: it changes the horizontal component and it also changes the crossing time through the vertical component.

Particular Drift or Particular Direction of \(\vec v_b\)

So far, we have considered optimization problems in which the boatman changes the heading angle to minimize or maximize a quantity. But the same framework can also be used when a particular drift or a particular direction of the ground velocity is specified.

Suppose the required drift is \(D\). Since the boat must cross a river of width \(L\), its displacement from the starting point to the landing point is

\[ \Delta\vec r=D\hat i+L\hat j. \]

Therefore, the direction of the resultant ground velocity \(\vec v_b\) is already fixed by the required drift. If \(\beta\) is the angle that \(\vec v_b\) makes with the perpendicular to the river, then

\[ \tan\beta=\frac{D}{L}. \]

Thus, specifying the drift is equivalent to specifying the direction of \(\vec v_b\).

Once the direction of \(\vec v_b\) is known, the problem becomes a vector-addition problem:

\[ \vec v_b=\vec v_{br}+\vec v_r. \]

The magnitudes \(v_{br}\) and \(v_r\) are fixed, while the direction of \(\vec v_{br}\) is controlled by the boatman.

Let the heading angle of the boat be \(\theta\), measured from the perpendicular to the river. Then

\[ \vec v_{br} = -v_{br}\sin\theta\,\hat i + v_{br}\cos\theta\,\hat j. \]

If the resultant velocity makes an angle \(\beta\) with the perpendicular, its direction is completely known. The three vectors can therefore be represented as a velocity triangle.

The angle between \(\vec v_{br}\) and \(\vec v_b\) is \(\theta+\beta\). Applying the sine rule to the velocity triangle gives

\[ \frac{v_r}{\sin(\theta+\beta)} = \frac{v_{br}}{\cos\beta}. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ \sin(\theta+\beta) = \frac{v_r}{v_{br}}\cos\beta }. \]

Thus, a specified drift \(D\), or equivalently a specified direction \(\beta\) of \(\vec v_b\), can be used to determine the required heading angle \(\theta\).

Once the heading is known, the resultant velocity can be obtained and the crossing time follows from its perpendicular component:

\[ T=\frac{L}{v_{b/y}}. \]
Particular drift and particular resultant direction are essentially the same type of problem.
The river width \(L\) is fixed. Therefore, specifying the drift \(D\) fixes the direction of the resultant displacement and hence the direction of the ground velocity \(\vec v_b\).

The problem then becomes: \[ \boxed{ \text{fixed }|\vec v_r|,\ |\vec v_{br}| + \text{specified direction of }\vec v_b \rightarrow \text{required heading of the boat} } \]

Wind-Aeroplane Problems

Wind-aeroplane problems have essentially the same mathematical structure as river-boat problems. There are three velocity vectors:

  • \(\vec v_w\) — velocity of the wind relative to the ground.
  • \(\vec v_{pw}\) — velocity of the plane relative to the wind, i.e. its velocity in still air.
  • \(\vec v_p\) — velocity of the plane relative to the ground.

They are related by the same relative-velocity equation:

\[ \boxed{\vec v_p=\vec v_{pw}+\vec v_w} \]

As in the river-boat model, the magnitudes of the wind velocity and the plane's airspeed are taken as fixed. The pilot's controllable quantity is the heading direction of the plane.

Depending on the direction of the wind relative to the plane's heading, three common situations arise:

  • Headwind: the wind has a component opposite to the direction of the plane's heading.
  • Tailwind: the wind has a component along the direction of the plane's heading.
  • Crosswind: the wind is perpendicular to the plane's heading.

Thus, the same vector-addition framework applies:

\[ \vec v_p=\vec v_{pw}+\vec v_w \]

The practical problem is again to determine how the heading of the controllable velocity vector changes the resultant ground velocity.

River-boat and wind-aeroplane problems are the same vector framework in different physical settings.
In both cases, one velocity is imposed by the environment and another has a fixed magnitude but controllable direction. Changing the heading changes the resultant velocity.

This is why crosswind problems are particularly important in aviation: the pilot must account for the wind vector while choosing the aircraft's heading so that the resulting ground velocity has the required direction.
