2 D constant acceleration motion.

Constant Acceleration Motion

In Motion in 1D, we studied the five equations of motion involving displacement (\(s\)), initial velocity (\(u\)), final velocity (\(v\)), acceleration (\(a\)) and time (\(t\)).

These equations were developed for a very specific mathematical situation: motion under constant acceleration.

Projectile motion is nothing but motion under free fall. Since the gravitational acceleration remains constant and directed vertically downward, projectile motion is also a special case of constant acceleration motion.

Therefore, the equations of motion remain valid for projectile motion as well.

However, there is an important mathematical difference. In one-dimensional motion, quantities such as displacement, velocity and acceleration can be represented using signed scalars. In projectile motion, the motion occurs simultaneously in horizontal and vertical directions.

As a result, displacement, velocity and acceleration must now be treated as vectors.

Projectile motion is not a new mathematical theory.
It is the same constant acceleration model studied in Motion in 1D, represented in a higher-dimensional vector framework.

Thus, the objective of this section is not to learn a new set of equations, but to learn how the familiar one-dimensional equations transform into vector form while describing the same physical situation.

Vector Form of Equations of Motion

The equations of motion are fundamentally vector equations.

In Motion in 1D, the vector nature remains hidden because all motion occurs along a single straight line. Since every vector is constrained to the same direction, the vector notation can be replaced by algebraic signs.

Projectile motion removes this geometric restriction. The motion now occurs simultaneously in horizontal and vertical directions, making the vector nature of the equations explicit.

The five equations of motion therefore become:

\[ \vec{s} = \vec{u}t + \frac{1}{2}\vec{a}t^2 \] \[ \vec{s} = \vec{v}t - \frac{1}{2}\vec{a}t^2 \] \[ \vec{v} = \vec{u} + \vec{a}t \] \[ \vec{s} = \left(\frac{\vec{u}+\vec{v}}{2}\right)t \] \[ \vec{v}\cdot\vec{v} = \vec{u}\cdot\vec{u} + 2\vec{a}\cdot\vec{s} \]

Notice that the equations themselves remain unchanged.

Only the mathematical representation changes from scalar quantities to vectors.

Motion in 1D and projectile motion are governed by the same constant-acceleration mathematics.
The difference is not in the physics.
The difference is only in the geometric representation.

This idea appears repeatedly throughout physics. The mathematical structure remains the same while the representation becomes more general.

Example Use of the Vector Framework

At first glance, the vector form of the equations may appear unnecessarily complicated.

In practice, however, vector equations often reveal symmetries and relationships that are difficult to see through component-based calculations alone.

The following example illustrates both approaches.

Illustration 1

A projectile is thrown from the ground with a speed of \(50\text{ m/s}\) at an angle of \(57^\circ\) with the horizontal.

Find the time instants at which its velocity makes an angle of \(45^\circ\) with the horizontal.

Take \(g = 10\text{ m/s}^2\).

Structural Observation

We are not asked about the trajectory of the projectile.

We are asked about the orientation of the velocity vector.

Since projectile motion is symmetric about its highest point, a particular velocity orientation may occur more than once during the flight.

A common examination trap is to find only one time coordinate.
Whenever a projectile is launched and lands at the same level, always check whether symmetry produces multiple physical solutions.

We first resolve the initial velocity into rectangular components:

\[ u_x = 50\cos57^\circ = 30\text{ m/s} \] \[ u_y = 50\sin57^\circ = 40\text{ m/s} \]

Since horizontal acceleration is zero:

\[ v_x = 30\text{ m/s} \]

The vertical velocity evolves according to:

\[ v_y = 40 - 10t \]

The inclination of the velocity vector is determined by:

\[ \tan\alpha = \frac{v_y}{v_x} \]

Ascending Phase

During ascent, the velocity vector points above the horizontal, so:

\[ \alpha = 45^\circ \] \[ 1 = \frac{40-10t}{30} \] \[ t=1\text{ s} \]

Descending Phase

During descent, the velocity vector points below the horizontal, so:

\[ \alpha=-45^\circ \] \[ -1 = \frac{40-10t}{30} \] \[ t=7\text{ s} \]

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{t=1\text{ s},\;7\text{ s}} \]
