Cartesian Vectors

Understanding the absolute spatial rigidity of the mathematical \(\hat{i}, \hat{j}\) framework.

Free Vector vs. Bound Vector

Before analyzing specific coordinate systems, it is vital to mathematically distinguish between two fundamental classes of vectors utilized in classical mechanics:

Free Vectors: A vector whose physical action or mathematical definition is not rigidly tied to a specific point in space. It is characterized strictly by its magnitude and direction. If you parallel-translate a free vector anywhere across the coordinate plane, it remains the exact identical vector functionally. (e.g., a uniform velocity field, or a generic displacement vector \(\Delta\vec{r}\)).

Bound Vectors (or Localized Vectors): A vector whose geometric meaning or physical effect strictly depends on its exact point of application (its "tail"). If you physically translate it to a different location, it fundamentally changes the physical scenario. (e.g., the position vector \(\vec{r}\) originating specifically from the origin, or a force vector applied to a specific hinge on a door).

Constant Direction of the Unit Vector

The standard two-dimensional Cartesian coordinate system explicitly relies on orthogonal unit vectors \(\hat{i}\) (pointing along the positive x-axis) and \(\hat{j}\) (pointing along the positive y-axis).

A profound and mathematically convenient property of Cartesian unit vectors \(\hat{i}\) and \(\hat{j}\) is that they are entirely free vectors with respect to their directional definition. Their orientation is absolutely logically invariant everywhere in the universe.

Regardless of whether a particle is located at \((1,1)\) or at \((1000, -500)\), the \(\hat{i}\) vector at that localized position invariably points "horizontally to the right", and the \(\hat{j}\) vector invariably points "vertically upward".

Because their geometric orientation securely never structurally physically changes as a particle dynamically moves through space, the mathematical algebraic derivatives of these specific unit vectors with respect to time are identically zero:

\[ \frac{d\hat{i}}{dt} = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{d\hat{j}}{dt} = 0 \]

This absolute spatial invariant rigidity is precisely why deriving velocity in Cartesian coordinates is mechanically straightforward: \[ \frac{d}{dt}(x\hat{i} + y\hat{j}) = \left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)\hat{i} + x\left(\frac{d\hat{i}}{dt}\right) + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)\hat{j} + y\left(\frac{d\hat{j}}{dt}\right) = \dot{x}\hat{i} + \dot{y}\hat{j} \] The unit vectors transparently pass safely consistently directly through the time-derivative unbroken.
