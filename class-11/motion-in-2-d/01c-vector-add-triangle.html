Vector Addition — Triangle Law

The fundamental operation for combining vectors.

Vector Addition

A defining feature of vectors is that they can be added to one another to produce another vector.

Vector addition is applicable to vectors in one, two and three dimensions. This makes it a fundamental operation for combining vectors.

Multiplication of a vector by a scalar is also closely related to addition. For example,

\[ 3\vec A=\vec A+\vec A+\vec A \]

Thus, scalar multiplication can be viewed as repeated addition of the same vector.

Other vector operations have different characteristics. The dot product of two vectors produces a scalar, while the cross product is a special operation applicable to vectors in three dimensions.

Vector addition is the fundamental operation for combining vectors.
Scalar multiplication is built upon it.

Triangle Law of Vector Addition

In the previous chapter, we already learned how to add vectors in \(1D\), i.e. collinear vectors. This does not really require the triangle law; we simply add their magnitudes algebraically, with signs accounting for their opposite directions.

However, when adding two non-collinear vectors, their directions cannot be represented by an algebraic sign alone. We need a geometrical rule that accounts for both their magnitudes and directions.

To add two vectors, arrange them head-to-tail: place the tail of the second vector at the head of the first vector.

The resultant vector is drawn from the tail of the first vector to the head of the second vector.

\[ \boxed{\vec R=\vec A+\vec B} \]

Thus, the three vectors form a triangle, with the resultant as the third side joining the tail of \(\vec A\) to the head of \(\vec B\). This geometrical construction is known as the triangle law of vector addition.

Geometry

Suppose \(\vec A\) and \(\vec B\) are arranged head-to-tail:

\[ \vec A \quad\longrightarrow\quad \vec B \]

The resultant \(\vec R\) is the third side of the triangle joining the tail of \(\vec A\) to the head of \(\vec B\). Thus,

\[ \boxed{\vec A+\vec B=\vec R} \]

In other words, the resultant is the vector whose tail coincides with the tail of the first vector and whose head coincides with the head of the second vector.

Vector Addition Formula

If the magnitudes of two vectors are \(A\) and \(B\), and the angle between them is \(\theta\), then the magnitude of their resultant is

\[ \boxed{R=\sqrt{A^2+B^2+2AB\cos\theta}} \]

The direction of the resultant can be determined from the geometry of the triangle.

Vector Subtraction

Vector subtraction can be expressed in terms of vector addition:

\[ \boxed{\vec A-\vec B=\vec A+(-\vec B)} \]

Thus, subtracting a vector is equivalent to adding a vector of the same magnitude but opposite direction.

Resultant Variation

Thus, if two vectors have constant magnitudes \(a\) and \(b\), and we vary the angle \(\theta\) between them, then the magnitude of their resultant varies from \(a+b\) to \(\lvert a-b\rvert\).

When the vectors are in the same direction, \(\theta=0^\circ\), and the resultant is maximum:

\[ \boxed{R_{\max}=a+b} \]

When the vectors are in opposite directions, \(\theta=180^\circ\), and the resultant is minimum:

\[ \boxed{R_{\min}=\lvert a-b\rvert} \]

This variation of the resultant with the angle between the vectors is frequently used in problems.
