Projectile from a Height

Examining trajectories initiated above the landing plane.

Projection from a Tower

Projection from a tower is closely related to the motion under gravity studied in the previous chapter. There, the ball was simply dropped from the tower.

Here, the particle is given an initial horizontal velocity. More generally, we can also consider an oblique projection.

Consider a particle projected exactly horizontally with an initial speed \(u\) from the top of a tower of height \(H\) .

As in motion under gravity from a tower, the key known quantities are \(u\) and \(H\) . We take the launch point as the origin, so the ground is at a vertical displacement \(s_y=-H\)

Since the projection is purely horizontal, the initial velocity has the components

  • \(u_x=u\)
  • \(u_y=0\)
Time of Descent (\(T\)):

The time taken to reach the ground is determined entirely by the vertical motion. Using \[ s_y=-H \qquad u_y=0 \qquad a_y=-g \] we get

\[ -H=(0)T+\frac12(-g)T^2 \]

and hence

\[ \boxed{T=\sqrt{\frac{2H}{g}}} \]
A particle dropped from the top of a tower and another particle projected horizontally from the same point, at the same instant, reach the ground at the same time.
Their horizontal motions are different, but their vertical motions are identical.
Horizontal Range (\(R\)):

Since there is no horizontal acceleration, \(a_x=0\) , the horizontal velocity remains constant at \(u_x=u\). Therefore, during the time of descent \(T\),

\[ R=u_xT=uT \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{R=u\sqrt{\frac{2H}{g}}} \]

The given information in a problem may not be \(u\) and \(H\) directly. It may instead provide quantities such as the time of descent \(T\) and horizontal range \(R\). From

\[ H=\frac12gT^2 \]

we can determine \(H\), and from

\[ u=\frac{R}{T} \]

we can determine \(u\).

Oblique Projection from a Tower

Now suppose the particle is projected from the tower of height \(h\) with an initial speed \(u\), but at an angle \(\theta\) above the horizontal.

We set the launch point as the origin \((0,0)\). The ground is now physically located at a vertical displacement of \(y = -h\).

  • \(u_x = u\cos\theta\)
  • \(u_y = u\sin\theta\)
  • \(a_y = -g\)
Time of Flight Calculation:

We substitute the final displacement into the vertical kinematic equation: \[ y = u_y t + \frac{1}{2} a_y t^2 \] \[ -h = (u\sin\theta)t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2 \] \[ \frac{1}{2}gt^2 - (u\sin\theta)t - h = 0 \] This yields a quadratic equation in \(t\). Solving this quadratic provides the total time of flight \(T\). Only the mathematically positive root is physically meaningful.

Once the time of flight \(T\) is secured, the horizontal range from the base of the tower is cleanly calculated as: \[ R = (u\cos\theta)T \]
