Theory and problems of Angle between Vectors.

Relations between Vectors

If we can perform mathematical operations on vectors, we can also define relations between vector quantities. But how do we compare two vectors?

For scalar quantities, we can directly compare their numerical values using \(\gt\), \(\lt\) and \(=\). For vectors, however, direction does not have an ordering. It is meaningless to say that east is greater than north or that north is less than south.

Therefore, when we compare vector quantities using greater-than or less-than language, the comparison is implicitly made using their magnitudes.

For example, suppose the velocity of car A is \(5\,\mathrm{m/s}\) towards east and the velocity of car B is \(6\,\mathrm{m/s}\) towards west. Taking east as positive,

\[ \vec v_A=5\hat i \qquad \vec v_B=-6\hat i \]

The velocities have different directions, so there is no meaningful sense in which one direction is greater than the other. But their magnitudes can be compared:

\[ |\vec v_A|=5\,\mathrm{m/s} \qquad |\vec v_B|=6\,\mathrm{m/s} \]

Thus, when we say that car B has a greater velocity in the usual physical language, what is actually being compared is the magnitude of velocity:

\[ |\vec v_B|>|\vec v_A| \]

The same idea appears when we say that the velocity of a car "increases" or "decreases". For example, if the velocity of car B changes from \(-6\hat i\) to \(-10\hat i\) , then its velocity is said to have increased in magnitude:

\[ |\vec v_B|:6\rightarrow10\,\mathrm{m/s} \]

The vector has become more negative along the chosen positive direction, but its magnitude has increased. This is what is ordinarily meant when we say that the velocity has increased in such a context.

For greater-than and less-than comparisons of vector quantities, the comparison is implicitly a comparison of their magnitudes, because magnitude has an ordinary numerical ordering whereas direction does not.

Equality of vectors however, is different. For two vectors to be equal, their magnitudes as well as their directions must be the same:

\[ \boxed{\vec A=\vec B\iff|\vec A|=|\vec B|\quad\text{and}\quad\vec A,\vec B\text{ have the same direction}} \]

Therefore, two vectors can have equal magnitudes without being equal vectors. For example,

\[ |\vec A|=|\vec B| \]

does not imply

\[ \vec A=\vec B \]

if the two vectors point in different directions.

Magnitude comparison and vector equality are different relations. Greater/lesser language refers to magnitude, while equality of vectors requires both magnitude and direction to agree.

Any comparison of physical quantities is meaningful only when the quantities are of the same physical kind. This applies equally to scalar and vector quantities: mass can be compared with mass, and velocity with velocity, but mass cannot be compared with temperature or velocity with acceleration.

Other Relations

Vectors can be related to each other in ways other than their magnitude or equality. Since direction is an essential part of a vector, we can relate vectors based on their directional and geometrical relationships.

One such relation is parallelism. Two vectors are parallel when they have the same or opposite direction. The physical quantities represented by the vectors do not need to be the same.

For example, consider a force of \(5\,\mathrm N\) towards east and a velocity of \(5\,\mathrm{m/s}\) towards east:

\[ \vec F=5\hat i\qquad\vec v=5\hat i \]

Although force and velocity are different physical quantities and have different dimensions, their vectors point in the same direction. Therefore, they are parallel vectors.

If two vectors point in the same direction, they are called parallel. If they point in exactly opposite directions, they are called anti-parallel.

\[ \boxed{\vec A\parallel\vec B\quad\text{if their directions are the same or opposite}} \]

For two non-zero parallel vectors, their magnitudes can be separated from their common directional information. For example,

\[ \vec A=A\hat a,\qquad\vec B=B\hat a \]

when they point in the same direction. Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\frac{\vec A}{A}=\frac{\vec B}{B}=\hat a} \]

The common unit vector \(\hat a\) represents their common direction. Notice that the vectors \(\vec A\) and \(\vec B\) can represent completely different physical quantities; their magnitudes may even have different physical dimensions. Their common unit vector, however, represents only the common spatial direction.

A unit vector represents direction only. It has unit magnitude and therefore carries no magnitude information of the physical quantity represented by the original vector.

Since a unit vector is a ratio of a vector to its magnitude,

\[ \hat a=\frac{\vec A}{|\vec A|} \]

the physical dimensions cancel. For example,

\[ \frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{N}}=1,\qquad\frac{\mathrm{m/s}}{\mathrm{m/s}}=1 \]

Thus, a unit vector is dimensionless. It is also unitless: it does not have a physical unit such as metre, second or newton. Its numerical magnitude is \(1\), while its role is to retain directional information.

The same idea applies to anti-parallel vectors. If \(\vec A\) and \(\vec B\) point in opposite directions, then

\[ \frac{\vec A}{A}=-\frac{\vec B}{B} \]

Thus, the unit vectors are equal for parallel vectors and opposite for anti-parallel vectors.

Other Geometrical Relations

Vectors can also be related by their geometrical arrangement in space.

Two (or more) parallel or anti-parallel vectors are also called as collinear vectors.

Three or more vectors are coplanar if they lie in the same plane.

Two non-collinear vectors are always co-planar (just like 2 points are always collinear).

Coplanarity is a geometrical relation and does not require the vectors to have the same physical dimensions or magnitude.

Vectors can also be related by the geometrical arrangement of their lines of action.

Vectors are called concurrent when their lines of action pass through a common point. This terminology is particularly useful in mechanics, where several forces acting on a particle are concurrent at the particle.

Concurrency is different from parallelism. Parallel vectors have the same or opposite direction, whereas concurrent vectors are identified by the intersection of their lines of action.

  • Parallel Vectors — same direction
  • Anti-Parallel Vectors — opposite directions
  • Coplanar Vectors — vectors lying in the same plane
  • Concurrent Vectors — lines of action passing through a common point

Summary of Relations between Vectors

A vector contains both magnitude and directional information. Therefore, different types of comparison can be made by comparing different parts of this information.

What is compared? Relation Condition
Magnitude Greater than / Less than \(|\vec A|>|\vec B|\) or \(|\vec A|\lt|\vec B|\)
Direction Parallel Same direction
Direction Anti-parallel Opposite directions
Direction Perpendicular/Orthogonal \(\theta=90^\circ\)
Complete vector Equal \(|\vec A|=|\vec B|\) and same direction
Equality is the complete comparison.
Greater-than and less-than comparisons involve only magnitude, while directional relations such as parallel and perpendicular involve direction. Equality of vectors requires both magnitude and direction to be identical.

Thus, the different relations between vectors can be understood by asking which part of the vector's information is being compared: its magnitude, its direction, or the complete vector.
