Orthonormal Resolution

The Gram matrix explains why orthonormal components make vector multiplication so simple.

Orthonormal Resolution

We learned that if there are two vectors \(\vec P\) and \(\vec Q\), then we can perform operations such as:

  • Vector Addition
  • Dot Product
  • Cross Product

To perform these operations, we need to know their magnitudes \(|\vec P|\) and \(|\vec Q|\), and the angle \(\theta\) between them. This much information completely specifies the mutual geometry between them.

For the vector operations:

\[ \vec P\cdot\vec Q = |\vec P||\vec Q|\cos\theta \] \[ |\vec P\times\vec Q|=|\vec P||\vec Q|\sin\theta \] \[ |\vec P+\vec Q|^2=|\vec P|^2+|\vec Q|^2+2|\vec P||\vec Q|\cos\theta \]

However, instead of describing the vectors by their magnitudes and their mutual angle, we can choose a basis (a system of unit vectors) and resolve each vector into components.

For example, choose two basis vectors \(\hat i\) and \(\hat j\), and write \(\vec P\) as \(\vec P=P_x\hat i+P_y\hat j\) and \(\vec Q=Q_x\hat i+Q_y\hat j\) .

Now these vectors are described by their components only. Like \(\vec P\) is described by \(P_x\) and \(P_y\) and \(\vec Q\) by \(Q_x\) and \(Q_y\) .

With this information, we dont need to separately know their magnitudes \(|\vec P|\) and \(|\vec Q|\), and the angle \(\theta\) between them to perform vector operations.

So resolution does not add or remove information. It simply provides a different way of encoding the same vectors.

\[ \boxed{|\vec P|,\, |\vec Q|,\, \theta \qquad \longrightarrow \qquad P_x,\, P_y,\, Q_x,\, Q_y} \]

The advantage of this representation is that vector operations on \(\vec P\) and \(\vec Q\) can now be performed by simple arithmetic operations on \(P_x,\, P_y,\, Q_x,\, Q_y\) .

In this topic, we try to understand what benefit orthonormal resolution provides over some other angle.

When we talk of orthonormal resolution, angle between \(\hat i\) and \(\hat j\) is \(90^\circ\) .

So, instead of \(\hat i\) and \(\hat j\) , we consider a basis having angle \(60^\circ\) degree between them, then how would operations be different.

Like consider \(\hat a\) and \(\hat b\) , such that

\[ |\hat a| = |\hat b| = 1 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \angle(\hat{a},\hat{b})=60^\circ \]

Now write \(\vec P\) as \(\vec P=P_a\hat a+P_b\hat b\) and \(\vec Q=Q_a\hat a+Q_b\hat b\) .

What advantage does \(P_x,\, P_y,\, Q_x,\, Q_y\) give over \(P_a,\, P_b,\, Q_a,\, Q_b\) .

Vector Addition

We first compare vector addition operation.

For the orthonormal resolution:

\[ \vec P=P_x\hat i+P_y\hat j \qquad \vec Q=Q_x\hat i+Q_y\hat j \] \[ \vec P+\vec Q=(P_x+Q_x)\hat i+(P_y+Q_y)\hat j \]

So the components of \(\vec P+\vec Q\) are simply

\[ \boxed{R_x=P_x+Q_x \qquad R_y=P_y+Q_y} \]

Now consider the \(60^\circ\) resolution:

\[ \vec P=P_a\hat a+P_b\hat b \qquad \vec Q=Q_a\hat a+Q_b\hat b \] \[ \vec P+\vec Q=(P_a+Q_a)\hat a+(P_b+Q_b)\hat b \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{R_a=P_a+Q_a \qquad R_b=P_b+Q_b} \]

The addition rule is exactly the same in both resolutions : Addition is componentwise, regardless of the angle between the basis vectors.

Therefore, orthonormal resolution provides no special advantage for vector addition operation.

Dot Product

Now we compare the dot product operation which demonstrates the main benefit.

In the case of orthonormal resolution \(\hat i\) and \(\hat j\) are unit vectors and perpendicular. So we get,

\[ \boxed{\vec P\cdot\vec Q=P_xQ_x+P_yQ_y} \]

This is because:

\[ \hat i\cdot\hat i=\hat j\cdot\hat j=1 \qquad \hat i\cdot\hat j=\hat j\cdot\hat i=0 \]

In the case of \(60^\circ\) resolution:

\[ \hat a\cdot\hat a=\hat b\cdot\hat b=1 \qquad \hat a\cdot\hat b=\hat b\cdot\hat a=\cos60^\circ=\frac12 \]

And so for dot product of \(\vec P=P_a\hat a+P_b\hat b\) and \(\vec Q=Q_a\hat a+Q_b\hat b\)

\[ \begin{aligned} \vec P\cdot\vec Q &=(P_a\hat a+P_b\hat b)\cdot(Q_a\hat a+Q_b\hat b)\\ &=P_aQ_a(\hat a\cdot\hat a)+P_aQ_b(\hat a\cdot\hat b)+P_bQ_a(\hat b\cdot\hat a)+P_bQ_b(\hat b\cdot\hat b) \end{aligned} \] \[ \boxed{ \vec P\cdot\vec Q=P_aQ_a+P_bQ_b+\frac12(P_aQ_b+P_bQ_a)} \]

The difference is clear. In the orthonormal resolution, multiplication of different components gives \(0\), while multiplication of a component with itself gives \(1\). Thus, we can use "1" as a multiplicative identity in the framework of directional multiplication.

"1" is called as multiplicative identity and "0" is called as additive identity.
We use this like \(1^2 = 1^3 = 1\) .

Matrix Representation

Write the components of \(\vec P\) and \(\vec Q\) as column matrices:

$$ \mathbf p= \begin{pmatrix} P_x\\ P_y \end{pmatrix} \qquad \mathbf q= \begin{pmatrix} Q_x\\ Q_y \end{pmatrix} $$

For the orthonormal basis, the multiplication between the basis vectors can be represented by the matrix:

$$ \boxed{ G= \begin{pmatrix} \hat i\cdot\hat i & \hat i\cdot\hat j\\ \hat j\cdot\hat i & \hat j\cdot\hat j \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1&0\\ 0&1 \end{pmatrix} =I } $$

This matrix is called the Gram matrix of the basis. It contains all the information about how the basis vectors multiply with each other under dot product.

Therefore, the dot product can be written as:

$$ \boxed{ \vec P\cdot\vec Q=\mathbf p^{T}G\mathbf q } $$

For the orthonormal basis, \(G=I\), so:

$$ \vec P\cdot\vec Q = \mathbf p^{T}I\mathbf q = \mathbf p^{T}\mathbf q $$ $$ \boxed{ \vec P\cdot\vec Q = \begin{pmatrix} P_x&P_y \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} Q_x\\ Q_y \end{pmatrix} = P_xQ_x+P_yQ_y } $$

So the Gram matrix being the identity matrix is exactly what allows the dot product to be obtained by ordinary matrix multiplication of the component matrices.

Now consider the \(60^\circ\) basis. Its Gram matrix is:

$$ \boxed{ G= \begin{pmatrix} \hat a\cdot\hat a & \hat a\cdot\hat b\\ \hat b\cdot\hat a & \hat b\cdot\hat b \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1&\frac12\\ \frac12&1 \end{pmatrix} } $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \vec P\cdot\vec Q = \begin{pmatrix} P_a&P_b \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1&\frac12\\ \frac12&1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} Q_a\\ Q_b \end{pmatrix} } $$

The extra \(\frac12\) terms appear because the basis vectors are not perpendicular.

Thus, the difference between the two resolutions can be summarized by their Gram matrices:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Orthonormal: }\quad G=I= \begin{pmatrix} 1&0\\ 0&1 \end{pmatrix} } $$ $$ \boxed{ 60^\circ:\quad G= \begin{pmatrix} 1&\frac12\\ \frac12&1 \end{pmatrix} } $$

Therefore, the main advantage of orthonormal resolution is that the Gram matrix of the basis is the identity matrix. The basis multiplication then requires no additional factors or cross terms.
