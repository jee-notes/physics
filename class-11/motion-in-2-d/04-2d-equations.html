Vector Equations of Motion

Using Orthogonal Independence in Kinematics.

Orthogonal Independence

What is orthogonal independence? It means that the effect of changing one component along one direction can be treated independently of the component along the other direction.

But why are two orthogonal directions independent? This is often presented as if it were simply a fact about directions.

Consider first two collinear directions. They are not independent because one direction is only a scalar multiple of the other. If \(\hat u\) and \(\hat v\) are collinear, then

\[ \hat v=\lambda\hat u \]

for some scalar \(\lambda\). Therefore, there is really only one independent direction.

Now consider two non-collinear directions. Neither can be obtained as a scalar multiple of the other, so they provide two independent directions.

Orthogonality is a particularly useful case of this independence. If

\[ \hat u\perp\hat v \]

then the two directions are not only independent, but the components along them have a special mathematical separation. For

\[ \vec R=a\hat u+b\hat v \]

the magnitude is

\[ R^2=a^2+b^2 \]

with no cross-term involving both \(a\) and \(b\). Consequently, changing \(a\) does not introduce a contribution proportional to \(b\), and changing \(b\) does not introduce a contribution proportional to \(a\).

Orthogonal independence is not a mystical property of \(90^\circ\).
It is the mathematical fact that perpendicular component directions provide independent directions, while also eliminating the coupling term between their magnitudes.

Orthogonal Independence Area

Another way to understand orthogonal independence is geometrically, in terms of the spatial extent of the two directions.

If two vectors are collinear, their spatial extents completely overlap: both lie along the same line. There is therefore no new spatial extent contributed by the second direction.

As the angle between two vectors increases, the second vector contributes an increasing amount of spatial extent that is not along the first vector. For vectors of fixed magnitudes \(a\) and \(b\), the area of the parallelogram formed by them is

\[ A=ab\sin\theta \]

This area is a measure of the spatial extent generated by the two directions. It is zero when the vectors are collinear and becomes maximum when they are perpendicular:

\[ \theta=90^\circ \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad A_{\max}=ab \]

At \(90^\circ\), the two directions have no extent along each other. The component of one vector along the other is zero:

\[ b\cos90^\circ=0 \qquad a\cos90^\circ=0 \]

Thus, the two directions have zero directional overlap. This is a geometric way of understanding why orthogonal directions are maximally independent.

Orthogonality can be viewed as maximum directional independence:
for fixed magnitudes, perpendicular directions generate the maximum new spatial extent between them, while neither direction has any component along the other.

Directional Independence

Directional independence is a comparative concept. A direction by itself is neither independent nor dependent; independence is a relation between two directions.

Consider two workers pushing a heavy boulder with forces \(\vec A\) and \(\vec B\). If they push in the same direction, their efforts completely reinforce each other. If they push in opposite directions, they completely oppose each other.

Now imagine that I am the boss and want to pay each worker according to their own contribution. If their forces are at some arbitrary angle, either worker can claim that the other was assisting or opposing their work because part of one force acts along the direction of the other.

But if they push at \(90^\circ\),

\[ \vec A\perp\vec B \]

then neither force has any component along the direction of the other:

\[ A_{\parallel B}=A\cos90^\circ=0 \qquad B_{\parallel A}=B\cos90^\circ=0 \]

Therefore, neither worker can assist or oppose the other's work along the other's direction. Their contributions are completely separate directionally, so the work attributable to each direction can be distinguished independently.

Orthogonality represents maximum directional independence.
At \(0^\circ\), two vectors completely reinforce each other; at \(180^\circ\), they completely oppose each other; at \(90^\circ\), there is zero directional overlap, so neither contributes along the other's direction.

Orthogonal Independence

Consider two vectors \(\vec A\) and \(\vec B\), with an angle \(\theta\) between them. What does it actually mean for their directions to be independent?

If the two vectors are collinear, their directions are completely dependent. One vector can be expressed as a scalar multiple of the other:

\[ \vec B=\lambda\vec A \]

Thus, a separate directional description for \(\vec B\) is not required. The direction of \(\vec A\), together with the scalar \(\lambda\), completely specifies \(\vec B\).

As the vectors become non-collinear, one vector can no longer be completely expressed using the other. However, some part of one vector can still lie along the direction of the other. If the angle between them is \(\theta\), the component of \(\vec B\) along \(\vec A\) is

\[ B\cos\theta \]

Therefore, for two non-collinear directions, there can still be some directional overlap.

Now consider the special case

\[ \theta=90^\circ \]

Then

\[ B\cos90^\circ=0 \]

So \(\vec B\) has no component whatsoever along \(\vec A\). Similarly, \(\vec A\) has no component along \(\vec B\):

\[ A\cos90^\circ=0 \]

Thus, neither direction contains any part that can be represented using the other. The two directions provide completely separate directional information.

Orthogonal independence means zero directional overlap.
For collinear directions, one is completely expressible as a scalar multiple of the other. For non-collinear directions, some part of one may still overlap with the other. At \(90^\circ\), this overlap becomes zero, so neither direction contains any component that can be expressed using the other.

This is the precise sense in which orthogonal directions are independent. Orthogonality is therefore not an arbitrary rule imposed on directions; it is the special case in which the directional overlap between two independent directions is exactly zero.

This orthogonal independence is then used in resolution: a vector is represented by components along mutually perpendicular directions so that the directional contributions are completely separated.
