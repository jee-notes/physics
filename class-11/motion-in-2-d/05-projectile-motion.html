Projectile Motion

Representation of 2D motion as two simultaneous 1D motions.

Application of Orthogonal Independence

When an object is thrown into the air, its subsequent unpowered motion under the sole influence of gravity, with air resistance neglected, is called projectile motion.

The projectile moves in two spatial dimensions: horizontally and vertically. The key to solving this 2D motion is to resolve it into two mutually perpendicular 1D motions.

The horizontal and vertical directions are orthogonal, so their contributions can be treated independently. This is a direct application of the orthogonal independence discussed earlier.

Gravity acts vertically downward. Therefore, its horizontal component is zero:

\[ g_x=g\cos90^\circ=0 \]

Hence, the acceleration components are

\[ a_x=0 \qquad a_y=-g \]

Thus, the horizontal velocity component remains constant, while the vertical velocity component changes with time according to the vertical acceleration. The projectile can therefore be treated as two simultaneous 1D motions occurring over the same time interval.

  • Horizontal: uniform motion with constant velocity.
  • Vertical: 1D motion with constant downward acceleration \(g\).

This is the mathematical advantage of the orthogonal resolution: the horizontal motion can be analysed without needing the details of the vertical motion, and the vertical motion can be analysed without needing the details of the horizontal motion.

Resolving Projectile Motion

In projectile motion, we have three vector quantities to describe: position, velocity and acceleration. The question is not simply whether a vector should be resolved, but which vector should be resolved and along which directions.

To understand this, we first need to understand the physical significance of these quantities. Imagine a body at rest at the point \(O(0,0)\). If its velocity is zero, its position remains unchanged. Conversely, a non-zero velocity means that the position is changing with time. These are simply two ways of describing the same kinematic fact.

Similarly, a non-zero acceleration means that the velocity is changing with time. Again, this is a kinematic relation between the two quantities, not a causal statement.

In projectile motion, acceleration is specified by gravity, while the initial velocity is specified by how the body is projected. The two therefore enter the description of the motion as separately specified quantities.

The choice of what to resolve depends on what is already known and what needs to be determined. We choose the resolution that makes the unknown quantities easiest to track.

In projectile motion, the acceleration is completely known: its magnitude is \(g\), its direction is vertically downward, and it remains constant with time. Therefore, we use the direction of the known acceleration to choose the orthogonal component directions and resolve the initial velocity.

Suppose a particle is projected from the origin \((0,0)\) with initial speed \(u\) at an angle \(\theta\) above the horizontal.

Resolution along Horizontal and Vertical

We resolve the initial velocity because the subsequent \(\vec v(t)\) and \(\vec r(t)\) are determined by the initial velocity \(\vec u\) and the acceleration \(\vec a\). The acceleration is already expressed entirely along one of the chosen component directions because its direction itself is used as a reference direction.

The vertical direction is fixed by the known acceleration due to gravity. For the horizontal direction, any direction perpendicular to it could be chosen. We choose the direction of the horizontal projection of the initial velocity \(\vec u\).

\[ \vec u=u\cos\theta\,\hat i+u\sin\theta\,\hat j \] \[ \boxed{u_x=u\cos\theta \qquad u_y=u\sin\theta} \]

The acceleration is already known:

\[ \vec a=0\hat i-g\hat j \]

so

\[ \boxed{a_x=0 \qquad a_y=-g} \]

The velocity therefore evolves independently through its two components:

\[ \vec v(t)=v_x(t)\hat i+v_y(t)\hat j \]

where

\[ v_x(t)=u\cos\theta \] \[ v_y(t)=u\sin\theta-gt \]

Similarly, the position vector is

\[ \vec r(t)=x(t)\hat i+y(t)\hat j \]

where the two 1D position equations are

\[ x(t)=u\cos\theta\,t \] \[ y(t)=u\sin\theta\,t-\frac12gt^2 \]

Thus, the single 2D motion has been represented through the three resolved vectors

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \vec a(t)&=a_x\hat i+a_y\hat j\\ \vec v(t)&=v_x(t)\hat i+v_y(t)\hat j\\ \vec r(t)&=x(t)\hat i+y(t)\hat j \end{aligned} } \]

Each scalar component is then governed by the corresponding 1D kinematics. Thus, the 2D motion can be treated as two simultaneous 1D motions.

The two 1D motions are independent in their spatial components, but they occur simultaneously and therefore share the same time parameter \(t\). This common parameter connects the two component motions to form the single 2D motion.
