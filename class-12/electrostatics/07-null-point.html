Null Points and Equilibrium Analysis

Locating regions of zero field and analyzing the stability of charge systems.

What are Null Points and Equilibrium?

A null point (or neutral point) is a location in space where the net electric field due to a system of source charges is zero:

\[ \vec E_{\text{net}}=\vec 0 \]
A common doubt is that the electric field becomes zero at infinity, so shouldn't infinity also be a null point?
The answer is no. A null point is a point in space where a test charge can actually be placed and experiences zero net electric field. Infinity is not such a point.
We often hear statements like "parallel lines meet at infinity." If you take it literally then this is as much a joke as saying:
  • "He is rich with ₹0."
  • "He is poor with infinite money."
  • "There is a null point at infinity."
None of these statements should be interpreted literally. Parallel lines do not meet at all. A person with ₹0 is not rich. A person with infinite money is not poor. Likewise, null point at infinity means no null point at all.
It is only an attempt to enforce the mathematical fidelity even when it has no meaning physically.
We can only say that: \[ \lim_{r\to\infty}\vec E=\vec0 \] it doesnt mean that \(r=\infty\), because \(\infty\) is not even a real number.

Consequently, any test charge placed exactly at this point experiences zero electrostatic force and is therefore in electrostatic equilibrium.

However, locating a null point is only the first step. A complete equilibrium analysis requires determining the nature of the equilibrium. If the test charge is displaced slightly from the null point, will the electrostatic force restore it to its original position, drive it further away, or leave it in its new position?

Accordingly, equilibrium may be classified as stable, unstable, or neutral. This classification determines whether the equilibrium configuration is physically sustainable and whether small oscillations about the null point are possible.

Null point analysis consists of two independent tasks:

  • Locate the null point by finding where the net electric field is zero.
  • Classify the equilibrium by studying the force on a slightly displaced test charge.

Finding the null point alone does not complete the analysis.

How do we locate a Null Point?

For a system of two point charges \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) separated by a distance \(d\), the existence and location of the null point depend entirely on the signs and relative magnitudes of the charges.

Null-point analysis always has two stages:
  • Geometry: Determine the region where field cancellation is possible.
  • Algebra: Determine the exact coordinates within that region.
Never begin directly with equations.

If a finite null point exists for two point charges, it always lies on the line joining the charges.

Let \(q_1\) denote the charge with the smaller magnitude and \[ n=\frac{|q_2|}{|q_1|}\ge1 \] Let \(x\) be the distance of the null point from \(q_1\).

Case 1: Like Charges

Between two like charges, the electric fields are in opposite directions. Therefore, cancellation is possible only between the charges.

If the magnitudes are equal \[ q_1=q_2=\pm q \] symmetry immediately gives the null point at the midpoint.

Otherwise,

\[ \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1}{x^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_2}{(d-x)^2} \] \[ x=\frac{d}{\sqrt{\frac{q_2}{q_1}}+1} = \frac{d}{\sqrt n+1} \]

Case 2: Unlike Charges

Between unlike charges, the electric fields point in the same direction. Therefore, cancellation between the charges is impossible.

The null point, if it exists, must lie outside the charges, on the side of the charge having the smaller magnitude.

\[ \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1}{x^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_2}{(d+x)^2} \] \[ x=\frac{d}{\sqrt{\frac{q_2}{q_1}}-1} = \frac{d}{\sqrt n-1} \]
For unlike charges of equal magnitude (an ideal electric dipole), \[ \sqrt n-1=0 \] The formula therefore ceases to yield a finite distance. This simply means that an ideal electric dipole has no finite null point.

How do we analyze the Nature of Equilibrium?

Finding a null point only establishes equilibrium. To determine the nature of the equilibrium, we imagine displacing the test charge by a very small distance \(dx\) and analyze the direction of the resulting electrostatic force.

Equilibrium analysis is a thought experiment. The charge is imagined to be displaced slightly from the equilibrium position, and the resulting force is examined.
  • Stable Equilibrium: The electrostatic force acts opposite to the displacement, driving the charge back toward the equilibrium position. The potential energy is minimum: \[ \frac{d^2U}{dx^2}>0 \] For sufficiently small displacements, the restoring force is often proportional to displacement, leading to Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM).
  • Unstable Equilibrium: The electrostatic force acts in the same direction as the displacement, driving the charge farther away from equilibrium. The potential energy is maximum: \[ \frac{d^2U}{dx^2} \lt 0 \]
  • Neutral Equilibrium: The electrostatic force remains zero after displacement. The charge simply stays in its new position: \[ \frac{d^2U}{dx^2}=0 \]

Example: Charge Between Two Identical Source Charges

Consider two identical fixed positive charges \(+Q\) separated by a distance \(2a\). The midpoint is a null point.

  • If the test charge is positive:
    • Displacement along the line joining the charges → Stable.
    • Displacement perpendicular to the line joining the charges → Unstable.
  • If the test charge is negative:
    • Displacement along the line joining the charges → Unstable.
    • Displacement perpendicular to the line joining the charges → Stable.
Stability is direction-dependent.
A configuration may be stable along one direction and unstable along another.
Therefore, checking equilibrium along only one axis is not sufficient.
Its because, Equilibrium is a property of the point; but stability is a property of its neighbourhood.

Earnshaw's Theorem

Our previous examples show that symmetry can produce a null point. However, a natural question arises:

Can we arrange several fixed charges so that a test charge placed at the null point is stable against displacement in every direction?

For example:

  • Two equal charges are stable along one direction but unstable along another.
  • Four equal charges placed at the corners of a square are stable for displacements in the plane of the square but unstable for displacements perpendicular to the plane.
  • Eight equal charges placed at the corners of a cube are stable along the coordinate axes, but instability still appears along other directions.

Increasing the symmetry to a uniform charge distribution along a spherical surface too does not produce true three-dimensional stable equilibrium.

This observation is generalized by Earnshaw's Theorem, which states that a charge cannot be held in a stable stationary equilibrium solely by the electrostatic forces due to other stationary charges.

A null point only guarantees that the net force is zero at one position.
It does not guarantee that the force will remain restoring after a small displacement.
Earnshaw's theorem states that no arrangement of stationary charges can create a purely electrostatic "trap" that restores a displaced charge in every direction.

Maximum Field: Rolle's Theorem

Consider two identical fixed charges \(+q\) separated by a distance \(2R\). Let \(P\) be a point on the perpendicular bisector. If \(x\) is the distance of \(P\) from the midpoint and \(l\) is its distance from either charge, then

\[ l^2=x^2+R^2 \]

At the midpoint, \[ E(0)=0 \] while \[ \lim_{x\to\infty}E(x)=0 \]

A clever way to predict the existence of a maximum is to use Rolle's Theorem.
Since the electric field has the same value at the two ends, there must exist at least one intermediate point where \[ \frac{dE}{dx}=0 \] Thus, the electric field must attain at least one stationary value between the midpoint and infinity.
Since the field is positive throughout this region, this stationary value is the required maximum.

We now determine its exact location using a trigonometric parametrization.

Let \(\theta\) be the angle between the line joining the charge to point \(P\) and the perpendicular bisector.

\[ \tan\theta=\frac{R}{x} \]

The electric field due to each charge has magnitude

\[ E=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{l^2} \]

The components perpendicular to the bisector are equal and opposite, so they cancel each other. The components along the perpendicular bisector are equal and act in the same direction.

\[ E_{\text{net}}=2E\cos\theta \]

From the right triangle,

\[ \sin\theta=\frac{R}{l} \]

or

\[ l=\frac{R}{\sin\theta} \]

Substituting in the expression for \(E\),

\[ E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q}{\left(\dfrac{R}{\sin\theta}\right)^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q}{R^2}\sin^2\theta \] Therefore, \[ E_{\text{net}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{2q}{R^2} \sin^2\theta\cos\theta \]

Since all other quantities are constants, we only need to maximize

\[ \boxed{f(\theta)=\sin^2\theta\cos\theta} \]

Differentiating with respect to \(\theta\),

\[ \frac{df}{d\theta} = 2\sin\theta\cos^2\theta-\sin^3\theta \] \[ = \sin\theta\left(2\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta\right) \]

At the maximum,

\[ \frac{df}{d\theta}=0 \]

Ignoring the trivial solution \(\theta=0\),

\[ 2\cos^2\theta=\sin^2\theta \]

Dividing throughout by \(\cos^2\theta\),

\[ \tan^2\theta=2 \] \[ \tan\theta=\sqrt{2} \]

Since

\[ \tan\theta=\frac{R}{x} \]

the position of maximum electric field is

\[ \boxed{x=\frac{R}{\sqrt{2}}} \]
The above derivation is much more general than it appears.
Consider any regular polygon having \(n\) identical charges placed at its vertices. At every point on the axis passing through the centre and perpendicular to the plane of the polygon:
  • Each charge is at the same distance \(l\) from the observation point.
  • The components perpendicular to the axis cancel due to symmetry.
  • Only the axial components add.
Hence, \[ E_{\text{net}}=nE\cos\theta \] and using \[ E=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{l^2}, \qquad l=\frac{R}{\sin\theta}, \] we obtain \[ E_{\text{net}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{nq}{R^2} \sin^2\theta\cos\theta. \] The factor \(n\) is a constant. Therefore, the maximum always occurs when \[ \boxed{\tan\theta=\sqrt2} \] irrespective of the number of charges.
Consequently, the maximum electric field on the symmetry axis always occurs at \[ \boxed{x=\frac{R}{\sqrt2}} \] Taking the limiting case \(n\rightarrow\infty\), the regular polygon becomes a uniformly charged ring.
Hence, the same result immediately applies to the electric field on the axis of a uniformly charged ring.
