Gold Leaf Electroscope

Device to measure presence of charge

Gold Leaf Electroscope - Physical Setup

A Gold Leaf Electroscope (GLE) is a sensitive instrument used to detect the presence of electric charge. It consists of a conducting assembly enclosed inside a transparent glass container to protect it from air currents and moisture.

At the top is a metallic knob, which acts as the point where a charged body is brought in contact with the instrument.

The knob is connected to a conducting metal rod that passes through an insulating sheath or cover.

At the lower end of the rod are attached two extremely thin gold leaves.

The insulating stopper electrically isolates the conducting assembly from the outer case, preventing charge from leaking away.

The delicate gold leaves are free to move and serve as the indicator of the instrument. Any redistribution or transfer of charge to the conducting assembly eventually appears as a change in the separation of these leaves.

The conducting knob, metal rod, and gold leaves together form a single conducting body.
Any charge transferred to or redistributed within the electroscope spreads over this entire conducting assembly.

Working of the Gold Leaf Electroscope

The Gold Leaf Electroscope operates on the principles of charge redistribution and electrostatic repulsion. By observing the separation of the leaves under different conditions, we can deduce the nature of an unknown body.

1. Interaction by Contact (Conduction)

When a body physically touches the metallic knob, charge is actively transferred between the body and the electroscope's conducting assembly until their electrical potentials balance.

Case 1: Initially Neutral (No Separation) + Touch with Charged Rod

If the electroscope is neutral, the leaves hang straight down. When a charged rod touches the knob, charge flows into the assembly.

  • If the rod is negative, excess electrons flow into the electroscope, spreading down to the leaves.
  • If the rod is positive, it pulls free electrons out of the electroscope, leaving the entire assembly with a net positive charge.

In either case, both leaves acquire the same polarity. They repel each other, causing the leaves to diverge.

Case 2: Initially Charged (Existing Separation) + Touch with Charged Rod

Suppose the electroscope has already been given a known charge (e.g., positive), so its leaves are separated. We can use this to determine the polarity of an unknown charged body.

  • Leaves diverge further: The unknown body has the same polarity (positive). It pulls more electrons out of the electroscope, increasing the positive charge and repulsion on the leaves.
  • Leaves move closer together: The unknown body has the opposite polarity (negative). It donates electrons to the electroscope, partially or fully neutralizing the existing positive charge, which reduces the repulsion.

Case 3: Initially Charged (Existing Separation) + Touch with Neutral Conductor

If a completely neutral conducting body touches the knob of the charged electroscope, charge will redistribute between the two conductors. Because the total charge is now shared over a larger combined volume, the net charge remaining on the electroscope decreases.

As a result, the leaves will move closer together.

A decrease in leaf separation occurs if the unknown body is oppositely charged OR if it is a neutral conductor.
Therefore, decreased separation is not a definitive test for opposite polarity.
However, an increase in separation only happens with a like-charged body, making further divergence (repulsion) the surest test of charge.

2. Interaction by Induction (Without Contact)

Physical contact is not necessary for the electroscope to respond. When a body is merely brought near the metallic knob, its electric field influences the free electrons within the electroscope, redistributing them without adding or removing any net charge.

Case 1: Initially Neutral (No Separation) + Bring Charged Rod Near

When a charged rod is brought close to the knob of a neutral electroscope, the electric field polarizes the conducting assembly.

  • A positive rod attracts free electrons up into the knob. This leaves the bottom gold leaves deficient in electrons (net positive).
  • A negative rod repels free electrons down into the leaves, making them net negative.

In both scenarios, the leaves acquire the same polarity and repel each other, causing them to diverge. If the rod is removed, the electrons redistribute evenly, and the leaves collapse back to neutral.

Case 2: Initially Charged (Existing Separation) + Bring Charged Rod Near

Suppose the electroscope is already charged (e.g., positive) and the leaves are diverging. Bringing an unknown charged rod near the knob (without touching) will shift the existing charge distribution:

  • Leaves diverge further: The rod has the same polarity (positive). It attracts the remaining free electrons up toward the knob, leaving the gold leaves even more positively charged, increasing repulsion.
  • Leaves move closer together: The rod has the opposite polarity (negative). It repels free electrons down into the leaves. These electrons neutralize some of the positive charge on the leaves, decreasing the repulsion.

Case 3: Initially Charged (Existing Separation) + Bring Neutral Conductor Near

This is a fascinating, subtle effect. If you bring a neutral conductor near the charged knob (let's say the knob is positive), the knob's electric field will induce a polarization in the neutral conductor. The side of the neutral conductor closest to the knob becomes negative, and the far side becomes positive.

This newly induced negative charge on the near side of the external conductor exerts its own attractive pull, drawing some electrons up from the gold leaves into the knob. As the positive leaves gain some electrons, their net positive charge decreases slightly, causing the leaves to move slightly closer together.

In induction, the electroscope remains electrically neutral as a whole (in Case 1) or maintains its constant net charge (in Cases 2 and 3).
Just like with contact, a neutral body can cause a reduction in leaf separation.
