Charge

The intrinsic property of matter responsible for all electromagnetic interactions.

Concept

If we need to qualitatively understand what is charge in terms of physical experience, then it would be difficult to describe.

For example, we can describe force as a push or a pull and mass of a body can be related to rest inertia.

Even heat and temperature has some physical significance in terms of sensing it from a human perspective.

But charge cannot be described qualitatively with common experiences. However, the phenomenon of static charge transfer is somewhat common.

Electric currents are simply streams of charged particles flowing within wires in response to electric forces.

Lightning observed during storms is a phenomenon of charge transfer.

Electric charge is a fundamental, intrinsic property of matter, much like mass. Just as objects with mass are accelerated by gravitational forces, so electrically charged objects are accelerated by electric forces.

It is the root source of all electromagnetic phenomena.

While mass dictates how matter interacts gravitationally, charge dictates how matter interacts via electric and magnetic fields.

Fundamental particles (electron, proton) possess both mass and charge. The standard SI unit of charge is the Coulomb (\(\text{C}\)).

How does a body acquire charge?

Macroscopic bodies are generally electrically neutral because they contain equal numbers of protons and electrons.

Electrification — the process of a body acquiring a net charge — occurs exclusively through the transfer of electrons. It means that charge transfer is actually transfer of matter only.

Since protons are tightly bound within the atomic nucleus by strong nuclear forces, they do not participate in ordinary charging processes.

Therefore, if a macroscopic body exhibits a net positive charge, it is lacking electrons. If it exhibits a net negative charge, it has an excess of electrons.

Historical Context: Electromagnetic Phenomena

Historically, physicists treated electric and magnetic phenomena as distinct, unrelated subjects.

Ancient civilizations observed that amber, when rubbed with fur, attracted light objects such as feathers (the electric effect).

Separately, they observed that naturally occurring lodestone attracted iron (the magnetic effect).

It was initially believed that these behaviors were eccentricities unique to specific materials.

In 1600, William Gilbert conducted extensive experiments on static amber and lodestones to rigorously distinguish electric attraction from magnetic attraction.

Gilbert's conclusions were right: at the macroscopic level of stationary matter, electricity and magnetism are distinct phenomena.

A rubbed piece of amber does not attract iron, and a magnet does not attract bits of paper.

Subsequent discoveries revealed that electrical phenomena are not restricted to specific solids.

Around 1750, Benjamin Franklin demonstrated that lightning is electrical in nature, and around 1800, Alessandro Volta showed that chemical reactions could produce continuous electric currents.

Electricity was clearly a universal property of nature, later traced to fundamental charged particles present in all matter.

The bridge between electricity and magnetism finally emerged through motion and change.

In 1820, Hans Christian Oersted discovered that an electric current deflects a magnetic compass needle, proving that moving charges produce magnetic effects.

In 1831, Michael Faraday published his discovery of electromagnetic induction, showing that a changing magnetic environment generates an electric current.

These breakthroughs confirmed that electricity and magnetism are deeply linked — just not in the superficial, static way Gilbert had ruled out.

Faraday’s work also gave birth to field theory: the idea that electric and magnetic fields are real physical entities existing in space and time, rather than mere mathematical conveniences.

James Clerk Maxwell later unified electricity and magnetism into a single mathematical theory of electromagnetism.

Subsequently, Albert Einstein's special theory of relativity provided a deeper understanding of this unity by showing that electric and magnetic fields are not independently invariant entities.

Their description depends on the observer's reference frame: what one observer describes in terms of an electric field alone may be described by another observer in terms of both electric and magnetic fields.

Thus, electric and magnetic fields are not two independent entities but different manifestations of a single, unified electromagnetic field.

In Maxwell's theory, electric charge and electric current (the motion of charge) act as sources of electromagnetic fields.

Stationary charges give rise to electric fields, whereas moving charges produce both electric and magnetic fields.

This historical progression provides a roadmap of how electromagnetism developed in physics.
It is known as the second great unification in physics—following Newton's first great unification, which showed that the same gravitational law governs both falling objects on Earth and the orbits of the Moon and planets.
The electromagnetism section is different from the mechanics section in the sense that, in mechanics, we study \(F=ma\), where the net Force \(F\) is the cause and Acceleration \(a\) is the effect.
In electromagnetism, we go one level deeper into the interaction. We study how the Electromagnetic Field \((E,B)\) is the cause that produces electromagnetic Force \(F\) as an effect on a charged particle.
Thus, while mechanics studies how forces produce acceleration, electromagnetism also investigates the physical fields responsible for producing those forces.

Fundamental Properties of Charge

To formulate equations in electrostatics, we must rely on three rigorously established mathematical properties of isolated charge:

1. Quantization of Charge

Charge does not exist in continuous amounts. It exists only in discrete packets. The total charge \(q\) on a body is always an integral multiple of the fundamental elementary charge \(e\) (the charge of a single electron or proton).

Mathematically, this is expressed as:

\[q = \pm ne\]

where \(n\) is an integer (\(1, 2, 3, \dots\)) and \(e \approx 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}\).

2. Conservation of Charge

For any isolated system, the total algebraic sum of charges remains absolutely constant over time. Charge can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be transferred from one body to another. Even in high-energy nuclear reactions where mass is converted to energy, the net charge before and after the reaction remains identical.

3. Charge is associated with mass

As charge is a property of matter like mass, existence of charge implies existence of matter implies existence of mass. Charge transfer implies mass transfer. A negatively charged macroscopic body is slightly heavier than the same neutral body - because excess charge implies excess mass.

4. Charge is bipolar

Two different types of charges exist. Benjamin Franklin named them "positive" and "negative". They are two different types. It is not same as sub-zero temperature.

Conceptual Question

The charge on a proton is \( +1.6 \times 10^{-19} \, \text{C} \) and that on an electron is \( -1.6 \times 10^{-19} \, \text{C} \). Does this mean the electron has \( 3.2 \times 10^{-19} \, \text{C} \) less charge than the proton?

Solution

No. This statement is physically incorrect because it treats polarity as a simple algebraic comparison.

1. Physical Meaning of Sign (Polarity)
  • The positive (+) and negative (−) signs indicate two fundamentally different types of a physical property.
  • An electron does not have "less charge" than a proton; it has the same magnitude but opposite polarity.
  • The only meaningful comparison is magnitude: \( e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \, \text{C} \).
2. Contrast with Temperature
  • Temperature does not have polarity. \( +2^\circ \text{C} \) and \( -2^\circ \text{C} \) are simply two points on a continuous scale.
  • Thus, saying \( -2^\circ \text{C} \) is \( 4^\circ \text{C} \) less than \( +2^\circ \text{C} \) is meaningful.

Charge differs because its sign represents polarity, not position on a continuous scalar scale.

3. When "More" or "Less" Is Valid
  • Same polarity: +5 C and +4 C → the first has 1 C more positive charge.
  • Same polarity: −5 C and −4 C → the first has 1 C more negative charge (greater magnitude of that polarity).
  • Comparison across opposite polarities is physically meaningless and in that case only magnitudes can be compared.

Additivity vs Comparison

Charge is additive:

\( (+5 \, \text{C}) + (-7 \, \text{C}) = -2 \, \text{C} \)

This represents a netting process between opposite polarities.

However, algebraic comparison (e.g., −5 < +4) does not correspond to a physically meaningful comparison between different polarities.

5. Relativistic Invariance

Unlike mass, which increases as an object's velocity approaches the speed of light, the charge of a particle is entirely independent of its speed or the frame of reference of the observer. A charge \(q\) measured at rest will measure exactly as \(q\) when moving at any velocity \(v\).

How was charge discovered?

Historically, charge was discovered primarily through its observable behavior of attraction and repulsion. Scientists studied phenomena such as charge transfer, charge induction and the attraction and repulsion between charged bodies.

Macroscopic bodies were examined experimentally. It was found that charge can be transferred by rubbing two bodies against each other. Metallic conducting bodies were a special case — charge transfer between metals was possible by conduction.

In induction, charge is not transferred but redistributed within a macroscopic body. As a result, even a body that is neutral as a whole may attract a charged body. Therefore, repulsion is the surest test of charging.

Question 1

Five Styrofoam balls A, B, C, D, and E are used in an experiment. The following observations are made:

  1. Ball A repels C and attracts B.
  2. Ball D attracts B and has no effect on E.
  3. A negatively charged rod attracts both A and E.

Note: An electrically neutral Styrofoam ball is very sensitive to charge induction and gets attracted considerably when placed near a charged body.

What are the charges, if any, on each ball?

Option A B C D E
(A) + - + 0 +
(B) + - + + 0
(C) + - + 0 0
(D) - + - - 0

Solution

We deduce the charge of each ball step by step using the following principles:

  • Repulsion is the surest test of charge. It occurs only between objects with the same type of charge.
  • Attraction can occur either:
    1. Between oppositely charged objects.
    2. Between a charged object and a neutral object (due to induction).

From Observation (i)

"Ball A repels C"

  • Repulsion implies both A and C are charged and have the same sign.

"Ball A attracts B"

  • B is either oppositely charged to A or neutral.

From Observation (ii)

“Ball D has no effect on E”

  • No effect implies both D and E are neutral.

"Ball D attracts B"

  • Since D is neutral, B must be charged.
  • Because A and C have the same charge, B must have the opposite charge.

From Observation (iii)

“A negatively charged rod attracts A”

  • A must be positively charged (since like charges repel).
  • Therefore, C is also positive, and B is negative.

"A negatively charged rod attracts E"

  • This is consistent with E being neutral (attraction by induction).

Final Charges

  • A: Positive (+)
  • B: Negative (−)
  • C: Positive (+)
  • D: Neutral (0)
  • E: Neutral (0)

This corresponds to option (C): + − + 0 0.
