Charge

The intrinsic property of matter responsible for all electromagnetic interactions.

What is Charge?

Electric charge is a fundamental, intrinsic property of matter, much like mass. It is the root source of all electromagnetic phenomena. While mass dictates how matter interacts gravitationally, charge dictates how matter interacts via electric and magnetic fields.

Fundamental particles (electron, proton) possess both mass and charge. The standard SI unit of charge is the Coulomb (\(\text{C}\)).

How does a body acquire charge?

Macroscopic bodies are generally electrically neutral because they contain equal numbers of protons and electrons. Electrification—the process of a body acquiring a net charge—occurs exclusively through the transfer of electrons.

Since protons are tightly bound within the atomic nucleus by strong nuclear forces, they do not participate in ordinary charging processes. Therefore, if a macroscopic body exhibits a net positive charge, it is lacking electrons. If it exhibits a net negative charge, it has an excess of electrons.

Historical Context: Electromagnetic Phenomena

Historically, physicists treated electric and magnetic phenomena as distinct, unrelated subjects. Ancient civilizations observed that amber, when rubbed with fur, attracted light objects like feathers (electric effect). Separately, they observed that naturally occurring lodestone attracted iron (magnetic effect). It was initially believed that these phenomena were uniquely dependent on the specific materials involved.

Later discoveries found out that its not due to specific materials, but due to fundamental particles present in all materials. The specific materials behave specifically because of their specific configuration / behaviour.

Even later discoveries unified these fields, revealing that both phenomena are dictated by charge. Stationary charges produce electric fields, whereas moving charges produce magnetic fields. Charge is the one fundamental unifying entity that is the source of one electromagnetic field.

Fundamental Properties of Charge

To formulate equations in electrostatics, we must rely on three rigorously established mathematical properties of isolated charge:

1. Quantization of Charge

Charge does not exist in continuous amounts. It exists only in discrete packets. The total charge \(q\) on a body is always an integral multiple of the fundamental elementary charge \(e\) (the charge of a single electron or proton).

Mathematically, this is expressed as:

\[q = \pm ne\]

where \(n\) is an integer (\(1, 2, 3, \dots\)) and \(e \approx 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}\).

2. Conservation of Charge

For any isolated system, the total algebraic sum of charges remains absolutely constant over time. Charge can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be transferred from one body to another. Even in high-energy nuclear reactions where mass is converted to energy, the net charge before and after the reaction remains identical.

3. Charge is associated with mass

As charge is a property of matter like mass, existence of charge implies existence of matter implies existence of mass. Charge transfer implies mass transfer. A negatively charged macroscopic body is slightly heavier than the same neutral body - because excess charge implies excess mass.

4. Charge is bipolar

Two different types of charges exist. Benjamin Franklin named them "positive" and "negative". They are two different types. It is not same as sub-zero temperature.

Conceptual Question

The charge on a proton is \( +1.6 \times 10^{-19} \, \text{C} \) and that on an electron is \( -1.6 \times 10^{-19} \, \text{C} \). Does this mean the electron has \( 3.2 \times 10^{-19} \, \text{C} \) less charge than the proton?

Solution

No. This statement is physically incorrect because it treats polarity as a simple algebraic comparison.

1. Physical Meaning of Sign (Polarity)
  • The positive (+) and negative (−) signs indicate two fundamentally different types of a physical property.
  • An electron does not have "less charge" than a proton; it has the same magnitude but opposite polarity.
  • The only meaningful comparison is magnitude: \( e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \, \text{C} \).
2. Contrast with Temperature
  • Temperature does not have polarity. \( +2^\circ \text{C} \) and \( -2^\circ \text{C} \) are simply two points on a continuous scale.
  • Thus, saying \( -2^\circ \text{C} \) is \( 4^\circ \text{C} \) less than \( +2^\circ \text{C} \) is meaningful.

Charge differs because its sign represents polarity, not position on a continuous scalar scale.

3. When "More" or "Less" Is Valid
  • Same polarity: +5 C and +4 C → the first has 1 C more positive charge.
  • Same polarity: −5 C and −4 C → the first has 1 C more negative charge (greater magnitude of that polarity).
  • Comparison across opposite polarities is physically meaningless.

Additivity vs Comparison

Charge is additive:

\( (+5 \, \text{C}) + (-7 \, \text{C}) = -2 \, \text{C} \)

This represents a netting process between opposite polarities.

However, algebraic comparison (e.g., −5 < +4) does not correspond to a physically meaningful comparison between different polarities.

5. Relativistic Invariance

Unlike mass, which increases as an object's velocity approaches the speed of light, the charge of a particle is entirely independent of its speed or the frame of reference of the observer. A charge \(q\) measured at rest will measure exactly as \(q\) when moving at any velocity \(v\).

How was charge discovered?

Historically, charge was discovered primarily through its observable behavior of attraction and repulsion. Scientists studied phenomena such as charge transfer, charge induction and the attraction and repulsion between charged bodies.

Macroscopic bodies were examined experimentally. It was found that charge can be transferred by rubbing two bodies against each other. Metallic conducting bodies were a special case — charge transfer between metals was possible by conduction.

In induction, charge is not transferred but redistributed within a macroscopic body. As a result, even a body that is neutral as a whole may attract a charged body. Therefore, repulsion is the surest test of charging.

Question 1

Five Styrofoam balls A, B, C, D, and E are used in an experiment. The following observations are made:

  1. Ball A repels C and attracts B.
  2. Ball D attracts B and has no effect on E.
  3. A negatively charged rod attracts both A and E.

Note: An electrically neutral Styrofoam ball is very sensitive to charge induction and gets attracted considerably when placed near a charged body.

What are the charges, if any, on each ball?

Option A B C D E
(A) + - + 0 +
(B) + - + + 0
(C) + - + 0 0
(D) - + - - 0

Solution

We deduce the charge of each ball step by step using the following principles:

  • Repulsion is the surest test of charge. It occurs only between objects with the same type of charge.
  • Attraction can occur either:
    1. Between oppositely charged objects.
    2. Between a charged object and a neutral object (due to induction).

From Observation (i)

"Ball A repels C"

  • Repulsion implies both A and C are charged and have the same sign.

"Ball A attracts B"

  • B is either oppositely charged to A or neutral.

From Observation (ii)

“Ball D has no effect on E”

  • No effect implies both D and E are neutral.

"Ball D attracts B"

  • Since D is neutral, B must be charged.
  • Because A and C have the same charge, B must have the opposite charge.

From Observation (iii)

“A negatively charged rod attracts A”

  • A must be positively charged (since like charges repel).
  • Therefore, C is also positive, and B is negative.

"A negatively charged rod attracts E"

  • This is consistent with E being neutral (attraction by induction).

Final Charges

  • A: Positive (+)
  • B: Negative (−)
  • C: Positive (+)
  • D: Neutral (0)
  • E: Neutral (0)

This corresponds to option (C): + − + 0 0.
