Series and Parallel Combinations

Structural reduction of linear networks into equivalent black-box parameters.

The Concept of Equivalent Resistance

The core principle of circuit reduction is replacement without external alteration. If a complex network of resistors is placed inside a "black box" with two emerging terminals, the equivalent resistance \( R_{\text{eq}} \) is the value of a single resistor that could replace the entire box such that for the same applied potential difference \( V \), the exact same total current \( I \) is drawn from the source.

Series Combination

Resistors are in series if they are connected end-to-end with no branching nodes between them. Because charge cannot accumulate at any point in a steady-state conductor, the same current \( I \) flows through every resistor.

Derivation

The total potential drop must equal the sum of individual drops (Conservation of Energy):

\[V_{\text{total}} = V_1 + V_2 + V_3 + \cdots\]

Applying Ohm's Law (\( V = IR \)) to each:

\[I R_{\text{eq}} = IR_1 + IR_2 + IR_3 + \cdots\] \[\boxed{R_{\text{eq}} = R_1 + R_2 + R_3 + \cdots}\]
  • \( R_{\text{eq}} \) is always greater than the largest individual resistance.
  • Voltage divides in proportion to resistance: \( V_i = V \cdot R_i/R_{\text{eq}} \).
  • The series combination acts as a voltage divider.
If one element in a series chain breaks (open circuits), the entire current stops — the chain is interrupted. This is why old-style Christmas lights failed completely when one bulb burned out.

Parallel Combination

Resistors are in parallel if all their starting ends share one common node and all their terminating ends share another. All resistors therefore have the same potential difference \( V \) across them.

Derivation

Total current entering the junction equals the sum of branch currents (Conservation of Charge):

\[I_{\text{total}} = I_1 + I_2 + I_3 + \cdots\]

Applying Ohm's Law (\( I = V/R \)) to each branch:

\[\frac{V}{R_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{V}{R_1} + \frac{V}{R_2} + \frac{V}{R_3} + \cdots\] \[\boxed{\frac{1}{R_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3} + \cdots}\]
  • \( R_{\text{eq}} \) is always less than the smallest individual resistance.
  • Current divides inversely with resistance: \( I_i = I \cdot R_{\text{eq}}/R_i \).
  • For two resistors: \( R_{\text{eq}} = R_1 R_2/(R_1 + R_2) \).
  • The parallel combination acts as a current divider.
If one branch in a parallel circuit breaks, the other branches are unaffected — they still see the same voltage. Household appliances are wired in parallel so each device operates at 230 V and can be switched independently.

Equivalent R \( R_{\text{eq}} = \sum R_i \) \( 1/R_{\text{eq}} = \sum 1/R_i \)
\( R_{\text{eq}} \) vs. individual Greater than largest Less than smallest
Role Voltage divider Current divider
Failure effect All branches fail Other branches OK

n Identical Resistors

A useful special case worth memorising:

  • Series: \( R_{\text{eq}} = nR \) — resistance multiplied by \( n \).
  • Parallel: \( R_{\text{eq}} = R/n \) — resistance divided by \( n \).

The ratio of series to parallel equivalent: \( R_{\text{series}}/R_{\text{parallel}} = n^2 \). For \( n = 3 \) identical resistors, this ratio is 9.
