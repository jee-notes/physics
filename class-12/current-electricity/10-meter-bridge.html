Meter Bridge and Potentiometer

Two precision instruments based on the Wheatstone bridge and Ohm's law — for accurate measurement of resistance and EMF.

The Meter Bridge

The meter bridge (also called a slide-wire bridge) is a practical implementation of the Wheatstone bridge. It uses a 1-metre uniform resistance wire stretched over a metre scale as two of the four bridge arms.

Construction

  • A 1 m long wire of uniform resistance per unit length is fixed between two thick copper strips at A and C.
  • Unknown resistance \( X \) is placed in the left gap; known resistance \( R \) in the right gap.
  • A jockey (sliding contact) D is moved along the wire to find the null point — where the galvanometer reads zero.
  • Let the null point be at distance \( l \) cm from end A. The wire from A to D has resistance \( \propto l \); from D to C \( \propto (100-l) \).

Balance Condition

At balance, using the Wheatstone bridge condition \( P/Q = X/R \):

\[ \frac{\text{Resistance of AD}}{\text{Resistance of DC}} = \frac{X}{R} \] \[ \frac{l}{100 - l} = \frac{X}{R} \] \[ \boxed{X = R \cdot \frac{l}{100 - l}} \]
The meter bridge is a special case of the Wheatstone bridge where the ratio arms \( P \) and \( Q \) are portions of a uniform wire. Its advantage over a general Wheatstone bridge: no separate ratio box is needed — the ratio \( l/(100-l) \) is read directly off the metre scale.

Precautions and Error Sources

  • The null point should ideally lie between 30 cm and 70 cm for accuracy — extreme positions reduce sensitivity.
  • The wire must be uniform; end resistances (at thick copper junctions) introduce systematic errors.
  • Connections should be tight; loose contacts introduce additional resistance.
  • The jockey should not be dragged along the wire — this causes wear, making the wire non-uniform.

The Potentiometer

A potentiometer is a versatile instrument that measures EMF, potential difference, and internal resistance with very high accuracy — without drawing any current from the circuit being measured (at null point).

Principle

A long uniform wire AB of length \( L \) carries a steady current \( I \) from a driver cell. The potential falls uniformly along the wire. If the wire has resistance per unit length \( r_0 \), then potential drop per unit length (potential gradient):

\[ k = \frac{V_{AB}}{L} = I \cdot r_0 \quad \text{(V/m)} \]

The potential at a point distance \( l \) from A is \( V = kl \). By sliding a jockey along the wire, we can balance this potential against an unknown EMF.

Measuring EMF of a Cell

Connect the unknown cell (EMF \( \mathcal{E} \)) between A and the jockey through a galvanometer. Move the jockey until the galvanometer reads zero (null point at length \( l \)). At this point, no current flows through the cell — it is in a genuine open-circuit condition — so:

\[ \mathcal{E} = kl \]

To compare two EMFs \( \mathcal{E}_1 \) and \mathcal{E}_2 \), find their respective null lengths \( l_1 \) and \( l_2 \):

\[ \frac{\mathcal{E}_1}{\mathcal{E}_2} = \frac{l_1}{l_2} \]

Measuring Internal Resistance of a Cell

1. Find null length \( l_1 \) with the external resistance \( R \) disconnected (open circuit). So \( \mathcal{E} = kl_1 \).

2. Connect \( R \) across the cell. Find new null length \( l_2 \). Terminal voltage \( V = kl_2 \).

Since \( \mathcal{E} = I(R + r) \) and \( V = IR \):

\[ \frac{\mathcal{E}}{V} = \frac{l_1}{l_2} = \frac{R + r}{R} \] \[ \boxed{r = R\left(\frac{l_1 - l_2}{l_2}\right)} \]
The key advantage of the potentiometer over a voltmeter: at the null point, no current is drawn from the cell being measured. This means we measure the true EMF (open-circuit voltage), not the terminal voltage reduced by internal resistance. A voltmeter always draws some current, so it always measures a slightly low value.

Meter Bridge vs. Potentiometer — Summary

Feature Meter Bridge Potentiometer
Based on Wheatstone bridge Uniform potential gradient
Measures Unknown resistance EMF, internal resistance, V
Current from source? Yes (during measurement) No (at null point)
Accuracy Good Very high (null method)
Key formula \( X = R\cdot l/(100-l) \) \( \mathcal{E} = kl \)
