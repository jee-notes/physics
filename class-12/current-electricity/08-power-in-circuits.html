Power in Electrical Circuits

How electrical energy is transferred, dissipated, and delivered — and conditions for maximum power.

Electric Power — Definition

Power is the rate at which energy is transferred. In an electrical circuit, when a current \( I \) flows through a potential difference \( V \), the rate of energy transfer is:

\[ P = \frac{dW}{dt} = \frac{dq \cdot V}{dt} = V \cdot \frac{dq}{dt} = VI \] \[ \boxed{P = VI = I^2 R = \frac{V^2}{R}} \]

The SI unit of power is the Watt (W): \( 1 \text{ W} = 1 \text{ J/s} \). All three forms are equivalent — use whichever form the problem gives more directly.

Joule Heating

When current flows through a resistor, electrons undergo repeated collisions with the lattice ions. Each collision converts kinetic energy of the directed electron drift into random thermal vibration of the lattice — heat. This is Joule heating (also called resistive heating or Ohmic heating).

Heat generated in a resistor \( R \) carrying current \( I \) for time \( t \):

\[ H = I^2 R t \]

This is Joule's Law. The heat generated is proportional to the square of the current, the resistance, and the time.

The \( I^2 \) dependence is why transmission lines carry power at high voltage and low current. Doubling the current would quadruple the heating losses in the line for the same resistance. This is the primary motivation for high-voltage AC power transmission.

Power Delivered by a Source (EMF)

A cell with EMF \( \mathcal{E} \) and internal resistance \( r \), connected to an external resistance \( R \):

\[ I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R + r} \]

The total power delivered by the EMF source:

\[ P_{\text{source}} = \mathcal{E} I = \frac{\mathcal{E}^2}{R + r} \]

This power splits into two parts:

\[ P_{\text{external}} = I^2 R \quad \text{(useful power — delivered to load)} \] \[ P_{\text{internal}} = I^2 r \quad \text{(wasted as heat in the cell's internal resistance)} \]

The efficiency of the cell is:

\[ \eta = \frac{P_{\text{external}}}{P_{\text{source}}} = \frac{IR}{\mathcal{E}} = \frac{V_{\text{terminal}}}{\mathcal{E}} = \frac{R}{R+r} \]

Maximum Power Transfer Theorem

Given a source of EMF \( \mathcal{E} \) and internal resistance \( r \), what value of external resistance \( R \) draws maximum power?

\[ P_{\text{external}} = I^2 R = \frac{\mathcal{E}^2 R}{(R + r)^2} \]

Differentiating with respect to \( R \) and setting \( dP/dR = 0 \):

\[ \frac{dP}{dR} = \mathcal{E}^2 \cdot \frac{(R+r)^2 - 2R(R+r)}{(R+r)^4} = 0 \] \[ (R+r) - 2R = 0 \implies \boxed{R = r} \]

Maximum power is transferred to the external resistance when \( R = r \) — the load resistance equals the source's internal resistance.

\[ P_{\text{max}} = \frac{\mathcal{E}^2}{4r} \quad \text{(when } R = r \text{)} \]
At maximum power transfer (\( R = r \)), the efficiency is exactly 50% — half the power is wasted in internal resistance. This trade-off exists in all real sources. Power delivery and efficiency have opposite optimal conditions: maximum efficiency at \( R \gg r \), maximum power at \( R = r \).

Power in Series and Parallel Circuits

Series Combination

Same current \( I \) through each resistor. Power dissipated in each:

\[ P_i = I^2 R_i \propto R_i \]

The highest resistance in a series circuit dissipates the most power.

Parallel Combination

Same voltage \( V \) across each resistor. Power dissipated in each:

\[ P_i = \frac{V^2}{R_i} \propto \frac{1}{R_i} \]

The lowest resistance in a parallel circuit dissipates the most power.

Practical Implication

When identical bulbs are connected in series, they share the total voltage equally and each gets less than rated power. In parallel, each gets full voltage — full rated power. This is why parallel wiring is used for home lighting; series wiring would make all lights dim.

Electrical Energy and the kWh

Commercial electrical energy is measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh):

\[ 1 \text{ kWh} = 1000 \text{ W} \times 3600 \text{ s} = 3.6 \times 10^6 \text{ J} \]

A 100 W bulb running for 10 hours consumes \( 100 \times 10 = 1000 \text{ Wh} = 1 \text{ kWh} \) of electrical energy — one "unit" on your electricity bill.
