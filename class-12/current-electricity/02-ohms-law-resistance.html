Ohm's Law and Resistance

The steady-state solution of Newton's law under momentum relaxation.

The Need for Ohm's Law: Failure of Pure Kinematics

Consider an electron in vacuum subjected to a constant electric field \(\vec{E}\).

It experiences a force:

\[ \vec{F} = -e\vec{E} \]

By Newton's Second Law:

\[ \vec{a} = -\frac{e\vec{E}}{m} \]

If this were the whole story, kinematics predicts:

\[ v = u + at \]

Velocity would increase without bound as long as the field exists. Consequently, current would grow indefinitely.

The Contradiction: In real conductors, current does not grow indefinitely. After a very short transient time (~10-14 s in metals), it settles to a steady constant value.

Pure Newtonian acceleration fails because a conductor is not vacuum. Electrons constantly collide with vibrating lattice ions. These collisions randomize momentum and prevent indefinite acceleration.

The system reaches a steady state when:

\[ \langle m a \rangle = 0 \]

This does not mean the electric force vanishes. It means the average acceleration becomes zero because momentum gained from the field is continuously lost in collisions.

Ohm’s law is the mathematical model describing this steady-state balance.

Microscopic Picture: Momentum Relaxation

The motion of an electron inside a conductor can be modeled as:

\[ m \frac{dv}{dt} = -eE - \frac{m v}{\tau} \]

The second term represents momentum relaxation due to collisions. It plays the role of a friction-like force.

In steady state, \( dv/dt = 0 \), giving:

\[ v_d = \frac{e\tau}{m} E \]

This is the drift velocity: a constant average velocity proportional to the applied field.

Current density is:

\[ J = ne v_d \]

Substituting:

\[ J = \left(\frac{ne^2 \tau}{m}\right) E \]

The grouped quantity is defined as Conductivity (\(\sigma\)):

\[ \sigma = \frac{ne^2 \tau}{m} \]

Microscopic Ohm's Law

\[ \vec{J} = \sigma \vec{E} \]

Resistivity is defined as \( \rho = 1/\sigma \).

This is the fundamental transport law: Local current density is proportional to local electric field.

Macroscopic Form (Circuit Level)

We measure current (\(I\)) and potential difference (\(V\)), not \(J\) and \(E\).

For a uniform cylindrical conductor of length \(L\) and cross-sectional area \(A\):

  • \(E = V/L\)
  • \(J = I/A\)

Substituting into microscopic law:

\[ \frac{I}{A} = \sigma \frac{V}{L} \] \[ V = I \left(\frac{L}{\sigma A}\right) \]

Defining:

\[ R = \rho \frac{L}{A} \]

Macroscopic Ohm's Law

\[ V = IR \]

This allows circuit-level analysis without tracking microscopic collisions.

The Structural Meaning of Resistance

Resistance is not a physical blockade. If it were, charge would accumulate and violate charge conservation.

Resistance quantifies the rate at which electrical potential energy is irreversibly converted into thermal energy (Joule heating). It is the electrical analogue of mechanical friction. Just like how kinetic friction does not physically blockade motion but oppose motion. Just like a mechanical body that is moving on a rough surface with constant velocity, needs a constant force to be applied against kinetic friction and it doesn't accelerate.

Current is conserved. Energy is dissipated.

Ohm’s law is therefore not a fundamental force law. It is an emergent linear-response transport law.

Domain of Validity

Ohm’s law holds in the linear response regime, where:

  • Drift velocity is much smaller than thermal velocity.
  • Temperature changes are negligible.
  • Relaxation time \(\tau\) remains constant.

At very high electric fields, heating alters \(\tau\), and conduction becomes nonlinear. In such regimes, \(J \neq \sigma E\).

Classification of Variables in Drift Model

1. Material Properties

  • \(n\) — Charge carrier density
  • \(\tau\) — Relaxation time

2. Geometric Properties

  • \(A\) — Cross-sectional area
  • \(L\) — Length

3. Universal Constants

  • \(e\) — Elementary charge
  • \(m\) — Electron mass

4. Dynamic Variables

  • \(E\) — Electric field
  • \(v_d\) — Drift velocity
  • \(I\) — Current
