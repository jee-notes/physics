Electric Current

The macroscopic flow of charge and its microscopic mechanical origin.

The Definition of Current: Motion vs. Flow

Electric current is not merely the presence of moving charges; it is the macroscopic flow of charge. Flow is a mathematical concept that requires a defined boundary — a surface or "gate" — through which the entity passes. A conductor already has charges (electrons) in random thermal motion. But still it does not imply that conductor has non-zero current flowing through it.

If a net charge \(\Delta Q\) crosses a specific cross-sectional area in time \(\Delta t\), the average current is defined as:

\[I_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t}\]

For instantaneous flow, we take the limit as \(\Delta t \to 0\):

\[i = \frac{dQ}{dt}\]
The SI unit of current is the Ampere (\(\text{A}\)), where \(1 \text{ A} = 1 \text{ C/s}\). By convention, the direction of macroscopic current is taken as the direction of flow of positive charges, which is opposite to the actual physical flow of electrons.

Current Density

Current is a scalar quantity defined for a given cross-section. To describe the flow locally at a point inside the conductor, we introduce a vector field called current density, denoted by \( \vec{J} \).

For a small surface element \( d\vec{A} \), the infinitesimal current flowing through it is:

\[ di = \vec{J} \cdot d\vec{A} \]

Integrating over the entire cross-section gives the total current:

\[ I = \int \vec{J} \cdot d\vec{A} \]
Current is therefore the flux of current density through a surface — completely analogous to electric flux in electrostatics.

Frame Invariance of Current

A consequence of defining current with respect to a physical "gate" (the cross-sectional area of the conductor) is that steady state current flowing in conductor is frame-invariant quantity.

Consider an observer moving at a constant velocity parallel to a current-carrying wire. To the moving observer, the velocity of the flowing electrons changes. However, the velocity of flowing electrons wrt the area remains same as it is wrt to the wire (conductor) itself.

Current is determined by the relative velocity between the charge carriers and the physical cross-section of the wire. Since Galilean transformations shift both velocities equally, the relative velocity remains constant. Therefore, the rate of charge crossing the gate (\(dQ/dt\)) is invariant regardless of the observer's inertial frame.

Ways in Which Current Can Arise

Electric current can be produced in several physical situations. It is not restricted to metallic wires.

When a metal is heated to sufficiently high temperatures, it emits electrons. These emitted electrons travel through space. If we consider any surface perpendicular to their velocity, charge crosses that surface — and therefore a current exists through it.

In electrolytic solutions, both positive and negative ions move. When an external electric field is applied, positive ions drift along the field while negative ions drift opposite to it. Both motions contribute to a net current in the direction of the electric field. This is important to note because current is due to flow of net charge and not 1 type of charge alone. If positive (+) negative (-) both are moving in the same direction, then too net current will be a subtraction. But if they are moving in opposite directions, then net charge flowing through a cross-section adds up.

When a battery is connected across a capacitor, charges move through the connecting wires toward the capacitor plates. As long as charge continues to cross any cross-section of the wire, there is a current in the circuit.

Current is a universal transport phenomenon — it occurs whenever charge crosses a defined surface, regardless of the physical medium.

In this chapter, our focus will be on electric current in conductors when an electric field is established inside them.

Solved Example

Question 1

An electron beam has a circular aperture of area \(1.0 \, \text{mm}^2\). A total of \(6.0 \times 10^{16}\) electrons pass through any perpendicular cross-section per second. Find:

  • (a) The electric current in the beam
  • (b) The current density in the beam

Solution

(a) Current

Total charge crossing per second:

\[ q = ne = (6.0 \times 10^{16})(1.6 \times 10^{-19}) \, \text{C} \] \[ q = 9.6 \times 10^{-3} \, \text{C} \] \[ I = \frac{q}{t} = 9.6 \times 10^{-3} \, \text{A} \]

Since electrons carry negative charge, the conventional current direction is opposite to the direction of motion of the electron beam.

(b) Current Density

Convert area:

\[ 1.0 \, \text{mm}^2 = 1.0 \times 10^{-6} \, \text{m}^2 \] \[ J = \frac{I}{A} = \frac{9.6 \times 10^{-3}}{1.0 \times 10^{-6}} \] \[ J = 9.6 \times 10^{3} \, \text{A m}^{-2} \]
