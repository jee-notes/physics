Electric Current

The macroscopic flow of charge and its microscopic mechanical origin.

The Definition of Current: Motion v/s Flow

Electric current is not merely the presence of moving charges; it is the macroscopic flow of charge through a cross-sectional area. "Flow" is a mathematical concept that requires a defined boundary — a surface or "gate" — through which the flowing entity crosses. For example, free electrons in a conductor at any temperature above absolute zero are in constant thermal motion. However, because this motion is perfectly random, the net charge crossing any plane is zero, meaning the macroscopic current is zero.

If a net charge \(\Delta Q\) crosses a specific cross-sectional area in time \(\Delta t\), the average current is defined as:

\[I_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t}\]

For instantaneous flow, we take the limit as \(\Delta t \to 0\):

\[i = \frac{dQ}{dt}\]
The SI unit of current is the Ampere (\(\text{A}\)), where \(1 \text{ A} = 1 \text{ C/s}\). By convention, the direction of macroscopic current is taken as the direction of flow of positive charges, which is opposite to the actual physical flow of electrons.

Current Density

Current is a scalar quantity defined for a given cross-section. To describe the flow locally (at a point) inside the conductor, we introduce a vector field called current density, denoted by \( \vec{J} \).

For a small surface element \( d\vec{A} \), the infinitesimal current flowing through it is:

\[ di = \vec{J} \cdot d\vec{A} \]

Integrating over the entire cross-section gives the total current:

\[ I = \int \vec{J} \cdot d\vec{A} \]

Current is therefore the flux of current density through a surface — completely analogous to electric flux in electrostatics.

If any scalar physical quantity \(PQ\) is defined as: \(PQ = \int \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A}\), then \(PQ\) is called flux of \(\vec{E}\). Spatially, \(\vec{E}\) is defined for a point and \(PQ\) is defined for Area.

Frame Invariance of Current

The value of steady current in a conductor does not depend on whether the conductor itself is at rest or moving.

Consider a current-carrying wire placed on the ground. Now imagine the same wire inside a car moving with constant velocity. The car may move in the same direction as the drift velocity of electrons or in the opposite direction. In either case, the measured current in the wire remains the same.

Why does this happen?

Current is defined as the rate at which charge crosses a specific cross-sectional area of the conductor. Both the charge carriers and the cross-section belong to the same physical object — the conductor. If the entire conductor moves, the cross-section moves along with the charges.

Current depends on the relative motion between charge carriers and the cross-section of the conductor. Under a Galilean shift (uniform change of reference frame), both velocities change by the same amount. Their relative velocity remains unchanged. Hence the rate of charge crossing the cross-section (\( dQ/dt \)) remains the same.

Therefore, steady current in a conductor is invariant under uniform translational motion of the entire conductor.

Ways in Which Current Can Arise

Electric current is introduced as a physical quantity whenever charge in motion results in transport across a specified surface. It is not tied to a particular material or device. The defining condition is simple: charge must cross a chosen boundary.

1. Electron Emission into Free Space

When a metal is heated to sufficiently high temperatures, it emits electrons (thermionic emission). These electrons move through space.

If we imagine any surface placed perpendicular to their direction of motion, electrons cross that surface. Since charge is transported across a defined area, a current exists through that surface.

2. Electrolytic Conduction

In electrolytic solutions, both positive and negative ions are mobile. When an external electric field is applied:

  • Positive ions drift in the direction of the field.
  • Negative ions drift opposite to the field.

Current depends on the net charge transported across a cross-section, not on the motion of a single type of charge.

If positive and negative charges move in opposite directions, their contributions to current add (since both transport net charge across the same surface in the same conventional direction).

If both types of charge move in the same direction, their contributions subtract according to their sign.

3. Charging a Capacitor

When a battery is connected across a capacitor, charges move through the connecting wires toward the plates.

As long as charge crosses any cross-section of the wire, current exists in that wire — even though charge does not pass through the dielectric gap between the plates. This demonstrates that transient current can exist even in the absence of a closed physical loop.

Current is a transport quantity. It arises whenever moving charge crosses a defined surface — irrespective of whether the medium is vacuum, electrolyte, or solid conductor.

In this chapter, we will focus on current in solid conductors when an electric field is established within them.

4. Lightning (Atmospheric Discharge)

During a thunderstorm, large amounts of charge accumulate in clouds due to charge separation processes. When the electric field between cloud and ground (or between clouds) becomes sufficiently strong, a rapid discharge occurs.

During this discharge, an enormous amount of charge crosses a surface in a very short time. Since current is defined as

\[ I = \frac{dQ}{dt}, \]

lightning is simply a very large electric current — typically of the order of kiloamperes to megaamperes.

5. Electric Current in the Nervous System

Electric current also exists inside living organisms. In the human nervous system, signals propagate due to controlled motion of ions across neuronal membranes.

When ions cross a membrane surface, charge is transported across that boundary. Therefore, a current exists. These biological currents are typically of the order of microamperes. Historically, Luigi Galvani's observation of these bioelectric currents in frog muscles directly inspired Alessandro Volta to invent the first electrochemical cell.

Solved Example

Question 1

An electron beam has a circular aperture of area \(1.0 \, \text{mm}^2\). A total of \(6.0 \times 10^{16}\) electrons pass through any perpendicular cross-section per second. Find:

  • (a) The electric current in the beam
  • (b) The current density in the beam

Solution

(a) Current

Total charge crossing per second:

\[ q = ne = (6.0 \times 10^{16})(1.6 \times 10^{-19}) \, \text{C} \] \[ q = 9.6 \times 10^{-3} \, \text{C} \] \[ I = \frac{q}{t} = 9.6 \times 10^{-3} \, \text{A} \]

Since electrons carry negative charge, the conventional current direction is opposite to the direction of motion of the electron beam.

(b) Current Density

Convert area:

\[ 1.0 \, \text{mm}^2 = 1.0 \times 10^{-6} \, \text{m}^2 \] \[ J = \frac{I}{A} = \frac{9.6 \times 10^{-3}}{1.0 \times 10^{-6}} \] \[ J = 9.6 \times 10^{3} \, \text{A m}^{-2} \]
