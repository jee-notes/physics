Electric Current

The macroscopic flow rate of charge through a cross-sectional area.

The Definition of Current: Motion vs Flow

Electric current is not merely the presence of moving charges; it is the macroscopic flow rate of electric charge through a specified cross-sectional area.

The word flow has a precise physical meaning. Flow always requires a well-defined boundary—a surface or an imaginary gate—across which the physical quantity is transported. Such motion is called transport. In other words, the charge must move from one side of the surface to the other. Mere motion is therefore not sufficient; transport across a boundary is essential.

This distinction explains why there is no electric current in an isolated metallic conductor (with no applied voltage), even though its free electrons are in continuous thermal motion at every temperature above absolute zero. Since this motion is completely random, equal amounts of charge cross any chosen cross-sectional area in opposite directions. Consequently, the net flow rate of charge through the cross-section is zero, and hence the macroscopic current is zero.

If a net charge \(\Delta Q\) is transported across a specified cross-sectional area during a time interval \(\Delta t\), the average current (average flow rate of charge) is defined as

\[ I_{\text{avg}}=\frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t} \]

The instantaneous current is the instantaneous flow rate of charge:

\[ i=\frac{dQ}{dt} \]

Units & Dimensions

The SI unit of electric current is the ampere (\(\mathrm{A}\)), where

\[ 1\,\mathrm{A}=1\,\mathrm{C\,s^{-1}} \]

Electric current is one of the seven SI base quantities. Hence, its dimensional formula is simply \([A]\).

Current is a scalar quantity. Although it possesses a direction of flow (positive or negative with respect to a chosen reference direction), it is not a vector because it does not obey the laws of vector addition.

By convention, the direction of electric current is taken as the direction of flow of positive charge. Consequently, in metallic conductors, the conventional current is opposite to the actual drift of electrons.

Although the definition of current always refers to a specified cross-sectional area, the relevant cross-section is usually implied by the physical situation. This is analogous to mechanics, where the reference frame for describing motion is often left implicit unless a different frame is explicitly specified.

Electric current is net flow rate of net charge, not merely charge in a state of motion.
Motion is defined with respect to a reference frame, whereas current is defined with respect to a chosen cross-sectional area.
Current exists only when there is a net transport of net charge across a specified boundary.

Frame Invariance of Current

The value of steady current in a conductor does not depend on whether the conductor itself is at rest or moving.

Consider a current-carrying wire placed on the ground. Now imagine the same wire inside a car moving with constant velocity. The car may move in the same direction as the drift velocity of electrons or in the opposite direction. In either case, the measured current in the wire remains the same.

Why does this happen?

Current is defined as the rate at which charge crosses a specific cross-sectional area of the conductor. Both the charge carriers and the cross-section belong to the same physical object — the conductor. If the entire conductor moves, the cross-section also moves along with the charges.

Current depends on the relative motion between charge carriers and the cross-section of the conductor.
Under a Galilean shift (uniform change of reference frame), both velocities change by the same amount.
Their relative velocity remains unchanged. Hence the rate of charge crossing the cross-section (\( dQ/dt \)) remains the same.

Therefore, steady current in a conductor is invariant under uniform translational motion of the entire conductor.

Ways in Which Current Can Arise

Electric current is introduced as a physical quantity whenever charge in motion results in transport across a specified surface. It is not tied to a particular material or device. The defining condition is simple: charge must cross a chosen boundary.

1. Electron Emission into Free Space

When a metal is heated to sufficiently high temperatures, it emits electrons (thermionic emission). These electrons move through space.

If we imagine any surface placed perpendicular to their direction of motion, electrons cross that surface. Since charge is transported across a defined area, a current exists through that surface.

2. Electrolytic Conduction

In electrolytic solutions, both positive and negative ions are mobile. When an external electric field is applied:

  • Positive ions drift in the direction of the field.
  • Negative ions drift opposite to the field.

Current depends on the net charge transported across a cross-section, not on the motion of a single type of charge.

If positive and negative charges move in opposite directions, their contributions to current add (since both transport net charge across the same surface in the same conventional direction).

If both types of charge move in the same direction, their contributions subtract according to their sign.

3. Charging a Capacitor

When a battery is connected across a capacitor, charges move through the connecting wires toward the plates.

As long as charge crosses any cross-section of the wire, current exists in that wire — even though charge does not pass through the dielectric gap between the plates. This demonstrates that transient current can exist even in the absence of a closed physical loop.

However, steady state current can exist only in a closed loop.

Current is a transport quantity.
It arises whenever moving charge crosses a defined surface — irrespective of whether the medium is vacuum, electrolyte, or solid conductor.

In this chapter, we will focus on current in solid conductors when an electric field is established within them.

4. Lightning (Atmospheric Discharge)

During a thunderstorm, large amounts of charge accumulate in clouds due to charge separation processes. When the electric field between cloud and ground (or between clouds) becomes sufficiently strong, a rapid discharge occurs.

During this discharge, an enormous amount of charge crosses a surface in a very short time. Since current is defined as

\[ I = \frac{dQ}{dt}, \]

lightning is simply a very large electric current — typically of the order of kiloamperes to megaamperes.

5. Electric Current in the Nervous System

Electric current also exists inside living organisms. In the human nervous system, signals propagate due to controlled motion of ions across neuronal membranes.

When ions cross a membrane surface, charge is transported across that boundary. Therefore, a current exists. These biological currents are typically of the order of microamperes. Historically, Luigi Galvani's observation of these bioelectric currents in frog muscles directly inspired Alessandro Volta to invent the first electrochemical cell.

Steady Current

A physical system may exist in either a static steady state or a dynamic steady state.

In a static steady state, the constituents of the system are at rest and the macroscopic state does not change with time.

In a dynamic steady state, the constituents of the system are continuously moving, yet the macroscopic state remains unchanged with time. Mathematically, if \(P(\mathbf r,t)\) denotes any macroscopic field variable of the system, then

\[ \frac{\partial P}{\partial t}=0 \]

Examples of dynamic steady states include steady flow of a fluid through a pipe, propagation of a steady travelling wave on a stretched string, and steady transport of charge through a conductor.

Current electricity deals primarily with steady current. Unless stated otherwise, all currents discussed in this chapter are assumed to be steady. For direct current (DC), this means

\[ \boxed{\frac{dI}{dt}=0} \]

A steady current does not imply that the charge carriers are at rest. Free electrons continue to drift through the conductor under the influence of the electric field. What remains constant is the macroscopic flow rate of charge through every cross-section of the conductor.

Since the current through every cross-section is the same, no net charge accumulates anywhere inside the conductor. Consequently,

\[ I_{\text{in}}=I_{\text{out}} \]

More generally, the local condition for steady current is that the current density does not vary with time:

\[ \frac{\partial \vec J}{\partial t}=0 \]
Steady current is a dynamic steady state.
The charge carriers are continuously moving through the conductor, but the macroscopic current and current density remain time-independent.
As a result, every cross-section carries the same constant flow rate of charge, and no net charge accumulates anywhere inside the conductor.

Conduction and Convection Current

Electric current is a general transport phenomenon. Depending on how charge is transported across a specified cross-sectional area, currents may be broadly classified into two types:

  • Conduction Current: Charge carriers move through a material medium while the medium itself remains essentially stationary.
  • Convection Current: The charged matter itself moves from one place to another, carrying its charge along with it.

Conduction Current

Conduction current occurs in conducting materials where certain charge carriers are free to move through the medium. The bulk material remains stationary, while the mobile charge carriers drift under the influence of an electric field.

Examples include:

  • Free electrons drifting through metallic conductors.
  • Positive and negative ions moving through electrolytic solutions.
  • Electrons and ions moving through ionized gases (plasmas).

The microscopic and macroscopic forms of Ohm's Law developed in this chapter describe conduction current. In particular, the constitutive relation

\[ \vec J=\sigma\vec E \]

is applicable to conducting media where the current arises due to the drift of mobile charge carriers through the material.

Convection Current

In convection current, the charged body or charged medium itself moves from one place to another, carrying its charge along with it. Unlike conduction current, the charge carriers do not drift through a stationary material; instead, the entire charged object is transported.

A simple example is a uniformly charged insulating rod having linear charge density \(\lambda\), moving with a constant velocity \(v\) along its length.

In a time interval \(dt\), a length \[ dx=v\,dt \] of the rod crosses a given cross-sectional plane. The charge transported across the plane is therefore \[ dQ=\lambda\,dx=\lambda v\,dt \] Hence, the current is \[ I=\frac{dQ}{dt}=\lambda v \]

Here, the current arises simply because the charged body itself is moving. This is an example of convection current.

  • A uniformly charged rod moving with velocity \(v\).
  • Charged oil droplets moving through air.
  • Charged dust particles carried by wind.
  • An electron beam moving through vacuum.

Convection current is not governed by Ohm's Law because it does not arise from charge carriers drifting through a stationary conducting medium. Instead, the entire charged body or charged fluid is in motion.

Both conduction current and convection current represent the transport of charge across a specified surface and therefore satisfy the definition of electric current.
However, this chapter develops the theory primarily for conduction current in metallic conductors.
Convection currents will be studied in greater detail in the chapter on Magnetism, where moving charges are treated as sources of magnetic fields.
