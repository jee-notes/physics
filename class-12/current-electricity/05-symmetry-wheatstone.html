Wheatstone Bridge and Symmetrical Circuits

Exploiting structural symmetry to simplify circuits — and the instrument for precise resistance measurement.

The Limits of Series–Parallel Reduction

Many circuits cannot be reduced by simply applying series and parallel formulas. A bridge circuit — where one resistor "crosses" between two branches — is the classic example. For such topologies, we use either Kirchhoff's Laws or exploit symmetry arguments to identify equipotential nodes.

Symmetry Method — Equipotential Nodes

If a circuit has a symmetry axis, all nodes on that axis are at the same potential. A resistor connected between two equipotential nodes carries no current and can be removed (open-circuited) or short-circuited without changing anything else in the circuit.

Equipotential node rule: If two nodes are connected by a wire (zero resistance), they must be at the same potential — so no current flows through it. Conversely, if symmetry forces two nodes to the same potential, the resistor between them carries no current, and you can remove it. Either operation simplifies the circuit drastically.

Example: A cube of 12 equal resistors \( R \). Connect a battery across a body diagonal. By 3-fold rotational symmetry, the three nodes adjacent to each battery terminal are all equipotential. This reduces the cube to a simple series–parallel chain, giving \( R_{\text{eq}} = 5R/6 \).

The Wheatstone Bridge

The Wheatstone bridge is a circuit used to measure an unknown resistance with high precision. It consists of four resistors \( P, Q, R, X \) arranged in a diamond (bridge) topology, with a battery across one diagonal and a galvanometer \( G \) across the other.

\[ \text{Circuit: } A\text{-}P\text{-}B \text{ and } A\text{-}Q\text{-}C \text{ are two branches; } B\text{-}R\text{-}D \text{ and } C\text{-}X\text{-}D \text{ close the bridge; galvanometer connects } B\text{-}C. \]

Balance Condition

The bridge is balanced when no current flows through the galvanometer, meaning \( V_B = V_C \). Applying this condition via KVL for both loops:

\[ \frac{P}{Q} = \frac{R}{X} \] \[ \boxed{X = \frac{Q \cdot R}{P}} \]

At balance, the unknown resistance \( X \) (placed in the fourth arm) is calculated from the known resistances \( P \), \( Q \), and \( R \) — with the galvanometer reading zero, independent of the source EMF and internal resistance.

The Wheatstone bridge is a null method — measurement accuracy depends only on the precision of the reference resistors \( P, Q, R \), not on calibration of the galvanometer. This makes it extremely accurate compared to direct voltage/current methods.

Sensitivity of the Wheatstone Bridge

The bridge is most sensitive (galvanometer gives the largest deflection for a small imbalance) when all four arms have equal resistance: \( P = Q = R = X \). Sensitivity decreases when the arms are very unequal.

Maximum sensitivity condition: \( P/Q = R/X \) and all arms of comparable magnitude. The ratio \( P/Q \) is usually selected from a set of standard ratios (0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100, 1000) on a post-office box.

Unbalanced Wheatstone Bridge

When the bridge is not at balance (\( PX \neq QR \)), current flows through the galvanometer. This case requires Kirchhoff's Laws (or the delta–star transformation) and is not tractable by series–parallel methods alone.

For a small imbalance \( \delta R \) in one arm, the galvanometer current is approximately:

\[ I_g \approx \frac{\mathcal{E}}{2(P+R)} \cdot \frac{\delta R}{R} \quad \text{(approximate, for equal arms)} \]

Delta–Star (Δ–Y) Transformation

When a three-node sub-network cannot be simplified by series/parallel methods, the delta–star transformation converts between a triangle (Δ) and a Y-shaped arrangement of three resistors.

Δ → Y: For a delta with resistors \( R_{12}, R_{23}, R_{31} \) opposite vertices 1, 2, 3:

\[ R_1 = \frac{R_{12}\cdot R_{31}}{R_{12}+R_{23}+R_{31}}, \quad R_2 = \frac{R_{12}\cdot R_{23}}{R_{12}+R_{23}+R_{31}}, \quad R_3 = \frac{R_{23}\cdot R_{31}}{R_{12}+R_{23}+R_{31}} \]

Y → Δ: Reverse transformation:

\[ R_{12} = R_1 + R_2 + \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_3}, \quad \text{(and cyclically)} \]

For three equal resistors \( R \): delta gives \( R_Y = R/3 \); star gives \( R_\Delta = 3R \) by the inverse.
