Galvanometer, Ammeter, and Voltmeter

Measuring current and voltage — converting a sensitive galvanometer into practical instruments.

The Moving Coil Galvanometer

A galvanometer is a sensitive instrument that detects and measures small electric currents. It works on the principle that a current-carrying coil in a magnetic field experiences a torque. The coil deflects until this magnetic torque is balanced by a restoring torque from a spiral spring.

At equilibrium, deflection \( \theta \propto I \). Key parameters of a galvanometer:

  • Full-scale deflection current \( I_g \): the current that gives maximum pointer deflection (typically \( 10^{-3} \) A to \( 10^{-5} \) A).
  • Galvanometer resistance \( G \): the resistance of the coil (typically \( 10 \,\Omega \) to \( 100 \,\Omega \)).
A galvanometer is delicate — it can only carry a very small current \( I_g \) without being damaged. To measure larger currents or voltages, we must modify it by adding external resistors.

Converting to an Ammeter (Shunt Resistor)

An ammeter measures current and must be connected in series in the circuit. It must have very low resistance so it does not alter the circuit's current.

To convert a galvanometer to an ammeter that can measure up to current \( I \): connect a small resistance \( S \) (shunt) in parallel with the galvanometer. The shunt diverts most of the current, allowing only \( I_g \) through the galvanometer.

Derivation

At full-scale deflection, voltage across galvanometer = voltage across shunt:

\[I_g \cdot G = (I - I_g) \cdot S\] \[\boxed{S = \frac{I_g \cdot G}{I - I_g}}\]

The effective resistance of the ammeter:

\[R_A = \frac{G \cdot S}{G + S} = \frac{G \cdot S}{G + S}\]

For \( I \gg I_g \): \( S \ll G \), so \( R_A \approx S \) — very small, as required.

  • Ideal ammeter: \( R_A = 0 \).
  • A smaller shunt allows measurement of larger currents.
  • Different shunts can be switched to select different current ranges.

Converting to a Voltmeter (Series Resistor)

A voltmeter measures potential difference and must be connected in parallel across the element. It must have very high resistance so it draws negligible current and does not disturb the circuit.

To convert a galvanometer to a voltmeter that measures up to voltage \( V \): connect a large resistance \( R_h \) (high resistance) in series with the galvanometer. The series resistor limits the current to \( I_g \) when the full-scale voltage is applied.

Derivation

At full-scale deflection, total voltage across galvanometer + series resistance = \( V \):

\[I_g(G + R_h) = V\] \[\boxed{R_h = \frac{V}{I_g} - G}\]

The effective resistance of the voltmeter:

\[R_V = G + R_h = \frac{V}{I_g}\]
  • Ideal voltmeter: \( R_V = \infty \).
  • A larger \( R_h \) allows measurement of higher voltages.
  • Sensitivity of a voltmeter: \( 1/I_g \), measured in \( \Omega/\text{V} \). Higher sensitivity (\( \Omega/\text{V} \)) means less loading effect on the circuit.

Comparison: Ideal vs. Real Instruments

Property Ammeter Voltmeter
Connected In series In parallel
Ideal resistance \( R_A = 0 \) \( R_V = \infty \)
Modification Shunt \( S \) in parallel High \( R_h \) in series
Effect when non-ideal Reduces circuit current Reduces measured voltage

Errors Due to Meter Resistance

Ammeter error: Since an ammeter has resistance \( R_A \), inserting it increases the total circuit resistance, so the measured current is slightly less than the true current.

\[ I_{\text{measured}} = \frac{V}{R + R_A} < \frac{V}{R}=I_{\text{true}} \]

Voltmeter error: Since a voltmeter has finite resistance \( R_V \), connecting it in parallel reduces the effective resistance, so the measured voltage is slightly less than the true voltage.

\[ V_{\text{measured}} = I \cdot \frac{R \cdot R_V}{R + R_V} < I \cdot R=V_{\text{true}} \]

Both errors are reduced by using better instruments (\( R_A \to 0 \), \( R_V \to \infty \)).
