Current Electricity

From electrostatic equilibrium to steady transport of charge.

Conceptual Shift from Rest to Motion

Electrostatics → Charges at rest (time-independent electric fields)
Current Electricity → Charges in motion (time-independent current)

Electrostatics studies electric charges that are at rest. The charge distribution does not change with time, and consequently the electric field produced by it is also time-independent. We are not concerned with why the charges are at rest — they may be bound inside atoms, embedded in solids, or even mechanically constrained. The essential condition is simply that there is no macroscopic motion of charge.

Current Electricity begins when charges are allowed to move. An electric charge experiences a force whenever an electric field is applied. If the charge is free to move, it accelerates and contributes to an electric current.

Where Do Free Charges Exist?

In ordinary matter, electrons and nuclei are bound together inside atoms and molecules. Because of this binding, they cannot move freely under small applied electric fields.

However, in bulk matter containing an enormous number of atoms (for example, one gram of water contains approximately \( 10^{22} \) molecules), the behavior of charges depends strongly on the material.

  • In some materials, charges remain tightly bound even when an electric field is applied. These materials are called insulators.
  • In other materials, certain charges are free to move through the bulk. These materials are called conductors.

Conductors are not limited to solids. In metallic solids, negatively charged electrons move through a lattice of fixed positive ions. In electrolytic solutions, both positive and negative ions can move through the medium. In ionized gases, charged particles also move freely.

Thus, the chapter on Current Electricity studies the motion of free charge carriers and the resulting steady flow of charge when an electric field is maintained.

Motion vs Flow

The mere motion of charge does not automatically imply the existence of current. Motion is defined with respect to an observer or a reference frame. A charged particle may move, rotate, vibrate, or translate — but motion alone is not sufficient to define current.

To understand this clearly, imagine two charged pith balls attached to the ends of a rigid stick. If the stick is rotated or vibrated, both charges are in motion. Yet we would not immediately say that an electric current exists. Why? Because current is not defined merely by motion — it is defined by charge moving across a surface boundary.

Why Motion Is Not Enough

Suppose a charged particle moves in space. Its velocity can be measured relative to an observer. But unless we specify a cross-sectional surface area and examine whether the charge crosses that surface, the concept of current has no meaning.

Thus:

  • Motion is defined with respect to a reference frame.
  • Current is defined with respect to a chosen area.

Without a surface boundary (a cross-section), there is motion but no defined flow.

Flow Requires a Cross-Section

To define electric current, we imagine a small area placed perpendicular to the possible direction of charge transport. This area acts as a "gate". Only when charge crosses this gate do we say that current exists.

Both positive and negative charges may cross this area in either direction. In a time interval t:

  • \( q_+ \) = net positive charge crossing forward
  • \( q_- \) = net negative charge crossing forward

The net charge transported forward across the surface is:

\[ q_{\text{net}} = q_+ - q_- \]

The average current is then defined as:

\[ I_{\text{avg}} = \frac{q_{\text{net}}}{t} \]

Motion is a kinematic concept — it describes how a particle changes position with time.

Current is a transport concept — it quantifies how much charge crosses a specified surface per unit time.

A charge may move without producing current. Current exists only when there is net transport of charge across a defined cross-sectional area.

Two Scales of Ohm’s Law

Ohm's Law merges NLM with electrostatic Coulomb's Law for a conductor. Current electricity operates simultaneously at two descriptive levels:

Macroscopic:

\[ V = IR \]

Microscopic:

\[ \vec{J} = \sigma \vec{E} \]

Resistance emerges from the intrinsic material property (resistivity) combined with the geometry of the conductor as a macroscopic property. At the microscopic level, electrical energy is continuously converted into thermal energy through interactions between electrons and the lattice.

Organisation of Circuit Analysis

Circuit is considered as a network of circuit elements. Potential difference is called Voltage which drives current in the circuit. Circuit analysis progresses from structural simplification to universal conservation laws:

  • Level 1 — Equivalent Resistance: Series and parallel reduction.
  • Level 2 — Symmetry & Structural Arguments: Equipotential nodes and bridge logic.
  • Level 3 — Kirchhoff’s Laws: Conservation of charge and energy for general networks.

Power in a Circuit

A circuit is not merely a closed loop for charge flow — it is a structured pathway for energy transfer.

In practical systems, electrical appliances such as bulbs, heaters etc. are connected to an electric circuit so that they may consume energy from circuit to perform useful work. In circuit analysis, such devices are modeled as a resistor for simplicity and called load. The role of the load is to consume electrical energy from the circuit and convert it into other useful forms such as light, heat or mechanical work.

The source (for example, a battery) supplies energy to the circuit by maintaining a potential difference. The circuit connects the source to the load, enabling continuous transfer of power from supply to consumption.

Understanding circuits therefore requires analyzing not just current and voltage, but how different network configurations influence the rate of energy transfer, i.e., electrical power.

Electric Measuring Instruments

In current electricity, we study the flow of charge and the transfer of electrical energy in circuits. To analyze and control this energy exchange, we require instruments that measure fundamental circuit parameters.

Each instrument is designed to measure a specific electrical quantity:

  • Galvanometer — Detects small currents (foundation for ammeter and voltmeter).
  • Ammeter — Measures electric current (\( I \)).
  • Voltmeter — Measures potential difference (\( V \)).
  • Meter Bridge — Determines resistance (\( R \)) using bridge principle.
  • Post Office Box (PO Box) — Measures resistance with higher precision.
  • Potentiometer — Measures battery EMF with higher precision.

These instruments translate abstract electrical quantities into measurable mechanical or numerical readings, allowing practical verification of circuit laws.
