Current Electricity

From electrostatic equilibrium to steady transport of charge.

Conceptual Shift

Electrostatics → Charge in equilibrium ( \( E = 0 \) inside conductor )
Current Electricity → Charge in steady motion ( \( E \neq 0 \) inside conductor )

Electrostatics deals with charge at rest. In that state, the electric field inside a conductor vanishes due to electrostatic equilibrium.

Current Electricity begins when this equilibrium is deliberately disturbed by an external energy source. A sustained electric field is established inside the conductor, producing a steady transport of charge.

The system is no longer static — it is in a non-equilibrium steady state.

Motion vs Flow

Individual electrons in a conductor already move randomly due to thermal energy. Yet this random motion produces no net current. Even when the current in conductor is 0, it does not imply that electrons are at rest individually. They are in a state of random motion but the average motion of all electrons is such that there is no net current measured.

Current is not about motion of electrons alone — it is about flow across an area.

We measure how much charge crosses a given cross-section per unit time:

\[ I = n e A v_d \]

The drift velocity \( v_d \) represents the tiny directional bias imposed by the external electric field. It is not the actual speed of electrons. It is similar to drift caused in swimmer river problems due to river velocity.

Two Scales of Ohm’s Law

Current electricity operates simultaneously at two descriptive levels:

Macroscopic:

\[ V = IR \]

Microscopic:

\[ \vec{J} = \sigma \vec{E} \]

Resistance emerges from the intrinsic material property (resistivity) combined with the geometry of the conductor as a macroscopic property. At the microscopic level, electrical energy is continuously converted into thermal energy through interactions between electrons and the lattice.
