Current Electricity

From static charges to the steady flow of electric charge.

Conceptual Shift from Rest to Motion

Electrostatics → Charges at rest (time-independent electric field)
Current Electricity → Steady flow of charge (steady electric current)

Electrostatics studies electric charges that are at rest. The charge distribution does not change with time, and consequently the electric field produced by it is also time-independent.

We are not concerned with how (or why i.e. the mechanism) the charges are at rest — they may be bound inside atoms, embedded in solids, or even mechanically constrained. The essential condition is simply that there is no macroscopic motion of charge.

Current Electricity begins when some charges are free to move rather than remaining bound to atoms or molecules.

Every electric charge experiences a force when an electric field is applied. If the charge is free to move, its state of motion changes, and the collective motion of such free charge carriers gives rise to an electric current.

Where Do Free Charges Exist?

Whether charges are free to move depends on the nature of the material. In most materials, electrons and nuclei are bound together inside atoms or molecules and cannot move appreciably under ordinary electric fields. Such materials behave as insulators.

However, some materials contain charge carriers that are free to move through the bulk. These materials are called conductors. Depending on the medium, the mobile charge carriers may be electrons, positive ions, negative ions, or a combination of these.

  • Metallic conductors (solid metals and liquid mercury): Conduction occurs due to the motion of free electrons through a lattice of fixed positive ions.
  • Electrolytic conductors (electrolytic solutions and molten salts): Conduction occurs due to the motion of both positive and negative ions.
  • Gaseous conductors (ionized gases or plasmas): Conduction occurs due to the motion of electrons and ions.
Current Electricity studies the motion of free charge carriers and the resulting steady flow of charge through a conducting material under the influence of an electric field.

Although electric current can exist in metallic conductors, electrolytic solutions, and ionized gases, this chapter develops the theory primarily for metallic conductors, where the charge carriers are free electrons.

Motion vs Flow

The mere motion of electric charge does not automatically imply the existence of an electric current.

Motion is defined with respect to an observer or a reference frame. A charged particle may translate, rotate, oscillate, or move along any trajectory. However, motion by itself is not sufficient to define current.

To appreciate this distinction, imagine two charged pith balls attached to the ends of a rigid rod. If the rod is rotated or vibrated, both charges are in motion. Nevertheless, we do not describe this as an electric current because current is not defined merely by motion—it is defined by the transport of charge across a specified surface.

Why Motion Is Not Enough

Suppose a charged particle moves in space. Its motion can be described with respect to a chosen reference frame. However, unless we specify a surface and determine whether the charge crosses that surface, the concept of current has no meaning.

  • Motion is defined with respect to a reference frame.
  • Current is defined with respect to a chosen surface (cross-section).

Thus, without a specified surface boundary, there may be motion, but there is no defined flow.

Flow Requires a Cross-Section

To define electric current, we imagine an oriented cross-sectional area placed inside the conducting medium. This surface acts as an imaginary gate. Electric current measures the rate at which net charge crosses this gate.

Both positive and negative charge carriers may cross the surface in either direction. During a time interval t,

  • \(q_+\) = net positive charge crossing in the chosen positive direction
  • \(q_-\) = net negative charge crossing in the chosen positive direction

The net charge transported across the surface is

\[ q_{\text{net}}=q_+-q_- \]

Hence, the average electric current is defined as

\[ I_{\text{avg}}=\frac{q_{\text{net}}}{t}. \]
Motion is a kinematic concept—it describes how the position of a particle changes with time.
Flow is a transport concept—it describes the transport of a physical quantity across a specified surface.
Electric current is the transport of electric charge. It exists only when there is a net flow of charge across a defined cross-sectional area.

Microscopic and Macroscopic Ohm's Law

Current electricity may be described at two different scales. At the microscopic scale, we study the motion of free charge carriers inside the conducting material under the action of an electric field. At the macroscopic scale, we ignore the detailed motion of individual charge carriers and instead describe the conductor as a whole using measurable quantities such as current, voltage and resistance.

The microscopic description forms the fundamental physical law of electrical conduction, while the macroscopic description is obtained by applying the microscopic law to a conductor of specified geometry.

Microscopic Description

Inside a conducting material, the local current density is proportional to the local electric field:

\[ \boxed{\vec J=\sigma\vec E} \]

This is the microscopic form of Ohm's Law. It is a constitutive law of the material and describes how free charge carriers respond locally to an applied electric field. At this level, electrical conduction is governed by electrostatics and Newtonian mechanics. The electric field exerts a force \(q\vec E\) on every free charge carrier, while collisions with the lattice continually modify their motion. The resulting drift of free charge carriers gives rise to the current density \(\vec J\).

The proportionality constant \(\sigma\), called the electrical conductivity, is an intrinsic property of the material.

Macroscopic Description

In circuit analysis, we are usually interested in the overall behaviour of an entire conductor rather than the local values of \(\vec E\) and \(\vec J\). For a conductor having uniform material properties and uniform electric field, the microscopic law may be integrated over the conductor to obtain the familiar macroscopic quantities.

The potential difference is obtained by integrating the electric field along the direction of current flow,

\[ V=\int \vec E\cdot d\vec l \]

while the electric current is obtained by integrating the current density over a cross-sectional area perpendicular to the current,

\[ I=\int \vec J\cdot d\vec A \]

For a uniform conductor,

\[ V=EL,\qquad I=JA \]

where \(L\) is measured along the direction of the electric field (or current), and \(A\) is the cross-sectional area perpendicular to the current. Thus,

\[ R=\frac{V}{I}=\frac{EL}{JA}=\frac{L}{\sigma A}=\rho\frac{L}{A} \]

where

\[ \rho=\frac1\sigma \]

is the resistivity of the material.

The microscopic law \[ \vec J=\sigma\vec E \] is the fundamental physical law describing electrical conduction inside a material.
The familiar macroscopic relation \[ V=IR \] is obtained by applying this law to a conductor of specified geometry.
Consequently, \[ R=\rho\frac{L}{A} \] contains both the intrinsic material property (resistivity) and the geometrical properties (length and cross-sectional area) of the conductor.

Organisation of Circuit Analysis

An electric circuit is viewed as a network of interconnected circuit elements such as cells, resistors and connecting wires. The objective of circuit analysis is to determine the current through, and the potential difference across, every element of the network.

A circuit diagram is the electrical analogue of a Free Body Diagram (FBD) in mechanics. Both are simplified graphical representations of a physical system that retain only the information relevant for analysis. They provide the framework for applying the fundamental physical laws under different constraining situations.

The methodology of circuit analysis is therefore very similar to the study of dynamics in mechanics. In mechanics, the same physical laws—Newton's Laws and the principles of conservation of momentum and energy—are applied to systems having different constraints such as strings, pulleys, hinges, rolling conditions and contact surfaces. Similarly, in current electricity, the same electrical laws are applied to circuits having different configurations and interconnections.

The source (cell or battery) maintains a potential difference, commonly called voltage, between different parts of the circuit. This potential difference acts as the driving agency for the motion of free charge carriers and continuously supplies energy to the circuit, much like gravity acts as the driving agency in many mechanics problems.

Circuit analysis therefore combines the physical laws of electricity with the structural constraints imposed by the circuit configuration.

  • Physical Laws
    • Ohm's Law
    • Kirchhoff's Junction Law (Conservation of Charge)
    • Kirchhoff's Loop Law (Conservation of Energy)
  • Structural Constraints
    • Series and parallel combinations
    • Equipotential conductors
    • Symmetric configurations
    • Bridge networks and other circuit topologies

In most problems, the structural constraints are exploited first to simplify the circuit. Equivalent resistance, symmetry and equipotential arguments often reduce the complexity considerably. Only when these simplifications are insufficient do we apply Kirchhoff's Laws to analyse the complete network.

  • Level 1 — Structural Simplification: Equivalent resistance using series and parallel combinations.
  • Level 2 — Structural Arguments: Symmetry, equipotential nodes and bridge analysis.
  • Level 3 — General Network Analysis: Application of Kirchhoff's Laws to arbitrary circuits.
Physics problems are solved by applying the same fundamental laws under different constraining situations.
Circuit analysis follows exactly the same philosophy as dynamics in mechanics.
The circuit diagram plays the role of a Free Body Diagram, voltage provides the driving agency, Ohm's and Kirchhoff's Laws provide the governing physical laws, while the circuit configuration supplies the structural constraints.

Power in a Circuit

A circuit is not merely a closed loop for charge flow — it is a structured pathway for energy transfer.

In practical systems, electrical appliances such as bulbs, heaters etc. are connected to an electric circuit so that they may consume energy from circuit to perform useful work.

In circuit analysis, such devices are modeled as a resistor for simplicity and called load. The role of the load is to consume electrical energy from the circuit and convert it into other useful forms such as light, heat or mechanical work.

The source (for example, a battery) supplies energy to the circuit by maintaining a potential difference. The circuit connects the source to the load, enabling continuous transfer of power from supply to consumption.

Understanding circuits therefore requires analyzing not just current and voltage, but how different network configurations influence the rate of energy transfer, i.e., electrical power.

Electric Measuring Instruments

In current electricity, we study the flow of charge and the transfer of electrical energy in circuits. To analyze and control this energy exchange, we require instruments that measure fundamental circuit parameters.

Each instrument is designed to measure a specific electrical quantity:

  • Galvanometer — Detects small currents (foundation for ammeter and voltmeter).
  • Ammeter — Measures electric current (\( I \)).
  • Voltmeter — Measures potential difference (\( V \)).
  • Meter Bridge — Determines resistance (\( R \)) using bridge principle.
  • Post Office Box (PO Box) — Measures resistance with higher precision.
  • Potentiometer — Measures battery EMF with higher precision.

These instruments translate abstract electrical quantities into measurable mechanical or numerical readings, allowing practical verification of circuit laws.
