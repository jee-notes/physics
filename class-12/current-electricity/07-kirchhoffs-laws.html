Kirchhoff's Laws and Circuit Analysis

Conservation of charge and energy — the universal tools for analysing any electrical network.

When Series–Parallel Reduction Fails

Series and parallel formulas are powerful shortcuts, but they apply only when all elements share the same current or the same voltage. They fail completely when:

  • A circuit contains multiple batteries in different branches.
  • The topology cannot be reduced to nodes (e.g., an unbalanced Wheatstone bridge).

For general networks, we abandon geometric reduction and apply Kirchhoff's Laws — which generate a system of linear equations whose solution gives all branch currents.

Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL) — Junction Rule

KCL is the circuit-level statement of Conservation of Charge. In a steady-state circuit, charge cannot accumulate or deplete at any junction.

The Junction Rule

The algebraic sum of all currents at any junction equals zero:

\[\sum I = 0\]

Sign Convention: Currents entering the junction are positive; currents leaving are negative. Equivalently: \(\sum I_{\text{in}} = \sum I_{\text{out}}\).

For a junction with 4 currents: if \( I_1 \) and \( I_2 \) enter, and \( I_3 \) and \( I_4 \) leave:

\[I_1 + I_2 = I_3 + I_4\]
KCL is deeply physical: charge is conserved. If more current flowed out of a junction than in, charge would be disappearing — impossible. The junction rule ensures the charge balance is maintained at every node at all times.

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL) — Loop Rule

KVL is the circuit-level statement of Conservation of Energy. Because the electric field is conservative (derived from a potential), the work done moving a charge around any closed loop is zero — meaning the total potential change is zero.

The Loop Rule

The algebraic sum of all potential changes around any closed loop is zero:

\[\sum \Delta V = 0\]

Sign Convention for Traversal:

  • Resistor \( R \): Traversing with assumed current direction → potential drop \( -IR \). Traversing against current → potential rise \( +IR \).
  • Battery \( \mathcal{E} \): Traversing from negative (−) to positive (+) terminal → potential rise \( +\mathcal{E} \). Traversing from (+) to (−) → potential drop \( -\mathcal{E} \).
  • Internal resistance \( r \): Treat as a resistor in series with the battery.

Procedure for Circuit Analysis

  1. Label branch currents — assign a current variable and a direction to every branch. The direction is arbitrary; a negative result means the actual current flows opposite to your assumed direction.
  2. Apply KCL at enough junctions — for a circuit with \( J \) junctions, you need \( (J-1) \) independent KCL equations.
  3. Apply KVL to enough independent loops — for a circuit with \( B \) branches and \( J \) junctions, you need \( B - (J-1) \) loop equations.
  4. Solve the system of equations for the unknown currents.
For a network with \( N \) unknown branch currents, you need exactly \( N \) independent equations — any combination of \( (J-1) \) KCL equations and sufficient KVL loop equations. More loops can be written than needed; choose independent ones that contain new branches.

Worked Example: Two-Battery Network

A circuit has a 10 V battery (internal resistance \( 1\,\Omega \)) in the left branch and a 4 V battery (internal resistance \( 1\,\Omega \)) in the right branch, joined by a \( 5\,\Omega \) resistor in the middle. Let \( I_1 \), \( I_2 \) flow from the positive terminals of battery 1 and 2 respectively, and \( I_3 \) flow through the middle resistor.

KCL at top junction:

\[I_1 + I_2 = I_3\]

KVL — Left loop (traversing clockwise):

\[10 - I_1(1) - I_3(5) = 0 \implies 10 = I_1 + 5I_3\]

KVL — Right loop (traversing clockwise):

\[4 - I_2(1) - I_3(5) = 0 \implies 4 = I_2 + 5I_3\]

Substituting \( I_3 = I_1 + I_2 \) and solving: \( I_1 = 1.5 \) A, \( I_2 = -0.5 \) A (flows opposite to assumed), \( I_3 = 1 \) A.
