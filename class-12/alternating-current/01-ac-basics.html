AC Quantities

Mathematical description of alternating physical quantities.

Concept

Whenever we begin a new chapter, we first establish the mathematical framework required to analyze its physics.

For Alternating Current, our syllabus focuses exclusively on sinusoidal electrical parameters.

Handling AC circuits smoothly requires fluency with the defining parameters of a sinusoid — amplitude, frequency, angular frequency, time period, average value, RMS value, and phase.

We have already encountered the basics of sinusoidal functions in Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM). Here, we build directly on that foundation and extend it to slightly more advanced mathematical tools.

Alternating Current

An AC source produces a voltage that varies sinusoidally with time:

\[ v(t) = V_p \sin(\omega t + \phi) \]

This is the standard form used to represent a physical quantity that varies sinusoidally with time. Here, \( v(t) \) is the instantaneous value, and the remaining symbols define the waveform:

  • Peak Voltage (\( V_p \)): Also called the amplitude (sometimes denoted \( V_0 \)), it represents the maximum magnitude reached by the oscillating voltage in either direction.
  • Angular Frequency (\( \omega \)): Appearing inside the trigonometric function as the coefficient of \( t \), it represents the phase angle swept per second. Its SI unit is radians per second (rad/s).
  • Frequency (\( f \)): Although not written explicitly in the expression, it is related to angular frequency by \( \omega = 2\pi f \). Its SI unit is cycles (or revolutions) per second, called Hertz (Hz).
  • Initial Phase (\( \phi \)): Sets the starting state (angle) of the sinusoid at \( t = 0 \), measured in radians (or degrees).

Notice the contrast in how sources are specified in circuit problems. For DC, we simply state that a \( 5\text{ V} \) battery is connected across two terminals with a fixed polarity.

For AC, because the polarity and magnitude constantly vary, a source is characterized by its frequency and amplitude — for example, a problem may state that "an AC source of frequency \( 50\text{ Hz} \) and peak voltage \( 10\text{ V} \) is connected in the circuit."

Assuming zero initial phase (\( \phi = 0 \)), we write the instantaneous voltage as: \( v(t) = 10 \sin(100\pi t)\text{ V} \).

Time Period

The time period of one complete cycle is \( T = 1/f = 2\pi/\omega \).

For the Indian power grid: \( f = 50\text{ Hz} \), \( T = 20\text{ ms} \), and \( \omega = 100\pi\text{ rad/s} \).

Phase

Phase is the most important parameter of a sinusoid—it represents the complete angular argument inside the trigonometric function:

\[ v(t) = V_p \sin(\omega t + \phi) \]

That is, the instantaneous phase at any time \( t \) is \( \theta(t) = \omega t + \phi \).

Evaluating this at \( t = 0 \) gives \( \theta(0) = \phi \), which is why \( \phi \) is called the initial phase (or epoch)—it specifies the starting state of the oscillation.

Phase Difference

When two quantities oscillate at the same angular frequency \( \omega \) with different initial phases:

\[ v(t) = V_p\sin(\omega t + \phi_v), \qquad i(t) = I_p\sin(\omega t + \phi_i) \]

the phase difference between voltage and current is the difference between their instantaneous phases:

\[ \Delta\phi = \theta_v(t) - \theta_i(t) = (\omega t + \phi_v) - (\omega t + \phi_i) = \phi_v - \phi_i \]

Because both oscillate at the same frequency, \( \omega t \) cancels out and \( \Delta\phi \) remains constant at all times:

  • If \( \Delta\phi \gt 0 \): voltage leads current (or current lags voltage)
  • If \( \Delta\phi \lt 0 \): voltage lags current (or current leads voltage)
  • If \( \Delta\phi = 0 \): voltage and current are in phase

Other Forms

The standard form for representing a sinusoid is \( v(t) = V_p \sin(\omega t + \phi) \), where the peak value \( V_p \) is strictly positive (\( V_p \gt 0 \)) and the trigonometric function is \( +\sin \).

However, problems frequently present non-standard forms—for example, \( v(t) = -V_p \sin(\omega t + \phi_v) \) or \( v(t) = V_p \cos(\omega t + \phi_v) \) — which must first be converted into standard form before reading the phase.

In \( v(t) = -V_p \sin(\omega t + \phi_v) \), the peak voltage is still \(V_p\) (never \(-V_p\)), and the angular frequency \(\omega\), frequency \(f\), and time period \(T\) remain completely unchanged.

However, the true initial phase \(\phi = \theta(0)\) is not equal to \(\phi_v\) .

To determine the true initial phase \( \phi \), we convert the expression into standard \( +\sin \) form using a sine-shifting rotation.

Sine-Shifting Rotation

A sine-shifting rotation is the angular phase shift introduced when converting non-standard sinusoidal expressions (involving \( \cos \) or negative signs) into the canonical \( +\sin(\omega t + \phi) \) form:

  • Cosine to Sine (\( +\cos \to +\sin \)): \[ V_p \cos(\omega t + \phi_0) = V_p \sin\!\left(\omega t + \phi_0 + \frac{\pi}{2}\right) \implies \phi = \phi_0 + 90^\circ \]
  • Negative Sine to Positive Sine (\( -\sin \to +\sin \)): \[ -V_p \sin(\omega t + \phi_0) = V_p \sin(\omega t + \phi_0 + \pi) \implies \phi = \phi_0 + 180^\circ \] (Equivalently, \( \phi = \phi_0 - 180^\circ \), since \( \pm 180^\circ \) represents the exact same state.)
  • Negative Cosine to Positive Sine (\( -\cos \to +\sin \)): \[ -V_p \cos(\omega t + \phi_0) = V_p \sin\!\left(\omega t + \phi_0 - \frac{\pi}{2}\right) \implies \phi = \phi_0 - 90^\circ \] (Equivalently, \( \phi = \phi_0 + 270^\circ \).)
Never subtract phases directly until both \(v(t)\) and \(i(t)\) are written in standard \(+\sin(\omega t + \phi)\) form with positive amplitudes.
For instance, consider the phase difference between \(v(t) = V_p\cos(\omega t)\) and \(i(t) = I_p\sin(\omega t)\).
We cannot say that \(\Delta\phi = 0\) or voltage and current are in phase.
Rewriting \(v(t) = V_p\sin(\omega t + 90^\circ)\) immediately reveals \(\phi_v = +90^\circ\) and \(\phi_i = 0^\circ\), giving \( \Delta\phi = +90^\circ \) (voltage leads current by \( 90^\circ \)).

Why Is Phase Difference Important?

Phase difference becomes critical specifically when adding (or subtracting) two or more sinusoids of the same angular frequency.

Consider two sinusoidal AC currents, \(i_1(t) = I_{p,1}\sin(\omega t + \phi_1)\) and \(i_2(t) = I_{p,2}\sin(\omega t + \phi_2)\), that need to be added:

\[ i_R(t) = I_{p,R}\sin(\omega t + \phi_R) = i_1(t) + i_2(t) \]

Here, both the resultant peak current \(I_{p,R}\) and the resultant initial phase \(\phi_R\) depend on the individual amplitudes and phases:

\[ I_{p,R} = f(I_{p,1},\, I_{p,2},\, \phi_1,\, \phi_2), \qquad \phi_R = g(I_{p,1},\, I_{p,2},\, \phi_1,\, \phi_2) \]

In particular, the resultant amplitude \(I_{p,R}\) lies between a minimum and a maximum bound:

\[ |I_{p,1} - I_{p,2}| \le I_{p,R} \le I_{p,1} + I_{p,2} \]

and its exact value within this range is governed by the phase difference:

\[ \Delta\phi = \phi_1 - \phi_2 \]

This works identically to vector addition, where the magnitude of the resultant of two vectors lies between \(|A - B|\) and \(A + B\) depending on the angle between them:

  • In phase (\(\Delta\phi = \phi_1 - \phi_2 = 0\)): The resultant amplitude is maximum, \(I_{p,R} = I_{p,1} + I_{p,2}\).
  • Out of phase by \(180^\circ\) (\(\Delta\phi = \phi_1 - \phi_2 = 180^\circ\)): The resultant amplitude is minimum, \(I_{p,R} = |I_{p,1} - I_{p,2}|\).

This behavior can also be visualized by plotting the graphs of the two sinusoids, for arbitrary phase angles. To simplify this addition, we use dedicated mathematical tools like phasors to add sinusoids.

The principle that the addition of sinusoids depends on their phase difference is used in other chapters as well, such as Wave Optics.
In that chapter, we relate phase difference to path difference to determine interference patterns.
