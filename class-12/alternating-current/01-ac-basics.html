AC Quantities: Peak, RMS, and Phase

Describing the sinusoidal variation of voltage and current — and the RMS values that make sense of power.

Instantaneous and Peak Values

An AC source produces a voltage that varies sinusoidally with time:

\[ v(t) = V_0 \sin(\omega t) \]

where \( V_0 \) is the peak voltage (amplitude) and \( \omega = 2\pi f \) is the angular frequency (rad/s). For a purely resistive circuit, the current is in phase with the voltage:

\[ i(t) = I_0 \sin(\omega t), \quad I_0 = \frac{V_0}{R} \]

The time period is \( T = 1/f = 2\pi/\omega \). For the Indian power grid: \( f = 50 \) Hz, \( T = 20 \) ms, \( \omega = 100\pi \) rad/s.

Root Mean Square (RMS) Values

The average of a sinusoid over a complete cycle is zero — so the average voltage or current is not a useful measure of power. Instead, we use the RMS value, defined as the square root of the time-average of the square:

\[ V_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\langle v^2 \rangle} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T V_0^2\sin^2(\omega t)\,dt} \]

Since \( \langle \sin^2(\omega t) \rangle = 1/2 \):

\[ \boxed{V_{\text{rms}} = \frac{V_0}{\sqrt{2}}, \qquad I_{\text{rms}} = \frac{I_0}{\sqrt{2}}} \]
The RMS value of an AC voltage is the DC voltage that would produce the same average power dissipation in a resistor. When we say "230 V mains supply," we mean \( V_{\text{rms}} = 230 \) V — the peak voltage is \( V_0 = 230\sqrt{2} \approx 325 \) V.

Phase and Phase Difference

In the general sinusoidal form \( v(t) = V_0\sin(\omega t + \phi) \), the quantity \( \phi \) is the initial phase. When two quantities oscillate at the same frequency but with different initial phases:

\[ v = V_0\sin(\omega t + \phi_v), \quad i = I_0\sin(\omega t + \phi_i) \]

the phase difference is \( \Delta\phi = \phi_v - \phi_i \).

  • If \( \Delta\phi > 0 \): voltage leads current (or current lags voltage) — inductive.
  • If \( \Delta\phi < 0 \): voltage lags current (or current leads voltage) — capacitive.
  • If \( \Delta\phi = 0 \): voltage and current are in phase — purely resistive.
