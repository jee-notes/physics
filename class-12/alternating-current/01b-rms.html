RMS Value

Physical and Mathematical meaning of RMS quantity.

Concept

A phasor is a mathematical tool used to add multiple sinusoidal quantities of the same frequency.

It is used heavily in AC circuit analysis—for example, when applying Kirchhoff's Current Law at a junction, \( i_1(t) + i_2(t) + i_3(t) + i_4(t) = 0 \), or Kirchhoff's Voltage Law around a loop where every term is a sinusoid.

While two sinusoids can be combined using standard trigonometric compound-angle identities, that approach becomes algebraically cumbersome when adding three or more sinusoids.

Phasor analysis solves this by mapping each sinusoid onto a 2D vector (or Argand) plane using polar coordinates—encoding only its amplitude and initial phase:

\[ v_1(t) = V_1 \sin(\omega t + \phi_1), \qquad v_2(t) = V_2 \sin(\omega t - \phi_2) \]

To compute \( v_1(t) + v_2(t) \), we represent them as 2D polar phasors \( \vec{V}_1 = (V_1, \phi_1) \) (often written \( V_1 \angle \phi_1 \)) and \( \vec{V}_2 = (V_2, -\phi_2) \), and add them like vectors.

A phasor is a rotating vector whose magnitude equals the amplitude (peak value) of a sinusoidal quantity, and whose angle with the positive x-axis equals the instantaneous phase.

The projection of the phasor onto the y-axis gives the instantaneous value of the sinusoid at time \( t \).

For a voltage \( v = V_0\sin(\omega t + \phi) \):

  • The phasor has length \( V_0 \).
  • It rotates anticlockwise with angular velocity \( \omega \).
  • At \( t = 0 \), it makes angle \( \phi \) with the x-axis.
  • Its y-projection at any time gives \( v(t) \).
All phasors in a circuit rotate at the same angular frequency \( \omega \).
So we can "freeze" time and compare them at \( t = 0 \) — their relative positions (angles) give the phase differences, and their lengths give the amplitudes.

Root Mean Square (RMS) Values

The average of a sinusoid over a complete cycle is zero — so the average voltage or current is not a useful measure of power.

Instead, we use the RMS value, defined as the square root of the time-average of the square:

\[ V_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\langle v^2 \rangle} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T V_0^2\sin^2(\omega t)\,dt} \]

Since \( \langle \sin^2(\omega t) \rangle = 1/2 \):

\[ \boxed{V_{\text{rms}} = \frac{V_0}{\sqrt{2}}} \]
The RMS value of an AC voltage is the DC voltage that would produce the same average power dissipation in a resistor.
When we say "230 V mains supply," we mean \( V_{\text{rms}} = 230 \) V — the peak voltage is \( V_0 = 230\sqrt{2} \approx 325 \) V.

Use Pre-Calculated Values

A major advantage of knowing standard full-cycle averages—such as \( \langle \sin(\omega t) \rangle = 0 \) and \( \langle \sin^2(\omega t) \rangle = 1/2 \)—is that we do not need to perform integration every time we analyze a composite waveform.

Illustration

An alternating voltage is given by \( v(t) = V_0 + V_1 \sin(\omega t) \). Find its RMS value.

Solution

By definition, finding the RMS value requires taking the time-average of \( v(t)^2 \) over a full cycle and then taking the square root. Instead of integrating directly, we can expand the square and apply our pre-calculated averages.

First, square the instantaneous voltage:

\[ v(t)^2 = \big(V_0 + V_1 \sin(\omega t)\big)^2 = V_0^2 + V_1^2 \sin^2(\omega t) + 2 V_0 V_1 \sin(\omega t) \]

Taking the full-cycle time-average on both sides and using the linearity of the averaging operator (constants come outside \( \langle \cdot \rangle \)):

\[ \langle v^2 \rangle = \langle V_0^2 \rangle + V_1^2 \langle \sin^2(\omega t) \rangle + 2 V_0 V_1 \langle \sin(\omega t) \rangle \]

Using the standard full-cycle results \( \langle V_0^2 \rangle = V_0^2 \), \( \langle \sin^2(\omega t) \rangle = 1/2 \), and \( \langle \sin(\omega t) \rangle = 0 \):

\[ \langle v^2 \rangle = V_0^2 + \frac{V_1^2}{2} + 0 = V_0^2 + \frac{V_1^2}{2} \]

Thus, the RMS voltage is:

\[ V_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\langle v^2 \rangle} = \sqrt{V_0^2 + \frac{V_1^2}{2}} \]
