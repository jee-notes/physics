Phasor Representation

Rotating vectors that encode both amplitude and phase — making AC circuit analysis algebraic.

What is a Phasor?

A phasor is a rotating vector whose magnitude equals the amplitude (peak value) of a sinusoidal quantity, and whose angle with the positive x-axis equals the instantaneous phase. The projection of the phasor onto the y-axis gives the instantaneous value of the sinusoid at time \( t \).

For a voltage \( v = V_0\sin(\omega t + \phi) \):

  • The phasor has length \( V_0 \).
  • It rotates anticlockwise with angular velocity \( \omega \).
  • At \( t = 0 \), it makes angle \( \phi \) with the x-axis.
  • Its y-projection at any time gives \( v(t) \).
All phasors in a circuit rotate at the same angular frequency \( \omega \). So we can "freeze" time and compare them at \( t = 0 \) — their relative positions (angles) give the phase differences, and their lengths give the amplitudes. This is the power of phasor analysis.

Adding Sinusoids via Phasors

Adding two sinusoids of the same frequency but different phases cannot be done by simply adding their amplitudes. But the corresponding phasors can be added as vectors (tip-to-tail or parallelogram rule), and the resultant phasor gives the amplitude and phase of the sum.

Example: \( v_1 = V_{01}\sin(\omega t) \) and \( v_2 = V_{02}\sin(\omega t + 90°) \).

Phasor 1 is along the y-axis (magnitude \( V_{01} \)), phasor 2 is along the x-axis (magnitude \( V_{02} \), leading by \( 90° \)). Their sum:

\[ V_0 = \sqrt{V_{01}^2 + V_{02}^2}, \quad \phi = \arctan\left(\frac{V_{02}}{V_{01}}\right) \]

This is exactly the calculation needed for the voltage across R and L in series.

Phasor Diagrams for Circuit Elements

Resistor (R)

Current and voltage are in phase (\( \Delta\phi = 0 \)). The phasors \( V_R \) and \( I \) point in the same direction.

Inductor (L)

\( v_L = L\,di/dt \). If \( i = I_0\sin(\omega t) \), then \( v_L = \omega L I_0\cos(\omega t) = X_L I_0\sin(\omega t + 90°) \).

Voltage leads current by \( 90° \). The \( V_L \) phasor is \( 90° \) ahead of \( I \) phasor (anticlockwise).

Capacitor (C)

\( i = C\,dv/dt \). If \( v = V_0\sin(\omega t) \), then \( i = \omega C V_0\cos(\omega t) = I_0\sin(\omega t + 90°) \).

Current leads voltage by \( 90° \), i.e., voltage lags current by \( 90°\). The \( V_C \) phasor is \( 90° \) behind \( I \) phasor (clockwise).

Series RLC

Taking \( I \) as reference, the phasors are:

  • \( V_R \): along \( I \) (same direction).
  • \( V_L \): perpendicular, \( 90° \) ahead of \( I \).
  • \( V_C \): perpendicular, \( 90° \) behind \( I \).

Net reactive phasor \( = V_L - V_C \) (since they oppose). Resultant voltage:

\[ V_0 = \sqrt{V_R^2 + (V_L - V_C)^2} \]

Complex Number Representation

Phasors are naturally represented using complex numbers. The impedances of circuit elements are:

\[ Z_R = R, \quad Z_L = j\omega L = jX_L, \quad Z_C = \frac{1}{j\omega C} = -jX_C \]

For a series RLC circuit, the complex impedance is:

\[ Z = R + j(X_L - X_C) = R + j\left(\omega L - \frac{1}{\omega C}\right) \] \[ |Z| = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2}, \quad \angle Z = \arctan\!\left(\frac{X_L - X_C}{R}\right) \]

This makes AC circuit analysis equivalent to DC analysis with complex (phasor) quantities — exactly analogous to Ohm's law: \( \tilde{V} = \tilde{I} Z \).
