RL circuit

RL circuit and inductive impedance.

Concept

We consider a resistor \(R\) and an inductor \(L\) connected in series across an alternating voltage source:

\[ v_S(t) = V_p \sin(\omega t) \]

Our goal is to determine the steady-state AC current flowing through both components in series, as well as the instantaneous and peak potential differences across each individual component.

We will also investigate the power dynamics of the circuit.

Physically, a series \(RL\) circuit is realized by a choke coil—a coil formed by winding many turns of conducting wire to create an inductor.

Because the conducting wire itself possesses finite electrical resistance, a real choke coil behaves electrically as an inductor \(L\) in series with a resistor \(R\).

An ideal choke coil has zero winding resistance (\(R = 0\)).

Phasor Analysis and Impedance

Because the resistor and inductor are connected in series, the same instantaneous current flows through both elements. Let this steady-state current be:

\[ i(t) = I_p \sin(\omega t - \phi) \]

where \(I_p\) is the peak current and \(\phi\) is the phase angle by which the source voltage leads the current (to be determined).

Applying Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL) around the loop at every instant (using the passive sign convention \(v = V_A - V_B\) across each element):

\[ v_S(t) = v_R(t) + v_L(t) \]

From the individual \(V\text{--}I\) characteristics of \(R\) and \(L\) for a current of peak value \(I_p\):

  • Voltage across \(R\): \(v_R(t) = V_{R,p}\sin(\omega t - \phi)\), where \(V_{R,p} = I_p R\) (in phase with \(i(t)\)).
  • Voltage across \(L\): \(v_L(t) = V_{L,p}\sin(\omega t - \phi + 90^\circ)\), where \(V_{L,p} = I_p X_L = I_p(\omega L)\) (leads \(i(t)\) by \(90^\circ\)).

In the phasor plane, taking the current phasor \(\vec{I}\) as our reference direction:

  • The resistive voltage phasor \(\vec{V}_R\) points along \(\vec{I}\) with magnitude \(V_{R,p} = I_p R\).
  • The inductive voltage phasor \(\vec{V}_L\) points \(90^\circ\) anticlockwise from \(\vec{I}\) with magnitude \(V_{L,p} = I_p X_L\).

Since \(\vec{V}_R\) and \(\vec{V}_L\) are mutually perpendicular, the resultant source voltage phasor \(\vec{V}_S = \vec{V}_R + \vec{V}_L\) has peak magnitude:

\[ V_p = \sqrt{V_{R,p}^2 + V_{L,p}^2} = \sqrt{(I_p R)^2 + (I_p X_L)^2} = I_p \sqrt{R^2 + X_L^2} \]

Defining the inductive impedance \(Z\) of the series \(RL\) circuit as the total effective opposition to AC current:

\[ \boxed{Z = \sqrt{R^2 + X_L^2} = \sqrt{R^2 + (\omega L)^2}} \quad (\text{unit: } \Omega) \]

so that Ohm's law for peak and RMS values takes the compact form:

\[ V_p = I_p Z, \qquad V_{\text{rms}} = I_{\text{rms}} Z \]
Why Peak Voltages Do Not Add Algebraically:
At every instant, \(v_S(t) = v_R(t) + v_L(t)\) holds strictly. However, because \(v_R(t)\) and \(v_L(t)\) are \(90^\circ\) out of phase, they never reach their peaks at the same instant—so \(V_p \neq V_{R,p} + V_{L,p}\), and instead \(V_p = \sqrt{V_{R,p}^2 + V_{L,p}^2} \lt V_{R,p} + V_{L,p}\).

Current and Component Voltages

From the right-angled voltage phasor triangle, the angle \(\phi\) by which \(\vec{V}_S\) leads \(\vec{I}\) is given by:

\[ \tan\phi = \frac{V_{L,p}}{V_{R,p}} = \frac{X_L}{R} = \frac{\omega L}{R}, \qquad \cos\phi = \frac{R}{Z}, \qquad \sin\phi = \frac{X_L}{Z} \]

Since \(R \gt 0\) and \(X_L \gt 0\), the phase angle always lies in the range \(0^\circ \lt \phi \lt 90^\circ\)—meaning the source voltage leads the circuit current by \(\phi\) (equivalently, current lags source voltage by \(\phi\)).

For the given source \(v_S(t) = V_p\sin(\omega t)\), the steady-state current and individual element voltages are:

  • Circuit current: \[ i(t) = I_p\sin(\omega t - \phi), \qquad \text{where } I_p = \frac{V_p}{\sqrt{R^2 + (\omega L)^2}} \]
  • Voltage across resistor \(R\): \[ v_R(t) = (I_p R)\sin(\omega t - \phi) \]
  • Voltage across inductor \(L\): \[ v_L(t) = (I_p X_L)\sin(\omega t - \phi + 90^\circ) = (I_p \omega L)\cos(\omega t - \phi) \]

Power Dynamics in an RL Circuit

Using the passive sign convention (\(p = vi \gt 0\) for power absorbed), the instantaneous power supplied by the source is shared between the resistor and the inductor:

\[ p_S(t) = v_S(t)\,i(t) = v_R(t)\,i(t) + v_L(t)\,i(t) = p_R(t) + p_L(t) \]
  • In the resistor (\(R\)): \(p_R(t) = i^2(t)R \ge 0\) at all instants. The resistor continuously dissipates electrical energy as Joule heat, with a full-cycle average power: \[ \langle P_R \rangle = I_{\text{rms}}^2 R = V_{\text{rms}} I_{\text{rms}} \cos\phi \]
  • In the inductor (\(L\)): \(p_L(t) = v_L(t)\,i(t) = \frac{1}{2} V_{L,p} I_p \sin\big(2(\omega t - \phi)\big)\). During quarter-cycles when \(|i|\) increases, \(p_L \gt 0\) and the inductor absorbs energy to build its magnetic field (\(\frac{1}{2}Li^2\)); during quarter-cycles when \(|i|\) decreases, \(p_L \lt 0\) and it returns that stored energy back to the source. Over a complete cycle, the net average power absorbed by the ideal inductor is zero: \[ \langle P_L \rangle = 0 \]

Consequently, all net average power drawn from the source in a series \(RL\) circuit is dissipated exclusively in the resistance \(R\):

\[ \boxed{\langle P \rangle = \langle P_R \rangle = V_{\text{rms}} I_{\text{rms}} \cos\phi, \qquad \text{where } \cos\phi = \frac{R}{Z} = \frac{R}{\sqrt{R^2 + (\omega L)^2}}} \]
Why Choke Coils Are Used in AC Circuits:
To limit AC current in a circuit using a pure resistor, large amounts of electrical energy would be wasted as heat (\(I_{\text{rms}}^2 R\)). An ideal choke coil (\(R \approx 0, \, \cos\phi \approx 0\)) limits current via its inductive reactance \(X_L = \omega L\) while consuming virtually zero average power—making it far more efficient than a rheostat for controlling AC current.
