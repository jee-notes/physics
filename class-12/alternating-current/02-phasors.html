Phasor Representation

Rotating vectors that encode amplitude and phase.

Concept

A phasor is a mathematical tool used to add multiple sinusoidal quantities of the same frequency.

It is used heavily in AC circuit analysis—for example, when applying Kirchhoff's Current Law at a junction, \( i_1(t) + i_2(t) + i_3(t) + i_4(t) = 0 \), or Kirchhoff's Voltage Law around a loop where every term is a sinusoid.

While two sinusoids can be combined using standard trigonometric compound-angle identities, that approach becomes algebraically cumbersome when adding three or more sinusoids.

Phasor analysis solves this by mapping each sinusoid onto a 2D vector (or Argand) plane using polar coordinates—encoding only its amplitude and initial phase:

\[ v_1(t) = V_1 \sin(\omega t + \phi_1), \qquad v_2(t) = V_2 \sin(\omega t - \phi_2) \]

To compute \( v_1(t) + v_2(t) \), we represent them as 2D polar phasors \( \vec{V}_1 = (V_1, \phi_1) \) (often written \( V_1 \angle \phi_1 \)) and \( \vec{V}_2 = (V_2, -\phi_2) \), and add them like vectors.

A phasor is a rotating vector whose magnitude equals the amplitude (peak value) of a sinusoidal quantity, and whose angle with the positive x-axis equals the instantaneous phase.

The projection of the phasor onto the y-axis gives the instantaneous value of the sinusoid at time \( t \).

For a voltage \( v = V_0\sin(\omega t + \phi) \):

  • The phasor has length \( V_0 \).
  • It rotates anticlockwise with angular velocity \( \omega \).
  • At \( t = 0 \), it makes angle \( \phi \) with the x-axis.
  • Its y-projection at any time gives \( v(t) \).
All phasors in a circuit rotate at the same angular frequency \( \omega \).
So we can "freeze" time and compare them at \( t = 0 \) — their relative positions (angles) give the phase differences, and their lengths give the amplitudes.

Phasor Plane

The phasor plane can be viewed as an Argand plane or as a 2D vector plane where the four fundamental sinusoidal functions map onto the coordinate unit vectors (in the frozen \( t = 0 \) reference frame):

  • \( \sin(\omega t) \) represents \( \hat{i} \)
  • \( \cos(\omega t) = \sin(\omega t + 90^\circ) \) represents \( \hat{j} \)
  • \( -\sin(\omega t) = \sin(\omega t + 180^\circ) \) represents \( -\hat{i} \)
  • \( -\cos(\omega t) = \sin(\omega t - 90^\circ) \) represents \( -\hat{j} \)

Why Does This Work?

This mapping works directly because of the trigonometric compound-angle identity. Consider \( v(t) = V\sin(\omega t + \phi) \). Expanding via \( \sin(\alpha + \beta) = \sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta \):

\[ v(t) = V\sin(\omega t + \phi) = \big(V\cos\phi\big)\sin(\omega t) + \big(V\sin\phi\big)\cos(\omega t) \]

Identifying \( \sin(\omega t) \leftrightarrow \hat{i} \) and \( \cos(\omega t) \leftrightarrow \hat{j} \), and setting \( V_x = V\cos\phi \) and \( V_y = V\sin\phi \), we obtain the 2D phasor vector:

\[ V\sin(\omega t + \phi) \rightleftharpoons V_x\,\hat{i} + V_y\,\hat{j} \]

A positive phase angle always indicates an anticlockwise rotation from the reference axis.

Consider an example that highlights how the sign of the phase angle determines the sense of rotation: add \( v_1(t) = V_1\sin(\omega t + 30^\circ) \) and \( v_2(t) = V_2\sin(\omega t - 45^\circ) \).

Using the compound-angle expansion above, we express \( v_1(t) = V_1\sin(\omega t + 30^\circ) \) in phasor form as:

\[ V_1\sin(\omega t + 30^\circ) = V_1 \cos(30^\circ)\sin(\omega t) + V_1 \sin(30^\circ)\cos(\omega t) \] \[ \vec{V}_1 = \left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}V_1\right)\hat{i} + \left(\frac{1}{2}V_1\right)\hat{j} \]

In the phasor plane, \( \vec{V}_1 \) is a vector of magnitude:

\[ \boxed{|\vec{V}_1| = \sqrt{V_{1x}^2 + V_{1y}^2}}, \qquad V_{1x} = V_1 \cos(30^\circ), \quad V_{1y} = V_1 \sin(30^\circ) \]

The angle made by \( \vec{V}_1 \) with the positive X-axis follows from right-triangle geometry:

\[ \tan\phi_1 = \frac{V_{1y}}{V_{1x}} = \tan(30^\circ) \implies \phi_1 = +30^\circ \]

Thus, phasor \( \vec{V}_1 \) points at an angle of \( 30^\circ \) anticlockwise from the positive X-axis (\( \hat{i} \)).

Applying the same logic to \( v_2(t) = V_2\sin(\omega t - 45^\circ) \):

\[ V_2\sin(\omega t - 45^\circ) = V_2 \cos(-45^\circ)\sin(\omega t) + V_2 \sin(-45^\circ)\cos(\omega t) \] \[ \vec{V}_2 = \left(\frac{V_2}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\hat{i} + \left(-\frac{V_2}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\hat{j} \]

In the phasor plane, the angle made by \( \vec{V}_2 \) with the positive X-axis is:

\[ \tan\phi_2 = \frac{V_{2y}}{V_{2x}} = \tan(-45^\circ) \implies \phi_2 = -45^\circ, \qquad V_{2x} = V_2 \cos(-45^\circ), \quad V_{2y} = V_2 \sin(-45^\circ) \]

Thus, phasor \( \vec{V}_2 \) points at an angle of \( 45^\circ \) clockwise from the positive X-axis (\( \hat{i} \)).

To add both vectors and find the resultant voltage \( v_R(t) = V_R\sin(\omega t + \phi_R) \), we compute the resultant vector \( \vec{V}_R = \vec{V}_1 + \vec{V}_2 \) by summing components along each axis:

\[ V_{Rx} = V_{1x} + V_{2x} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}V_1 + \frac{V_2}{\sqrt{2}}, \qquad V_{Ry} = V_{1y} + V_{2y} = \frac{V_1}{2} - \frac{V_2}{\sqrt{2}} \]

The resultant amplitude is \( V_R = |\vec{V}_R| = \sqrt{V_{Rx}^2 + V_{Ry}^2} \), and the angle made by \( \vec{V}_R \) with the positive X-axis (\( \hat{i} \)) is given by:

\[ \tan\phi_R = \frac{V_{Ry}}{V_{Rx}} \]

Thus, the combined time-domain signal is \( v_R(t) = V_R\sin(\omega t + \phi_R) \), where the quadrant of \( (V_{Rx}, V_{Ry}) \) uniquely fixes \( \phi_R \).

Working with Other Axes

Next, let us verify why the remaining three basis sinusoids align with \( +\hat{j} \), \( -\hat{i} \), and \( -\hat{j} \) by converting each into standard \( +\sin(\omega t + \phi) \) form:

  • Positive Y-axis (\( +\hat{j} \)): Consider \( v_1(t) = V_1\cos(\omega t) \). Using \( \cos\theta = \sin(\theta + 90^\circ) \), we rewrite this equivalently as \( v_1(t) = V_1\sin(\omega t + 90^\circ) \). In the phasor plane, \( \vec{V}_1 \) points \( 90^\circ \) anticlockwise from the positive X-axis (\( \hat{i} \)), placing \( \cos(\omega t) \) along the positive Y-axis (\( \hat{j} \)).
  • Negative X-axis (\( -\hat{i} \)): Consider \( v_1(t) = -V_1\sin(\omega t) \). Using \( -\sin\theta = \sin(\theta + 180^\circ) \), we rewrite this as \( v_1(t) = V_1\sin(\omega t + 180^\circ) \). In the phasor plane, \( \vec{V}_1 \) points \( 180^\circ \) anticlockwise from \( \hat{i} \), placing \( -\sin(\omega t) \) along the negative X-axis (\( -\hat{i} \)).
  • Negative Y-axis (\( -\hat{j} \)): Consider \( v_1(t) = -V_1\cos(\omega t) \). First, \( \cos(\omega t) = \sin(\omega t + 90^\circ) \) rotates the phasor \( 90^\circ \) anticlockwise; then, applying the minus sign via \( -\sin\theta = \sin(\theta + 180^\circ) \) rotates it an additional \( 180^\circ \) anticlockwise: \[ -\cos(\omega t) = -\sin(\omega t + 90^\circ) = \sin\big((\omega t + 90^\circ) + 180^\circ\big) = \sin(\omega t + 270^\circ) \] Thus, \( v_1(t) = V_1\sin(\omega t + 270^\circ) \) points \( 270^\circ \) anticlockwise (or \( 90^\circ \) clockwise) from \( \hat{i} \), placing \( -\cos(\omega t) \) along the negative Y-axis (\( -\hat{j} \)).

Angles with Other Axes

We don't need to convert every phasor to an angle measured from the positive X-axis (\( +\hat{i} \)) just to resolve it into components—that standard reference angle is only needed at the very end to state the resultant initial phase \( \phi_R \).

Consider an example that shows how measuring angles directly from the nearest basis axis reveals geometry immediately: add \( v_1(t) = -V_1\sin(\omega t + 30^\circ) \) and \( v_2(t) = V_2\cos(\omega t - 60^\circ) \).

In the phasor plane, the vectors \( \vec{V}_1 \) and \( \vec{V}_2 \) are collinear and antiparallel! Let's see why:

Using the negative-sine shift from above:

\[ v_1(t) = -V_1\sin(\omega t + 30^\circ) = V_1\sin(\omega t + 30^\circ + 180^\circ) = V_1\sin(\omega t + 210^\circ) \]

Instead of treating \( \vec{V}_1 \) as \( 210^\circ \) anticlockwise from \( +\hat{i} \), we can read it directly off the \( -\sin(\omega t) \) axis: \( \vec{V}_1 \) is oriented \( 30^\circ \) anticlockwise from \( -\hat{i} \) (into the 3rd quadrant).

Similarly, for \( v_2(t) \):

\[ v_2(t) = V_2\cos(\omega t - 60^\circ) = V_2\sin(\omega t - 60^\circ + 90^\circ) = V_2\sin(\omega t + 30^\circ) \]

Instead of converting to \( 30^\circ \) anticlockwise from \( +\hat{i} \), we can read \( v_2(t) \) directly off the \( +\cos(\omega t) \) axis: \( \vec{V}_2 \) is oriented \( 60^\circ \) clockwise from \( +\hat{j} \) (into the 1st quadrant).

Since \( 60^\circ \) clockwise from \( +\hat{j} \) is \( 30^\circ \) anticlockwise from \( +\hat{i} \), \( \vec{V}_2 \) points directly opposite to \( \vec{V}_1 \) (\( 30^\circ \) anticlockwise from \( -\hat{i} \)). Thus, the angle between \( \vec{V}_1 \) and \( \vec{V}_2 \) is \( 180^\circ \), and their resultant magnitude is simply \( |V_2 - V_1| \) along the larger phasor!

Solved Example

Generalizing to \( N \) Phasors:
For any number of sinusoids \( v_k(t) = V_k\sin(\omega t + \phi_k) \) of the same frequency,
resolve each into \( V_{kx} = V_k\cos\phi_k \) and \( V_{ky} = V_k\sin\phi_k \),
sum each axis (\( V_{Rx} = \sum V_{kx} \), \( V_{Ry} = \sum V_{ky} \)),
and reconstruct \( V_R = \sqrt{V_{Rx}^2 + V_{Ry}^2} \) and \( \tan\phi_R = V_{Ry}/V_{Rx} \)

Rather than converting every cosine and negative term into sines via trigonometric identities, we can place each phasor directly on the phasor plane using the four basis directions (\( \pm\hat{i}, \pm\hat{j} \)) as local reference axes.

Illustration

Find the resultant of the following five sinusoidal voltages:

  • \( v_1(t) = V_1 \sin(\omega t - 60^\circ) \)
  • \( v_2(t) = V_2 \sin(\omega t + 60^\circ) \)
  • \( v_3(t) = V_3 \cos(\omega t - 60^\circ) \)
  • \( v_4(t) = V_4 \cos(\omega t + 60^\circ) \)
  • \( v_5(t) = -V_5 \cos(\omega t + 45^\circ) \)

Solution

Step 1: Locate each phasor directly relative to its trigonometric basis axis (remembering that a positive angle rotates anticlockwise and a negative angle rotates clockwise):

  • Around the \( +\hat{i} \) axis (\( +\sin\omega t \)):
    \( \vec{V}_1 \) is oriented \( 60^\circ \) clockwise from \( +\hat{i} \) (4th quadrant), while \( \vec{V}_2 \) is oriented \( 60^\circ \) anticlockwise from \( +\hat{i} \) (1st quadrant)—distributed symmetrically around the X-axis.
  • Around the \( +\hat{j} \) axis (\( +\cos\omega t \)):
    \( \vec{V}_3 \) is oriented \( 60^\circ \) clockwise from \( +\hat{j} \) (1st quadrant), while \( \vec{V}_4 \) is oriented \( 60^\circ \) anticlockwise from \( +\hat{j} \) (2nd quadrant)—distributed symmetrically around the Y-axis in the exact same way \( \vec{V}_1 \) and \( \vec{V}_2 \) are around the X-axis.
  • Around the \( -\hat{j} \) axis (\( -\cos\omega t \)):
    \( \vec{V}_5 \) starts along \( -\hat{j} \) and rotates \( 45^\circ \) anticlockwise from \( -\hat{j} \) (pointing into the 4th quadrant).

Step 2: Resolve each phasor directly from its local reference axis onto \( \hat{i} \) and \( \hat{j} \):

  • \( \vec{V}_1 \) (\( 60^\circ \) below \( +\hat{i} \)): \( \quad V_{1x} = +V_1\cos 60^\circ = \dfrac{V_1}{2}, \qquad V_{1y} = -V_1\sin 60^\circ = -\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}V_1 \)
  • \( \vec{V}_2 \) (\( 60^\circ \) above \( +\hat{i} \)): \( \quad V_{2x} = +V_2\cos 60^\circ = \dfrac{V_2}{2}, \qquad V_{2y} = +V_2\sin 60^\circ = +\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}V_2 \)
  • \( \vec{V}_3 \) (\( 60^\circ \) right of \( +\hat{j} \)): \( \quad V_{3x} = +V_3\sin 60^\circ = +\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}V_3, \qquad V_{3y} = +V_3\cos 60^\circ = \dfrac{V_3}{2} \)
  • \( \vec{V}_4 \) (\( 60^\circ \) left of \( +\hat{j} \)): \( \quad V_{4x} = -V_4\sin 60^\circ = -\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}V_4, \qquad V_{4y} = +V_4\cos 60^\circ = \dfrac{V_4}{2} \)
  • \( \vec{V}_5 \) (\( 45^\circ \) right of \( -\hat{j} \)): \( \quad V_{5x} = +V_5\sin 45^\circ = +\dfrac{V_5}{\sqrt{2}}, \qquad V_{5y} = -V_5\cos 45^\circ = -\dfrac{V_5}{\sqrt{2}} \)

Step 3: Sum the \( \hat{i} \) and \( \hat{j} \) components separately:

\[ V_{Rx} = \frac{V_1 + V_2}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}(V_3 - V_4) + \frac{V_5}{\sqrt{2}} \] \[ V_{Ry} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}(V_2 - V_1) + \frac{V_3 + V_4}{2} - \frac{V_5}{\sqrt{2}} \]

Step 4: Reconstruct the resultant voltage \( v_R(t) = V_R\sin(\omega t + \phi_R) \):

\[ \boxed{V_R = \sqrt{V_{Rx}^2 + V_{Ry}^2}, \qquad \tan\phi_R = \frac{V_{Ry}}{V_{Rx}}} \]

(Use the signs of \( V_{Rx} \) and \( V_{Ry} \) to fix the quadrant of \( \phi_R \).)
