Ferromagnetism, Domains, and Hysteresis

Permanent magnetism, memory, and the non-linear world of iron.

What is Ferromagnetism?

Ferromagnetism is the phenomenon behind permanent magnets. It occurs in materials where atomic magnetic dipoles interact strongly with their neighbours through a quantum mechanical effect called the exchange interaction. This interaction causes large regions — called magnetic domains — within the material to have all their atomic dipoles aligned in the same direction, even without any external field.

The result is an enormously large susceptibility (\( \chi_m \sim 10^3 \) to \( 10^5 \)) and the ability to retain strong magnetisation after the external field is removed.

Unlike paramagnetism (which is a competition with thermal energy), ferromagnetism arises from the quantum mechanical exchange interaction that locks neighbouring spins together. Thermal energy disrupts this only above the Curie temperature.

Magnetic Domains

A piece of unmagnetised iron is not made up of randomly oriented atomic dipoles — it contains regions called magnetic domains, each of which is spontaneously magnetised to saturation. Within a single domain, all dipoles point in the same direction. The domains themselves point in different directions, so the bulk material has zero net magnetisation.

The boundaries between domains are called domain walls (or Bloch walls). The energy of the system is minimised by a balance between the energy stored in the magnetic field outside the material and the energy of the domain walls.

When an external field is applied:

  • Weak field: Domain walls move — domains aligned with the field grow at the expense of others. This is reversible.
  • Stronger field: Domains not aligned begin to rotate toward the field. This stage is largely irreversible.
  • Saturation: All domains align with the field. The material is fully magnetised.

The Hysteresis Loop

If we plot magnetisation \( M \) (or \( B \)) against the applied field \( H \) as \( H \) is cycled from a large positive value to a large negative value and back, we trace out a closed curve called the hysteresis loop.

Key points on the hysteresis loop:

  • Saturation magnetisation \( M_s \): The maximum magnetisation reached when all domains are aligned.
  • Retentivity (Remanence) \( M_r \): The magnetisation remaining when \( H \) is reduced to zero. This is why permanent magnets exist.
  • Coercivity \( H_c \): The reverse field required to bring \( M \) back to zero. A measure of how "hard" the magnet is — how difficult it is to demagnetise.
  • Area of the loop: Represents energy dissipated as heat per cycle of magnetisation. This is the hysteresis loss.
The area enclosed by the hysteresis loop is proportional to the energy dissipated as heat per unit volume per magnetisation cycle. In transformers, this is an important energy loss that must be minimised.

Hard vs Soft Magnetic Materials

Property Soft (e.g., soft iron) Hard (e.g., Alnico, steel)
Coercivity \( H_c \) Low High
Retentivity \( M_r \) Moderate High
Hysteresis loop area Small (narrow loop) Large (wide loop)
Energy loss per cycle Low High
Application Transformer cores, electromagnet cores Permanent magnets, memory storage

The Curie Temperature

Above a critical temperature called the Curie temperature \( T_C \), the thermal energy becomes large enough to overcome the exchange interaction. The domain structure collapses, and the material becomes paramagnetic, obeying the Curie-Weiss Law:

\[ \chi_m = \frac{C}{T - T_C} \quad (T > T_C) \]

Below \( T_C \), the material is ferromagnetic. Above \( T_C \), it is paramagnetic. This transition is a phase transition.

  • Iron (Fe): \( T_C \approx 1043\text{ K} \approx 770°\text{C} \)
  • Nickel (Ni): \( T_C \approx 631\text{ K} \approx 358°\text{C} \)
  • Cobalt (Co): \( T_C \approx 1388\text{ K} \approx 1115°\text{C} \)
Heating a permanent magnet above its Curie temperature destroys its magnetism. This is why fire or strong heating is one of the classical methods of demagnetisation.

Methods of Demagnetisation

  • Heating above \( T_C \) — destroys domain structure.
  • Mechanical shock — jarring the magnet randomises domain orientation.
  • Alternating field of decreasing amplitude — gradually shrinks the hysteresis loop toward zero, leaving the material in an unmagnetised state.
