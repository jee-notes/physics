Magnetic Properties of Materials

Why different materials respond differently to an applied magnetic field.

Ewing's Molecular Theory of Magnetism

Weber first proposed the idea that magnetism arises from tiny magnetic dipoles present inside matter. Later, Ewing refined this concept and proposed the molecular theory of magnetism.

A current loop behaves as a magnetic dipole. Inside atoms, electrons move around the nucleus and also possess intrinsic spin. These motions of electric charge are equivalent to extremely small current loops. As a result, each atom of a magnetic material can behave like a tiny magnetic dipole.

According to Ewing, the molecules of a magnetic substance act like small magnets, often called molecular magnets. These molecular magnets exist whether the material is magnetised or not.

In an unmagnetised magnetic substance, the molecular magnets are randomly oriented. Many of them arrange themselves in closed chains, where the north pole of one molecular magnet attracts the south pole of its neighbour. In this way the magnetic effects of different molecules cancel one another, and the specimen as a whole shows no observable magnetism.

When the material becomes magnetised, these molecular magnets tend to align in the same direction. The north poles of most molecular magnets point toward one side of the specimen, while their south poles point toward the opposite side.

Because of this alignment, one end of the material behaves like a strong north pole and the other end behaves like a strong south pole.

Thus, according to Ewing's theory, the difference between a magnetised and an unmagnetised specimen lies only in the arrangement of the molecular magnets.

Modern physics explains these molecular magnets more precisely. The magnetic dipole moment of atoms arises mainly from the orbital motion and intrinsic spin of electrons. Ewing's theory therefore captures the correct qualitative picture — magnetisation occurs when many microscopic magnetic dipoles inside a material align in the same direction.

Magnetisation

When a material is placed in an external magnetic field, the magnetic dipoles inside the material tend to align with the field to some extent. As a result, the material develops a net magnetic dipole moment.

The magnetic dipole moment per unit volume of a material is called the magnetisation \( \vec{M} \):

\[ \vec{M} = \frac{\text{net magnetic moment}}{\text{volume}} \]

The SI unit of magnetisation is \( \text{A/m} \).

The magnetic field inside a material is therefore not simply the externally applied field. It is the combined effect of the external field and the magnetic field produced by the magnetised material itself.

To separate these contributions, we introduce the magnetic field intensity (or auxiliary field) \( \vec{H} \), defined so that

\[ \vec{B} = \mu_0(\vec{H} + \vec{M}) \]

Here \( \vec{B} \) is the total magnetic field inside the material.

Magnetic Susceptibility and Permeability

For many materials, the magnetisation produced is proportional to the applied magnetic field intensity \( \vec{H} \). Such materials are called linear magnetic materials.

\[ \vec{M} = \chi_m \vec{H} \]

The proportionality constant \( \chi_m \) is called the magnetic susceptibility. It is a dimensionless quantity that measures how strongly a material becomes magnetised when placed in a magnetic field.

Substituting this relation into the expression for \( \vec{B} \):

\[ \vec{B} = \mu_0(\vec{H} + \vec{M}) \] \[ \vec{B} = \mu_0(1 + \chi_m)\vec{H} \]

The quantity \( 1 + \chi_m \) is defined as the relative permeability \( \mu_r \):

\[ \mu_r = 1 + \chi_m \]

Thus the magnetic field inside the material can be written as

\[ \vec{B} = \mu_r \mu_0 \vec{H} = \mu \vec{H} \]

where

\[ \mu = \mu_r \mu_0 \]

is called the absolute permeability of the material.

In vacuum, no magnetisation occurs. Therefore

\[ \chi_m = 0, \quad \mu_r = 1 \]

and the relation between the fields becomes

\[ \vec{B} = \mu_0 \vec{H}. \]

The parameters \( \chi_m \) and \( \mu_r \) therefore measure how a material modifies the magnetic field relative to vacuum.

Three Classes of Magnetic Materials

The magnetic behaviour of a material is determined primarily by the sign and magnitude of its magnetic susceptibility \( \chi_m \).

Based on this property, materials are broadly classified into three categories.

Property Diamagnetic Paramagnetic Ferromagnetic
Magnetic susceptibility \( \chi_m \) Small and negative \( (-10^{-6} \text{ to } -10^{-5}) \) Small and positive \( (10^{-6} \text{ to } 10^{-3}) \) Large and positive \( (10^3 \text{ to } 10^5) \)
Relative permeability \( \mu_r \) Slightly less than 1 Slightly greater than 1 Very large (≫ 1)
Behaviour in magnetic field Weakly repelled Weakly attracted Strongly attracted
Temperature dependence Nearly independent of temperature Follows Curie's law Loses ferromagnetism above Curie temperature
Examples Bismuth, copper, water, gold Aluminium, sodium, liquid oxygen Iron, cobalt, nickel, Alnico
