Paramagnetism and Curie's Law

Permanent atomic dipoles that partially align — and why heat disrupts them.

What is Paramagnetism?

Paramagnetism occurs in materials whose atoms (or ions) have a permanent, non-zero magnetic dipole moment — due to unpaired electrons with uncompensated spin or orbital angular momentum. In the absence of an external field, these dipoles point in random directions due to thermal agitation, and the net magnetisation is zero.

When an external field \( \vec{B} \) is applied, the dipoles experience a torque \( \vec{\tau} = \vec{\mu} \times \vec{B} \) that tends to align them along \( \vec{B} \). The result is a weak positive magnetisation in the direction of the applied field.

Paramagnetism is a competition between two tendencies: the magnetic field tries to align the dipoles, while thermal energy tries to randomise them. At high temperature or low field, thermal agitation wins and the alignment is poor.

Characteristic Properties

  • Magnetic susceptibility \( \chi_m \) is small and positive: typically \( 10^{-6} \) to \( 10^{-3} \).
  • Relative permeability \( \mu_r \) is slightly greater than 1.
  • The material is weakly attracted toward regions of stronger field.
  • \( \chi_m \) decreases with temperature — as temperature rises, thermal agitation increasingly randomises the alignment.
  • Magnetisation disappears completely when the field is removed.

Curie's Law

Pierre Curie experimentally established that the magnetic susceptibility of a paramagnetic material is inversely proportional to its absolute temperature \( T \):

\[ \chi_m = \frac{C}{T} \]

where \( C \) is the Curie constant, characteristic of the material. This is Curie's Law.

It can be rewritten in terms of magnetisation \( M \) and applied field \( H \):

\[ M = \frac{C}{T} H \]

Physical meaning: At low \( T \), thermal energy is small relative to \( \mu B \), so alignment is good and \( \chi_m \) is large. At high \( T \), random thermal motion overwhelms alignment and \( \chi_m \) is small. The \( 1/T \) dependence is the quantitative expression of this.

Curie's Law breaks down when the field is very strong and the temperature very low — in this saturation regime, all dipoles are aligned and increasing the field no longer increases the magnetisation.

Examples

  • Aluminium (Al), Sodium (Na), Platinum (Pt)
  • Liquid Oxygen (O\(_2\)) — visibly attracted to magnets
  • Chromium (Cr) in some oxidation states
  • Many transition metal salts and rare earth compounds

Langevin Theory (Qualitative)

The classical Langevin model treats the paramagnetic material as a collection of permanent dipoles of magnitude \( \mu \) in thermal equilibrium at temperature \( T \). The ratio of magnetic potential energy to thermal energy determines the degree of alignment:

\[ x = \frac{\mu B}{k_B T} \]

When \( x \ll 1 \) (weak field, high temperature):

\[ M \approx \frac{n\mu^2}{3k_B T} B = \frac{C}{T} H \]

This gives Curie's Law with \( C = n\mu^2\mu_0 / (3k_B) \). The quantum mechanical treatment (using discrete spin levels) gives the same result in the high-temperature, low-field limit.
