Earth's Magnetism

The planet as a giant magnetic dipole — understanding its field and magnetic elements.

Earth as a Magnetic Dipole

The Earth behaves approximately like a giant magnetic dipole. Its magnetic field is believed to originate from convective motion of electrically conducting molten iron and nickel in the Earth's liquid outer core. These circulating currents act as a natural dynamo, continuously generating and sustaining the planet's magnetic field.

Although the field resembles that of a dipole, the magnetic axis of the Earth does not coincide exactly with its rotational axis.

  • The geographic north pole is defined by the Earth's rotation axis and is the point where the axis intersects the northern hemisphere.
  • The magnetic south pole of the Earth's dipole lies near the geographic north pole (currently located in northern Canada). This explains why the north-seeking end of a compass needle points roughly toward the geographic north direction — it is attracted to Earth's magnetic south pole.

The magnetic axis of the Earth is tilted by approximately \(11.5^\circ\) relative to the rotation axis. In addition, the magnetic poles slowly drift over time due to changes in the flow of molten material inside the Earth's core.

Earth's magnetic field is not perfectly stable. Geological studies of magnetised rocks show that the Earth's magnetic field has reversed polarity many times in the past. During such events the north and south magnetic poles exchange positions — a phenomenon known as geomagnetic reversal.

The Three Magnetic Elements

At any location on the Earth's surface, the magnetic field can be completely described using three measurable quantities known as the magnetic elements.

Magnetic Declination (\( \alpha \))

Magnetic declination is the horizontal angle between the geographic meridian (true north–south direction) and the magnetic meridian (the direction of the horizontal component of Earth's magnetic field).

The angle is measured east or west depending on whether the magnetic north lies east or west of geographic north.

Practical significance: A compass needle aligns with the magnetic meridian rather than the geographic meridian. Navigators and surveyors therefore correct compass readings using the local value of declination in order to determine the true geographic direction.

Magnetic Dip (Inclination) (\( \delta \))

Magnetic dip, or inclination, is the angle that the total magnetic field vector \( \vec{B} \) makes with the horizontal plane at a given location.

\[ \tan\delta = \frac{B_V}{B_H} \]

where \(B_H\) is the horizontal component and \(B_V\) is the vertical component of the Earth's magnetic field.

  • At the magnetic equator: \( \delta = 0^\circ \) (the field is completely horizontal).
  • At the magnetic poles: \( \delta = 90^\circ \) (the field is vertical).
  • In India: \( \delta \approx 30^\circ \text{–} 45^\circ \), depending on location.

A dip needle is a magnetised needle mounted so that it can rotate freely in the vertical plane. It aligns with the direction of the Earth's total magnetic field vector. In the northern hemisphere the needle points downward, while in the southern hemisphere it points upward.

Horizontal Component (\( B_H \))

The horizontal component \(B_H\) is the projection of the Earth's magnetic field onto the horizontal plane.

This component is responsible for the orientation of a compass needle. Since the needle is free to rotate only in the horizontal plane, it aligns itself with \(B_H\).

\[ B_H = B\cos\delta \]

Resolution of Earth's Field

Let \(B\) represent the total magnetic field at a location on the Earth's surface. This field can be resolved into two perpendicular components:

  • \(B_H\) — horizontal component
  • \(B_V\) — vertical component

If the dip angle is \( \delta \), the following relations hold:

\[ B_H = B\cos\delta, \qquad B_V = B\sin\delta \] \[ \tan\delta = \frac{B_V}{B_H} \] \[ B = \sqrt{B_H^2 + B_V^2} \]

These relations allow any one magnetic element to be calculated if the others are known.

For most locations on Earth the magnitude of the magnetic field lies roughly between

\[ B \approx 40\text{–}60 \ \mu\text{T} \]

Apparent Dip

A dip needle measures the correct value of dip only when it is placed in the vertical plane of the magnetic meridian.

If the needle is placed in a vertical plane that makes an angle \( \phi \) with the magnetic meridian, the angle it measures is called the apparent dip \( \delta' \).

The relation between the true dip and the apparent dip is

\[ \tan\delta' = \frac{\tan\delta}{\cos\phi} \]

Since \( \cos\phi \leq 1 \), we obtain

\[ \tan\delta' \geq \tan\delta \]

Therefore the apparent dip is always greater than or equal to the true dip. The apparent dip becomes maximum (\(90^\circ\)) when the vertical plane is perpendicular to the magnetic meridian.

Neutral Points

When a bar magnet is placed in the Earth's magnetic field, the resultant magnetic field may vanish at certain points in space. These points are called neutral points.

At a neutral point the magnetic field produced by the magnet exactly cancels the horizontal component of the Earth's magnetic field \(B_H\).

  • If the magnet is placed in the north–south direction with its north pole pointing toward geographic north, the neutral points lie on the equatorial line of the magnet.
  • If the magnet is reversed so that its south pole faces geographic north, the neutral points lie on the axial line.

For example, at a neutral point located on the equatorial line of the magnet,

\[ \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{M}{(r^2+l^2)^{3/2}} = B_H \]

which expresses the condition that the magnetic field of the magnet balances the Earth's horizontal magnetic field.
