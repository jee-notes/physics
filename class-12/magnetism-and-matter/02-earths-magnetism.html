Earth's Magnetism

The planet as a giant magnetic dipole — understanding its field and elements.

Earth as a Magnetic Dipole

The Earth behaves approximately as a magnetic dipole. Its magnetic field is believed to originate from convective currents of molten iron and nickel in its liquid outer core — a natural dynamo generating a slowly drifting magnetic field.

The Earth's magnetic poles are not at the same location as its geographic poles:

  • The geographic north pole is the end of Earth's rotation axis in the northern hemisphere.
  • The magnetic south pole of Earth's dipole lies near the geographic north pole (in northern Canada). This is why the north-seeking end of a compass — which we call the north pole of the compass — is actually attracted toward Earth's magnetic south pole.

The two axes are inclined at approximately \( 11.5° \) to each other and the positions of the magnetic poles drift slowly over geological time.

Earth's magnetic field is not static. Paleomagnetic studies of rock samples show that the field has reversed its polarity hundreds of times over geological history — a phenomenon called geomagnetic reversal.

The Three Magnetic Elements

The Earth's magnetic field at any location on the surface is completely described by three quantities called the magnetic elements:

1. Magnetic Declination (\( \alpha \))

The horizontal angle between the geographic meridian (true north) and the magnetic meridian (direction of the horizontal component of Earth's field) at a given location. It is measured in degrees east or west.

Practical significance: A compass needle points toward magnetic north, not geographic north. Navigators must add or subtract the local declination to find the true bearing.

2. Magnetic Dip (Inclination) (\( \delta \))

The angle that the total magnetic field vector \( \vec{B} \) makes with the horizontal plane at a given location:

\[ \tan\delta = \frac{B_V}{B_H} \]
  • At the magnetic equator: \( \delta = 0° \) (field is horizontal).
  • At the magnetic poles: \( \delta = 90° \) (field is vertical).
  • In India: \( \delta \approx 30°\text{–}45° \) depending on location.
A dip needle (a magnetised needle free to rotate in the vertical plane) aligns with the total field vector, pointing downward in the northern hemisphere and upward in the southern hemisphere.

3. Horizontal Component (\( B_H \))

The component of the Earth's total field in the horizontal plane. It is the component that governs the deflection of a compass needle:

\[ B_H = B\cos\delta \]

Resolution of Earth's Field

Let \( B \) be the total field magnitude at a location, \( B_H \) the horizontal component, and \( B_V \) the vertical component. The dip angle is \( \delta \). Then:

\[ B_H = B\cos\delta, \quad B_V = B\sin\delta \] \[ \tan\delta = \frac{B_V}{B_H}, \quad B = \sqrt{B_H^2 + B_V^2} \]

These four relations are used to calculate any magnetic element given the others. The values for a typical location in India: \( B \approx 40\text{–}60 \;\mu\text{T} \), \( \delta \approx 30° \text{–}45° \).

Apparent Dip

If a dip needle is placed not in the magnetic meridian but in a vertical plane making angle \( \phi \) with it, the apparent dip \( \delta' \) it shows is related to the true dip \( \delta \) and \( \phi \) by:

\[ \tan\delta' = \frac{\tan\delta}{\cos\phi} \]

Since \( \cos\phi \leq 1 \), we have \( \tan\delta' \geq \tan\delta \) — the apparent dip is always greater than or equal to the true dip, and is maximum (\( 90° \)) in a vertical plane perpendicular to the magnetic meridian.

Neutral Points

When a bar magnet is placed in Earth's field, the resultant field is zero at certain points called neutral points. At these points, the field of the magnet exactly cancels Earth's horizontal component \( B_H \).

  • Magnet pointing north–south (north pole facing geographic north): neutral points lie on the equatorial line of the magnet (one on each side, equidistant from its centre).
  • Magnet pointing north–south (south pole facing north): neutral points lie on the axial line of the magnet.

At a neutral point where the equatorial field equals \( B_H \):

\[ \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{M}{(r^2+l^2)^{3/2}} = B_H \]
