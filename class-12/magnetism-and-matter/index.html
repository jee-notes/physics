Magnetism and Matter

From bar magnets to ferromagnets — the deep structure of magnetic matter.

Conceptual Overview

Every material by nature has a magnetic character. This is because magnetic and electric characters are due to charge, a fundamental property of matter. But, some materials are strongly magnetic, some weakly, and some are actively repelled by a magnetic field. This chapter explains why — using the concept of the magnetic dipole as the fundamental building block of all magnetic matter.

A bar magnet, a current loop, a spinning electron — these are all magnetic dipoles. The macroscopic magnetic behavior of any material is determined by how these microscopic dipoles align, partially align or oppose an applied magnetic field.

The Bar Magnet as a Magnetic Dipole

A bar magnet is the macroscopic embodiment of a magnetic dipole. Its two poles — north and south — are inseparable. Unlike electric charges, no isolated magnetic monopole has ever been observed. This is the content of Gauss's Law for Magnetism:

\[ \oint \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{A} = 0 \]

The magnetic field of a bar magnet outside the magnet is identical in form to the electric field of an electric dipole. On the axial line (along the dipole axis), the field points from south to north. On the equatorial line (perpendicular bisector), it points from north to south — opposite to the dipole direction.

Earth as a Magnet

The Earth itself behaves like a giant magnetic dipole. Its geographic and magnetic poles do not coincide — the magnetic south pole lies near the geographic north pole. Three quantities called the magnetic elements completely describe the Earth's field at any location:

  • Magnetic Declination — the horizontal angle between geographic north and magnetic north.
  • Magnetic Dip (Inclination) — the angle the total field vector makes with the horizontal.
  • Horizontal Component \( B_H \) — the component of Earth's field in the horizontal plane.

These three elements are related by: \( \tan I = B_V / B_H \) and \( B = \sqrt{B_H^2 + B_V^2} \).

Classification of Magnetic Materials

When a material is placed in an external magnetic field \( \vec{B}_0 \), it develops a magnetisation \( \vec{M} \) — a magnetic dipole moment per unit volume. The material is characterised by its magnetic susceptibility \( \chi_m \):

\[ \vec{M} = \chi_m \vec{H} \]

The total field inside the material is \( \vec{B} = \mu_0(\vec{H} + \vec{M}) = \mu_0(1 + \chi_m)\vec{H} = \mu_r \mu_0 \vec{H} \).

Based on the sign and magnitude of \( \chi_m \), all materials fall into three classes:

  • Diamagnetic — \( \chi_m \) is small and negative; the material is weakly repelled by a magnet.
  • Paramagnetic — \( \chi_m \) is small and positive; the material is weakly attracted.
  • Ferromagnetic — \( \chi_m \) is large and positive (\( \sim 10^3 \to 10^5 \)); the material is strongly attracted and can be permanently magnetised.

Core Topics

  • Bar Magnet — Dipole moment, axial and equatorial fields, torque and potential energy.
  • Gauss's Law for Magnetism — No magnetic monopoles; total magnetic flux through any closed surface is zero.
  • Earth's Magnetism — Magnetic elements: declination, dip, horizontal component.
  • Magnetisation and Susceptibility — \( \vec{M} \), \( \chi_m \), \( \mu_r \), and the \( \vec{B}\text{-}\vec{H}\text{-}\vec{M} \) relationship.
  • Diamagnetism — Lenz's law at the atomic level; negative susceptibility.
  • Paramagnetism — Curie's Law and thermal alignment of dipoles.
  • Ferromagnetism — Domains, hysteresis, retentivity, and coercivity.
