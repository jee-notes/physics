Magnetic Field of a Dipole

Magnetic field produced by a dipole along its axial and equatorial lines.

Bar Magnet Setup as Electric dipole

Consider a bar magnet of magnetic dipole moment \( M \) and half-length \( l \). The magnet can be modelled using the pole model as two magnetic poles of equal strength \( +m \) and \( -m \), separated by a distance \(2l\).

The north pole is located at \(+l\) from the centre and the south pole at \(-l\). Using this model we calculate the magnetic field at two important locations around the magnet:

  • Axial point \(P_1\): a point lying on the axis of the magnet at a distance \(r\) from the centre.
  • Equatorial point \(P_2\): a point on the perpendicular bisector (equatorial plane) of the magnet at a distance \(r\) from the centre.

These two locations are particularly useful because they represent directions parallel and perpendicular to the dipole moment. The magnetic field at these points is obtained by calculating the field produced by each pole separately and then adding the contributions vectorially.

Field at an Axial Point

Consider a point \(P_1\) on the axis of the magnet at a distance \(r\) from its centre.

The north pole produces a magnetic field directed away from itself, while the south pole produces a field directed toward itself. At the axial point these fields lie along the same straight line but act in opposite directions.

The net magnetic field is obtained by subtracting the two contributions. The resulting expression for the magnetic field on the axial line is

\[ \vec{B}_{\text{axial}} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{2Mr}{(r^2 - l^2)^2} \]

where \(M = m(2l)\) is the magnetic dipole moment of the magnet.

For points far from the magnet (\(r \gg l\)), the magnet behaves like a short dipole. In this limit

\[ (r^2 - l^2)^2 \approx r^4 \]

and the expression simplifies to

\[ \boxed{B_{\text{axial}} \approx \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{2M}{r^3}} \]

The direction of the magnetic field at the axial point is along the axis from the south pole toward the north pole. This is the same direction as the magnetic dipole moment \( \vec{M} \).

The structure of the magnetic dipole field is mathematically identical to that of an electric dipole field. For an electric dipole,

\[ E_{\text{axial}}=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2p}{r^3} \]

while for a magnetic dipole

\[ B_{\text{axial}}=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{2M}{r^3}. \]

The spatial dependence and geometry are identical; only the physical constants differ.

Field at an Equatorial Point

Now consider a point \(P_2\) on the equatorial plane of the magnet at a distance \(r\) from the centre.

At this location the fields produced by the two poles are symmetrically oriented. The components perpendicular to the axis cancel by symmetry, while the components along the axis add together.

The resulting magnetic field at the equatorial point is

\[ \vec{B}_{\text{equatorial}} = -\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{M}{(r^2 + l^2)^{3/2}} \]

For points far from the magnet (\(r \gg l\)) the expression simplifies to

\[ \boxed{B_{\text{equatorial}} \approx \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{M}{r^3}} \]

The negative sign indicates that the magnetic field at the equatorial point is directed opposite to the dipole moment \( \vec{M} \). In other words, along the equatorial line the field points from the north pole toward the south pole.

A useful rule emerges from these results. Along the axial line the magnetic field of a dipole is in the same direction as the dipole moment, while along the equatorial line it is in the opposite direction. This rule allows the direction of the dipole field to be determined quickly without repeating the full pole calculation.

Key Comparison

Property Axial Field Equatorial Field
Magnitude (short magnet) \( \dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\dfrac{2M}{r^3} \) \( \dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\dfrac{M}{r^3} \)
Ratio \( B_{\text{axial}} = 2\,B_{\text{equatorial}} \) at the same distance \(r\)
Direction relative to \( \vec{M} \) Parallel Antiparallel
Distance dependence \(1/r^3\) \(1/r^3\)
