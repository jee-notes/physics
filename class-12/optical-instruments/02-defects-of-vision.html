Defects of Vision

Refractive errors of the eye and spectacle correction.

Clear Vision

Before studying defects of vision, we first need to define what is meant by clear vision.

For a normal human eye, clear vision means that the accommodation mechanism can adjust the focal length of the eye's optical system so that light from an object is brought to focus on the fixed retinal screen, producing a sharp image.

Since the ability of the eye to change its focal length is limited, there are two limiting object positions that define the range of clear, unassisted vision.

  • Far Point (\(\text{FP}\)): The farthest point at which an object can be positioned and still form a sharp image on the retina. At the far point, the ciliary muscles are fully relaxed and the eye has its maximum focal length (\(f=f_{\max}\)). For a normal eye, \(\text{FP}\to\infty\) .
  • Near Point (\(\text{NP}\) or \(D\)): The closest point at which an object can be positioned and still form a sharp image on the retina. At the near point, the ciliary muscles are at maximum contraction and the eye has its minimum focal length (\(f=f_{\min}\)). For a normal adult eye, \(\text{NP}=D=25\) cm.

Therefore, a normal eye can see an object clearly without any external optical aid when the object lies between its near point and far point.

A defect of vision (refractive error) occurs when the eye's available range of focal lengths cannot produce a sharp image on the fixed retina for the required object distance. This can occur because:

  1. The converging power of the eye's refracting system is too high or too low.
  2. The axial length of the eyeball is longer or shorter than normal.

A spectacle lens is an optical instrument used to correct such a defect. It does not alter the retina or the eye's internal optical geometry. Instead, it acts as an intermediate optical element before the eye, modifying the rays coming from the object.

The spectacle lens forms a virtual image of the object at a position that lies within the defective eye's range of clear vision. The eye can then focus this virtual image sharply on its retina.

Above is the principle on which a corrective lens works.
To determine the corrective lens required, we need to know the defective eye's limiting point:
  • For a near-vision defect, we need the defective near point \(d_{\text{NP}}\).
  • For a distance-vision defect, we need the defective far point \(d_{\text{FP}}\).
For a normal eye, the corresponding ideal limits are: \[ \boxed{\text{NP}=D=25\,\text{cm}} \] and \[ \boxed{\text{FP}=\infty} \] The corrective lens is then designed to transform the required object position into a virtual image at the defective eye's limiting point, allowing the eye to focus that image on the retina.

Myopia (Near-Sightedness)

In a myopic eye, close objects are seen clearly, but distant objects appear blurred. This defect is also called near-sightedness.

The far point of a myopic eye is not at infinity; it has shifted inward to a finite distance \(d_{\text{FP}} \lt \infty\).

\[ \boxed{\text{Myopic Range of Distinct Vision: } d_{\text{NP}} \le |u| \le d_{\text{FP}} \lt \infty} \] \[ \boxed{\text{Normal Range of Distinct Vision: } d_{\text{NP}} \le |u| \lt \infty} \]

Optical Mechanism

When parallel light rays from a distant object (\(u\to-\infty\)) enter the fully relaxed myopic eye, the eye's optical system converges the rays too strongly.

Thus, the maximum focal length \(f_{\max}\) of the myopic eye is too small for the fixed retinal position, causing the real image to form in front of the retina.

By the time the rays reach the retina, they have already crossed and are diverging again, producing a blurred image rather than a sharp point image.

Root Physical Causes: Myopia can arise from an excessively powerful refracting system or from an eyeball that is too long along the visual axis. These physical conditions can have a hereditary component and can also be associated with environmental factors such as prolonged screen viewing.

  • Excessive converging power: The converging power of the eye's optical system is too large, so \(f_{\max}\) is too small.
  • Elongation of the eyeball: The distance between the eye's optical system and the retina is abnormally large.

Correction Mechanism

To see a distant object (\(u\to-\infty\)) with the eye fully relaxed, the corrective lens must diverge the incoming parallel rays such that they appear to originate from the defective far point (\(d_{\text{FP}}\)) of the eye.

Thus, for the spectacle lens, \[ u=-\infty \qquad v=-d_{\text{FP}} \] Applying the thin-lens equation,

\[ \frac{1}{f}=\frac{1}{v}-\frac{1}{u}=\frac{1}{-d_{\text{FP}}}-\frac{1}{-\infty} \] \[ \boxed{f=-d_{\text{FP}}} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \boxed{P=-\frac{1}{d_{\text{FP}}}} \]

Here \(d_{\text{FP}}>0\) because it represents a physical distance. Therefore, \(f \lt 0\) and \(P \lt 0\). Hence, myopia is corrected using a concave (diverging) lens.

Hypermetropia (Far-Sightedness)

In a hypermetropic eye, distant objects are seen clearly, but close objects appear blurred. This defect is also called far-sightedness.

The near point of a hypermetropic eye is not at the normal \(25\text{ cm}\) ; it has shifted outward to a finite distance \(d_{\text{NP}} > 25\text{ cm}\) .

\[ \boxed{\text{Hypermetropic Range of Distinct Vision: } d_{\text{NP}} \le |u| \lt \infty \quad d_{\text{NP}} > 25\text{ cm}} \] \[ \boxed{\text{Normal Range of Distinct Vision: } 25\text{ cm} \le |u| \lt \infty} \]

Optical Mechanism

When an object is placed at the standard near point (\(u=-25\text{ cm}\)), the hypermetropic eye, even at maximum accommodation, cannot converge the rays sufficiently.

Thus, the minimum focal length \(f_{\min}\) of the hypermetropic eye is too large for the fixed retinal position, causing the real image to form behind the retina.

By the time the rays reach the retina, they have not yet converged to the image point, producing a blurred image rather than a sharp point image.

Root Physical Causes: Hypermetropia can arise from an insufficiently powerful refracting system or from an eyeball that is too short along the visual axis. These physical conditions can arise from the natural structure of the eye.

  • Insufficient converging power: The converging power of the eye's optical system is too small, so \(f_{\min}\) is too large.
  • Shortening of the eyeball: The distance between the eye's optical system and the retina is abnormally small.

Correction Mechanism

To see an object placed at the standard near point (\(u=-25\text{ cm}\)), the corrective lens must converge the incoming rays such that they appear to originate from the defective near point (\(d_{\text{NP}}\)) of the eye.

Thus, for the spectacle lens, \[ u=-25\text{ cm} \qquad v=-d_{\text{NP}} \] Applying the thin-lens equation,

\[ \frac{1}{f}=\frac{1}{v}-\frac{1}{u}=\frac{1}{-d_{\text{NP}}}-\frac{1}{-25} \] \[ \frac{1}{f}=\frac{1}{D}-\frac{1}{d_{\text{NP}}} \qquad P=\frac{1}{25}-\frac{1}{d_{\text{NP}}} \]

In numerical problems, \(D\) is used in general for the normal near point. For a normal adult eye, \(D=25\text{ cm}\). When calculating optical power, however, all distances must be expressed in metres.

\[ \boxed{f = \frac{D d_{\text{NP}}}{d_{\text{NP}} - D}} \]

Here \(d_{\text{NP}}>25\text{ cm}\). Therefore, \[ \frac{1}{25}>\frac{1}{d_{\text{NP}}} \] so \(f>0\) and \(P>0\). Hence, hypermetropia is corrected using a convex (converging) lens.

Summary of Correction Mechanism

A defective eye has a fixed retina but an optical system whose available focal range cannot handle certain object positions.

A corrective lens is placed before the eye as an intermediate optical element. It does not physically alter the defective eye. Instead, it changes the rays coming from the object so that the object forms a virtual image at a position that the defective eye can focus.

\[ \boxed{\text{object at inaccessible position}\;\xrightarrow{\text{corrective lens}}\;\text{virtual image at accessible position}} \]

Thus, the corrective lens effectively transforms the object position required by a normal eye into an image position accessible to the defective eye.

Presbyopia and Astigmatism

Presbyopia (Aging Eye):

With increasing age, the crystalline lens gradually loses its elastic deformability, while the ciliary muscles also lose some of their ability to contract. As a result, the power of accommodation decreases, and the near point gradually recedes beyond the normal \(25\text{ cm}\).

The resulting difficulty in focusing on nearby objects is similar to that seen in hypermetropia, but the physical cause is different. Hypermetropia is associated with an insufficiently powerful optical system or a shortened eyeball, whereas presbyopia is primarily a consequence of the loss of accommodation with age.

Presbyopia is corrected using a convex reading lens, which provides the additional converging power required for near vision.

If a person has both myopia and presbyopia, the eye has difficulty with both distance vision and near vision. A bifocal lens can provide both corrections in a single spectacle:

  • Upper segment: Concave (diverging) lens for distance vision.
  • Lower segment: Convex (converging) lens for near vision.

Astigmatism (Non-Spherical Curvature):

Astigmatism is an optical defect caused when the cornea or crystalline lens does not have the same curvature in all directions.

Its curvature, and therefore its optical power, differs along different meridians.

In a real human eye, the cornea contributes \(\approx 40\text{ D}\) (fixed) and the crystalline lens contributes \(\approx 20\text{ D}\) (variable, adding \(+4\text{ D}\) during accommodation).
Thus, a major part of the eye's converging power comes from the cornea. A transparent cornea is therefore essential for clear vision, and severe corneal damage can be treated, in suitable cases, using a donor corneal transplant.
For sharp imaging, the cornea should have the appropriate smooth, approximately spherical curvature.

We studied that a lens is bounded by two curved surfaces and that their curvatures together determine its optical power.

In astigmatism, the curvature of the eye's refracting system is different along different meridians. Different meridians therefore have different optical powers and do not bring light to focus at the same position along the optical axis.

Astigmatism is corrected using a cylindrical lens with a suitable axis and power. A cylindrical lens provides refractive power selectively along one direction, allowing the unequal optical powers of the eye to be compensated.

The property of a cylindrical surface is used.
Cylindrical surface provides curvature along one dimension only and flatness along the axis.
By testing, it can be determined along which dimension the corrective curvature is required and by how much.

Examples

Problem 1: Correcting Far Point Limitation (Myopia)

A student cannot see objects clearly if they are situated beyond \(1.5\text{ m}\) from the eye. Calculate the nature, focal length, and optical power of the corrective lens required to restore normal distant vision.

Objective: An object at infinity (\(u = -\infty\)) must produce a virtual image at the student's actual far point (\(v = -1.5\text{ m} = -150\text{ cm}\)).

\[ \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} \implies \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{-1.5} - \frac{1}{-\infty} = -\frac{1}{1.5} \] \[ f = -1.5\text{ m} = -150\text{ cm} \] \[ P = \frac{1}{f(\text{in m})} = \frac{1}{-1.5} = -\frac{2}{3}\text{ D} \approx -0.67\text{ D} \]

Result: A concave lens of focal length \(-1.5\text{ m}\) and power \(-0.67\text{ D}\) is required.

Problem 2: Correcting Near Point Limitation (Hypermetropia)

The near point of a hypermetropic person is \(75\text{ cm}\). Find the focal length and power of the spectacle lens needed to enable distinct reading of a book held at the standard near point of \(25\text{ cm}\).

Objective: An object placed at the standard distance (\(u = -25\text{ cm} = -0.25\text{ m}\)) must form a virtual image at the person's defective near point (\(v = -75\text{ cm} = -0.75\text{ m}\)).

\[ \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} \implies \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{-75} - \left(-\frac{1}{25}\right) = -\frac{1}{75} + \frac{1}{25} \] \[ \frac{1}{f} = \frac{-1 + 3}{75} = \frac{2}{75}\text{ cm}^{-1} \implies f = +\frac{75}{2}\text{ cm} = +37.5\text{ cm} = +0.375\text{ m} \] \[ P = \frac{1}{f(\text{in m})} = \frac{1}{0.375} = +\frac{8}{3}\text{ D} \approx +2.67\text{ D} \]

Result: A convex lens of focal length \(+37.5\text{ cm}\) and power \(+2.67\text{ D}\) is required.
