Reflecting Telescope

Using mirrors to overcome the limitations of large lenses.

Cassegrain and Newtonian Designs

Reflecting telescopes use a large concave parabolic mirror as the primary objective instead of a lens. The two most common designs are:

  • Newtonian Telescope: Light from a distant object strikes a large concave primary mirror. Before converging to the focus, a small plane mirror placed at an angle of 45° reflects the light to the side of the tube, where an eyepiece lens magnifies the image.
  • Cassegrain Telescope: Light reflects off the large concave primary mirror towards a smaller convex secondary mirror. The secondary mirror reflects the light back through a small hole in the center of the primary mirror, where the eyepiece is located. This design allows for a very long effective focal length in a compact tube.
Most modern large astronomical observatories (like the Hubble Space Telescope and James Webb Space Telescope) use reflecting designs because of their structural and optical advantages.

Advantages over Refracting Telescopes

Reflecting telescopes are preferred over refracting telescopes for several critical reasons:

  1. No Chromatic Aberration: Mirrors reflect all wavelengths of light equally, whereas lenses refract different colors differently, causing colored fringes around images (chromatic aberration).
  2. Less Spherical Aberration: By using a parabolic primary mirror instead of a spherical one, spherical aberration can be completely eliminated.
  3. Mechanical Stability: A huge mirror can be supported from behind across its entire back surface. A large lens can only be supported at its edges, causing it to sag under its own weight and distort the image.
  4. Easier Manufacturing: Only one surface of a mirror needs to be polished and silvered, whereas a lens requires two perfectly ground and polished surfaces. Furthermore, the glass in a mirror does not need to be perfectly optically transparent.

Resolving Power of a Telescope

The resolving power of a telescope is its ability to distinguish two closely spaced stars as separate objects. The theoretical limit is given by the Rayleigh criterion.

\[ \text{Resolving Power} = \frac{1}{\Delta \theta} = \frac{D}{1.22 \lambda} \] Where:
  • \( \Delta \theta \) is the minimum angular separation between two point objects to be just resolved.
  • \( D \) is the diameter (aperture) of the objective lens or primary mirror.
  • \( \lambda \) is the wavelength of light.

A larger diameter \( D \) not only gathers more light (making faint objects visible) but also directly increases the resolving power, allowing finer details to be seen. This is why astronomers build increasingly larger telescopes.
