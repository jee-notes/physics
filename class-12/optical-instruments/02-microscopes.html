Microscopes

Peering into the microscopic world by maximizing angular magnification.

Introduction to Microscopes

A microscope is an optical instrument used to view very small, nearby objects. When an object is too small to see with the naked eye, bringing it closer to the eye increases the angle it subtends, making it appear larger. However, the human eye cannot focus on objects closer than the near point \( D \) (least distance of distinct vision, typically \( 25 \text{ cm} \)).

Microscopes solve this problem by using lenses to produce an enlarged virtual image of the object. This image is formed at a distance greater than or equal to \( D \), allowing the eye to comfortably focus on it while enjoying a much larger visual angle.

There are two main types of microscopes: the simple microscope (a single converging lens) and the compound microscope (a combination of two converging lenses for much higher magnification).

Magnifying Power and Angular Magnification

The performance of a microscope is quantified by its Magnifying Power or Angular Magnification, denoted by \( m \).

Definition of Magnifying Power (\( m \)): \[ m = \frac{\theta_i}{\theta_o} \] Where:
  • \( \theta_i \) is the visual angle subtended by the image at the eye when using the instrument.
  • \( \theta_o \) is the visual angle subtended by the object at the unaided eye when placed at the least distance of distinct vision (\( D \)).

Since the angles are usually small, we can approximate \( \tan \theta \approx \theta \), which simplifies the calculation of magnifying power using ray geometry. Note that magnifying power is a ratio of angles, distinguishing it from linear magnification (\( v/u \)), though the two are related.
