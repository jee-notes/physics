Telescopes

Far objects made visible by gathering light and increasing visual angle.

Telescope

Unlike microscopes, telescopes are not ordinarily classified as simple and compound instruments in the same sense.

A telescope is designed to observe large, very distant objects like planets, stars, and galaxies.

The objects are already large, and their object distances are fixed. Because of their vast distance, they subtend a very small visual angle at the eye, appearing tiny.

Thus, a telescope's design is that it contains an objective piece whose primary job is to gather as much light as possible from these distant objects and form an intermediate image.

Then the eye-piece acts as a simple microscope and creates a final image that subtends a larger visual angle at the eye than the object would subtend when viewed directly by the unaided eye.

In a microscope, the objective has a short focal length so that it can produce large linear magnification of a nearby small object.
In a telescope, the objective is designed with a large aperture to gather more light and a large focal length to provide high angular magnification when combined with the short-focal-length eyepiece.

Single Lens and a Distant Object

A single convex lens can itself form an image of a very distant object. For an object at a very large distance, such as the Sun, the incoming rays are approximately parallel and the lens forms a real image in its focal plane.

The Sun is extremely far from the Earth, but it is not a point object. It has a finite angular diameter of approximately \[ \boxed{\theta_\odot \approx 0.53^\circ} \] or \[ \theta_\odot \approx 9.3 \times 10^{-3}\text{ rad} \] Thus, the sunlight reaching the lens consists of rays coming from slightly different directions across the angular extent of the Sun.

For a convex lens of focal length \(f\), these rays form a small real image of the Sun near the focal plane. If \(d\) is the diameter of this image, then from the geometry of the focal plane:

\[ \tan\theta_\odot = \frac{d}{f} \]

For this small angle,

\[ \tan\theta_\odot \approx \theta_\odot \]

so

\[ \boxed{d \approx f \theta_\odot} \]

For example, a convex lens of focal length \(10\text{ cm}\) forms an image of the Sun with diameter:

\[ d \approx (10\text{ cm})(9.3 \times 10^{-3}) \approx 0.093\text{ cm} \] \[ \boxed{d \approx 0.93\text{ mm}} \]
Thus, even a single lens can convert the small angular size of a distant object into a finite image at its focal plane: \[ \boxed{\text{distant object} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{approximately parallel rays} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{real image near focal plane}} \]

This single lens, however, is not yet a telescope. The objective of a telescope performs essentially this first step: it gathers light from the distant object and forms an intermediate image. A second lens, the eyepiece, is then used to view this intermediate image at a much larger angular size.

Thus, a single convex lens performs essentially the objective's half of the telescope's job: it gathers light from the distant object and forms its intermediate image. The eyepiece performs the second half by angularly magnifying that image for the observer.

Types of Telescopes

Telescopes are broadly classified into two categories based on their objective element:

  • Refracting Telescopes: Use a combination of lenses (refraction) to form the image. The most common type is the Astronomical Telescope.
  • Reflecting Telescopes: Use a large curved mirror (reflection) as the primary objective to gather light and form the image. Examples include Newtonian and Cassegrain telescopes.
