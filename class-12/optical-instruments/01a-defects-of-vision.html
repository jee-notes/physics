Defects of Vision

Understanding and correcting the optical flaws of the human eye.

Myopia and Hypermetropia

Myopia (Near-sightedness)

A person with myopia can see nearby objects clearly but cannot see distant objects distinctly. The far point of the eye shifts closer than infinity. This defect occurs because the converging power of the eye lens is too high, or the eyeball is too long. As a result, the image of a distant object is formed in front of the retina.

Correction for Myopia: A concave (diverging) lens is used. It diverges the incoming parallel rays from infinity so they appear to come from the person's defective far point.

Hypermetropia (Far-sightedness)

A person with hypermetropia can see distant objects clearly but cannot see nearby objects distinctly. The near point of the eye recedes further than the normal \( 25 \text{ cm} \). This occurs because the converging power of the lens is too low, or the eyeball is too short. The image of a nearby object is formed behind the retina.

Correction for Hypermetropia: A convex (converging) lens is used. It provides the extra converging power needed to focus rays from the normal near point (\( 25 \text{ cm} \)) onto the retina.

Presbyopia and Astigmatism

Presbyopia

With age, the ciliary muscles weaken and the eye lens loses flexibility, resulting in a receding near point. This age-related hypermetropia is called presbyopia. It is corrected using a convex lens. In some cases, a person may suffer from both myopia and hypermetropia, requiring bifocal lenses (upper half concave for distant vision, lower half convex for reading).

Astigmatism

Astigmatism occurs when the cornea is not perfectly spherical, having different radii of curvature in different planes. A person with astigmatism cannot simultaneously focus on horizontal and vertical lines.

Astigmatism is corrected using a cylindrical lens, which provides different refractive power in different meridians to compensate for the asymmetrical cornea.

Calculations for Corrective Lenses

We use the lens formula to find the focal length \( f \) of the corrective lens: \[ \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} \] The power of the lens is given by \( P = \frac{1}{f (\text{in meters})} \) and is measured in Diopters (D).

Example: Myopia Correction

If a myopic person has a far point of \( 80 \text{ cm} \), what is the power of the lens required?

The lens must form an image at \( v = -80 \text{ cm} \) for an object at \( u = -\infty \). \[ \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{-80} - \frac{1}{-\infty} = -\frac{1}{80} \] \[ f = -80 \text{ cm} = -0.8 \text{ m} \] \[ P = \frac{1}{-0.8} = -1.25 \text{ D} \] A concave lens of power \( -1.25 \text{ D} \) is required.

Example: Hypermetropia Correction

If a hypermetropic person has a near point of \( 100 \text{ cm} \), what lens is required to read a book at \( 25 \text{ cm} \)?

The lens must form an image at \( v = -100 \text{ cm} \) for an object at \( u = -25 \text{ cm} \). \[ \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{-100} - \frac{1}{-25} = \frac{-1 + 4}{100} = \frac{3}{100} \] \[ f = \frac{100}{3} \text{ cm} = \frac{1}{3} \text{ m} \] \[ P = \frac{1}{1/3} = +3.0 \text{ D} \] A convex lens of power \( +3.0 \text{ D} \) is required.
