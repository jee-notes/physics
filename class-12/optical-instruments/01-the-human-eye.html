The Human Eye

The biological optical instrument.

Mechanism of the Eye

Optical instruments are ultimately used by the unaided eye. Therefore, before studying how optical instruments work, we first need to understand how the eye itself forms an image and enables us to see.

The unaided human eye works on a principle similar to that of a photographic camera. Its optical system converges incoming light rays and forms a real, inverted image on a light-sensitive screen called the retina.

The retina acts as the image screen of the eye because it contains light-sensitive cells.
Therefore, the optical system of the eye must form a real image on the retina.
This brings us to an important general property of image formation: \[ \boxed{\text{Real object} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{Real image} \; = \; \text{Inverted}} \] For a single optical element, whenever a real object forms a real image, the image is inverted.
The important condition is that both the object and the image are real.
This is the same principle seen in real-image formation by lenses, mirrors and refracting surfaces.
The eye therefore necessarily forms an inverted real image of the real objects around us.
The fact that we experience the world as upright is a consequence of the subsequent processing of the retinal signals by the brain.
The brain also processes the signals received from the two eyes to produce a single visual perception.
This is why cartoons often show a person suffering a head injury and then seeing two images of the same object, even though the eyes themselves are not damaged.
The cartoon is illustrating, in a simplified way, that normal vision depends not only on the optical images formed by the eyes, but also on how the brain processes those images.
Thus, the optical image formed on the retina and the visual perception experienced by us are not the same thing.

Light first enters the eye through the transparent, curved cornea. Behind it is the iris, a muscular diaphragm containing the pupil at its centre. By changing the size of the pupil, the iris regulates the amount of light entering the eye.

The eye contains a transparent, flexible crystalline lens. Unlike an ordinary glass lens, its shape and therefore its focal length can be changed. This change is produced by the ciliary muscles.

The crystalline lens is a transparent, flexible structure whose material is harder at the centre and progressively softer towards its outer regions. This deformability allows the ciliary muscles to alter its curvature and hence its focal length.

This leads to the key structural feature of the eye's optical mechanism.
The retina is fixed in position inside the eyeball, so the distance between the eye lens and the retina is essentially fixed.
Therefore, the image distance \(v\) is fixed.
To form a sharp image on this fixed retinal screen for objects at different distances, the eye changes the focal length \(f\) of its lens by changing the curvature of the crystalline lens.

Fixed Image Distance and Variable Focus

For the human eye, the position of the retina is fixed inside the eyeball. Therefore, the image distance \(v\), measured from the eye's effective optical system to the retina, is essentially fixed.

The 10th NCERT states that the diameter of the human eyeball is approximately \(2.3\,\text{cm}\) on average.
This gives an idea of the scale of the eye, although the eyeball diameter should not be directly identified with the optical image distance \(v\).

We can model the eye's optical system approximately as a converging lens system. For the corresponding thin-lens model, \(\frac{1}{f}=\frac{1}{v}-\frac{1}{u}\) where:

  • \(f\) is the focal length of the effective optical system of the eye, with sign (variable).
  • \(v\) is the image distance, with sign (essentially fixed).
  • \(u\) is the object distance, with sign (variable).

For real objects viewed by the eye, \(u \lt 0\), while the real image formed on the retina has \(v>0\). Since \(v\) remains essentially constant while \(u\) changes, the focal length \(f\) must change so that the image continues to fall on the retina.

When looking at distant objects (\(u\to-\infty\)), the ciliary muscles are relaxed, the crystalline lens is thin, and its focal length is maximum (\(f_{\max}\approx v\)). This is the state of least curvature of the lens and represents the relaxed state of the eye. Everything involved in accommodation is at its most relaxed state.

When looking at nearby objects, the ciliary muscles contract, the crystalline lens becomes thicker and more convex, and its focal length decreases. This is the state of maximum curvature of the lens and represents the stressed state of the eye. The ciliary muscles remain contracted to maintain the required accommodation.

This is particularly relevant to prolonged screen viewing.
A screen is normally held at a relatively short distance, so the eye has to remain in its accommodated, stressed state for a prolonged period rather than repeatedly returning to its relaxed state.
This sustained stress can contribute to eye strain and visual fatigue.
Hence the familiar recommendation to take breaks during prolonged screen use and look at distant objects. Looking into the distance allows the eye to move back towards its relaxed state, reducing the accommodation required from the ciliary muscles.

Accommodation

The ability of the eye to adjust the focal length of its crystalline lens so that objects at different distances can be seen clearly is called accommodation.

This ability is limited. The crystalline lens can increase its curvature only up to a certain limit; it cannot keep becoming more curved indefinitely to produce arbitrarily large optical power or a focal length approaching zero.

Thus, accommodation has two limiting points that define the range of distances over which a normal eye can obtain a distinct image:

  • Near Point (Least Distance of Distinct Vision, \(D\)): The minimum distance from the eye at which an object can be seen distinctly. For a normal adult eye, \(D=25\,\text{cm}\) . At the near point, the crystalline lens has its maximum curvature and minimum focal length, corresponding to the maximum accommodation and maximumstressed state of the eye.
  • Far Point: The maximum distance at which an object can be seen distinctly. For a normal eye, the far point is at \(\infty\) . When viewing an object at the far point, the crystalline lens is at its minimum curvature and maximum focal length, corresponding to the relaxed state of the eye.
The near point and far point therefore represent the two limits of the eye's variable-focus capability: \[ \boxed{\text{far point}\;\longleftrightarrow\;\text{relaxed state}} \] \[ \boxed{\text{near point}\;\longleftrightarrow\;\text{maximum stressed state}} \]

The power of accommodation is the change in the optical power of the eye between these two limiting states. Since optical power is \(P=1/f\), \[ \boxed{P_{\text{accommodation}}=\frac{1}{f_{\min}}-\frac{1}{f_{\max}}} \] where the focal lengths are measured in metres and the power is expressed in dioptres.

With increasing age, the crystalline lens gradually loses flexibility and the ability of the ciliary muscles to change its curvature also decreases. As a result, the range of accommodation becomes smaller and the near point moves farther away.

Apparent Largeness

The eye does not judge the apparent size of an object simply from its actual physical size.

What matters optically is the angle subtended by the object at the eye, called its angular size.

If an object of height \(h\) is at a distance \(u\) from the eye, then for a small angular size,

\[ \tan \theta \approx \theta = \frac{h}{u} \]

Thus, for the same object (same height \(h\)), increasing its distance \(u\) decreases its angular size and makes it appear smaller, while bringing it closer increases its angular size and makes it appear larger.

The Sun provides a good example.
The Sun is enormously larger than many objects around us, yet it appears quite small in the sky because it is also enormously farther away.
Its large physical size is offset by its great distance, so the angle it subtends at the eye is small.

The angular size determines the size of the image formed on the retina. For a given eye, a larger angular size produces a larger retinal image and therefore the object appears larger to the observer.

\[ \boxed{\text{larger angular size}\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{larger retinal image}\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{appears larger}} \]

This is the basic principle behind angular magnification in optical instruments.

For the unaided eye, there is a practical limit to increasing angular size simply by bringing the object closer. The object cannot be brought closer than the near point while still being seen distinctly. Therefore, once the object is already at the near point, the unaided eye cannot make it appear larger simply by reducing its distance further.

This is precisely where optical instruments become useful.
Instead of physically bringing the object closer than the near point, an optical instrument can make the object subtend a larger angle at the eye while still allowing the eye to focus it comfortably.
This is because, the image is at near point and the observer looks at that image.

Thus, instruments such as magnifying glasses and telescopes make an object appear larger primarily by increasing the angular size seen by the eye, rather than by physically enlarging the object.

What Does the Observer See?

In an optical diagram of refraction, we often draw both the object and the image. However, this does not mean that the observer sees both of them.

The object is the physical source of light, while the image represents the position from which the light rays reaching the observer appear to originate.

The observer sees the object at the position indicated by the image formed by the optical system.

Consider the familiar bird–fish problem.
Refraction at the water surface changes the direction of the rays coming from the fish.
When these refracted rays enter the bird's eye, they appear to come from a point different from the fish's actual position.
Therefore, even though the ray diagram shows both the actual fish (the physical object) and the apparent image of the fish (the position at which the fish is seen), the bird does not see two fish.
The actual fish and its apparent image are both drawn in the diagram only to represent the optical situation. The bird perceives the fish at the position of the apparent image.

Thus, whenever an optical diagram contains both an object and an image, it is important to distinguish between what physically exists and what the observer perceives.

This distinction is critical for understanding how an optical instrument improves upon unaided human vision.

In optical diagrams, human vision is generally represented by an eye. Whenever an eye is shown as the observer, we must therefore identify which image is actually being viewed by the eye.

Optical Instruments

Understanding how the eye works is crucial for understanding the working of optical instruments.

The first optical instrument we study is the spectacle lens, which is used to correct defects of vision. When the eye cannot form a sharp image on the retina because of a limitation in its accommodation or because of a change in the shape of the eyeball, an external lens is used to modify the incoming rays so that a sharp image can again be formed on the retina.

We then move beyond simply correcting the eye to using optical instruments to extend its visual capability. The two major instruments studied are the microscope, which allows very small objects to be viewed with greater angular size, and the telescope, which allows distant objects to be viewed with greater angular size.
