Electromagnetic Waves

Oscillating electric and magnetic fields travelling through space — light and radiation.

Conceptual Overview

For a long time, electricity and magnetism were studied as two apparently different physical phenomena. Electricity was associated with electric charges, sparks and currents, while magnetism was associated with magnets and their ability to attract or repel magnetic materials.

The connection between these two phenomena began to emerge in 1820, when Hans Christian Oersted discovered that an electric current flowing through a wire could deflect a nearby magnetic compass needle. This demonstrated that an electric current produces a magnetic effect.

A few years later, in 1831, Michael Faraday discovered electromagnetic induction. He demonstrated that a changing magnetic field could produce an electric current in a conductor. In other words, the relationship worked in the reverse direction as well: electric currents could produce magnetic effects, and changing magnetic fields could produce electric effects.

These discoveries suggested that electricity and magnetism were not independent phenomena but were somehow interconnected. Faraday's experimental investigations established this connection, while his field-based way of thinking provided a new perspective for understanding electromagnetic interactions.

James Clerk Maxwell took these experimental discoveries and the mathematical laws describing them and developed a unified mathematical theory of electromagnetism. While examining the consistency of the existing laws, he noticed that Ampere's law, in its original form, was incomplete. It accounted for the magnetic effect of electric current due to moving charges, but did not account for the magnetic effect associated with a changing electric field, such as in the gap between the plates of a charging capacitor.

To resolve this inconsistency, Maxwell introduced the concept of displacement current. He proposed that a changing electric field also contributes to producing a magnetic field, even in a region where no actual charge flows through the space.

With this addition, Maxwell established a more complete and consistent mathematical description of electricity and magnetism. In 1864, he presented his theory in a paper titled A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field, which was published in 1865.

Maxwell's work brought electricity and magnetism under a single coherent theoretical framework. This achievement is often regarded as the second great unification in physics, following Newton's unification of terrestrial and celestial mechanics through his laws of motion and universal gravitation.

But Maxwell's theory had a consequence far more remarkable than simply unifying two known phenomena. His equations predicted the existence of a completely new phenomenon: electromagnetic waves.

The key to this prediction was the mutual coupling between changing electric and magnetic fields. According to Faraday's law, a changing magnetic field is associated with an induced electric field. According to the modified Ampere's law, a changing electric field contributes to producing a magnetic field.

This meant that a disturbance in the electromagnetic field could propagate through space as a coupled variation of electric and magnetic fields. A changing electric field is associated with a changing magnetic field, which is in turn associated with a changing electric field. The two fields can sustain a propagating disturbance without requiring electric charges or a material medium to travel along with the wave.

An oscillating electric charge can create such a disturbance. As the charge oscillates, its electric field changes with time, and the resulting electromagnetic disturbance propagates outward through space as an electromagnetic wave. Thus, Maxwell's theory showed that electromagnetic waves could travel even through empty space.

Maxwell also calculated the speed at which these waves should propagate in free space. The electromagnetic constants of free space had already been determined through independent electrical and magnetic experiments. Using these values, he found that the predicted wave speed was approximately \(3.1 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}\).

This result was extraordinary because the experimentally measured speed of light was also approximately \(3 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}\). These two independently obtained values agreed remarkably well, even though the electromagnetic measurements used to calculate the wave speed did not depend on optical measurements of light.

Maxwell therefore concluded that light itself must be an electromagnetic disturbance propagating through space. In other words, light was not a separate physical phenomenon; it was an electromagnetic wave.

This was one of the greatest intellectual achievements in the history of physics. Maxwell had not only unified electricity and magnetism but had also revealed that light belonged to the same unified framework. Phenomena that appeared entirely different at first — electric currents, magnetic fields and light — were now understood as different manifestations of electromagnetism.

However, Maxwell's prediction was still a theoretical result. Its experimental confirmation came later, when Heinrich Hertz generated and detected electromagnetic waves in the laboratory during 1887–1888. Hertz demonstrated that these waves could be reflected and exhibited other wave properties, providing strong experimental confirmation of Maxwell's theory.

Today, we know that visible light is only a small part of the much broader electromagnetic spectrum, which also includes radio waves, microwaves, infrared radiation, ultraviolet radiation, X-rays and gamma rays. All of these are electromagnetic waves, differing primarily in their frequencies and wavelengths.

Displacement Current

Maxwell noticed an inconsistency in Ampere's Law: it only accounted for current due to moving charges, but not for the changing electric field in regions like the gap of a charging capacitor.

He added a term — the displacement current — to fix this:

\[ I_d = \varepsilon_0 \frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} \]

The modified Ampere's Law, with displacement current included, now reads:

\[ \oint \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0 \left(I + I_d\right) = \mu_0 \left(I +\varepsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt}\right) \]

This addition made the equations symmetric: a changing \( \vec{B} \) produces \( \vec{E} \) (Faraday), and a changing \( \vec{E} \) produces \( \vec{B} \) (modified Ampere).

Together, they predict self-propagating EM waves.

Speed of Electromagnetic Waves

From Maxwell's equations, the speed of electromagnetic waves in free space is:

\[ c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0 \varepsilon_0}} = 3 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s} \]

This is one of the most important constants in physics. In a medium of permittivity \( \varepsilon = K\varepsilon_0 \) and permeability \( \mu = \mu_r\mu_0 \):

\[ v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{K\mu_r}} = \frac{c}{n} \]

where \( n = \sqrt{K\mu_r} \) is the refractive index of the medium.
