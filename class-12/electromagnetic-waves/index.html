Electromagnetic Waves

Oscillating electric and magnetic fields travelling through space — light, radio, X-rays, and everything in between.

Conceptual Overview

In 1865, James Clerk Maxwell unified electricity and magnetism into a single coherent theory. His equations predicted something remarkable: a changing electric field produces a changing magnetic field, which in turn produces a changing electric field — a self-sustaining oscillation that propagates through empty space as a wave. He calculated the speed of this wave and found it exactly matched the measured speed of light. Light, he concluded, is an electromagnetic wave.

This was one of the greatest intellectual achievements in the history of physics. Maxwell's prediction was confirmed experimentally by Heinrich Hertz in 1888, who generated and detected radio waves in the laboratory.

The Missing Link — Displacement Current

Maxwell noticed an inconsistency in Ampere's Law: it only accounted for current due to moving charges, but not for the changing electric field in regions like the gap of a charging capacitor. He added a term — the displacement current — to fix this:

\[ I_d = \varepsilon_0 \frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} \]

The modified Ampere's Law, with displacement current included, now reads:

\[ \oint \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0 \left(I + I_d\right) = \mu_0 \left(I + \varepsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt}\right) \]

This addition made the equations symmetric: a changing \( \vec{B} \) produces \( \vec{E} \) (Faraday), and a changing \( \vec{E} \) produces \( \vec{B} \) (modified Ampere). Together, they predict self-propagating EM waves.

Speed of Electromagnetic Waves

From Maxwell's equations, the speed of electromagnetic waves in free space is:

\[ c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0 \varepsilon_0}} = 3 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s} \]

This is one of the most important constants in physics. In a medium of permittivity \( \varepsilon = K\varepsilon_0 \) and permeability \( \mu = \mu_r\mu_0 \):

\[ v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{K\mu_r}} = \frac{c}{n} \]

where \( n = \sqrt{K\mu_r} \) is the refractive index of the medium.
