Displacement Current in Detail

Maxwell's correction to Ampere's Law — and how it resolves the charging capacitor paradox.

The Problem with Ampere's Law

The original Ampere's Law relates the magnetic field around a closed loop to the net current threading through any surface bounded by that loop:

\[ \oint \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}} \]

The surface chosen to apply Ampere's Law is arbitrary — any surface bounded by the loop should give the same answer. However, consider a parallel plate capacitor being charged by a current \( I \):

  • For a flat surface cutting through the wire: \( I_{\text{enc}} = I \). ✓
  • For a bulging surface passing through the gap between the plates: no conduction current passes through it. \( I_{\text{enc}} = 0 \). ✗

Two surfaces bounded by the same loop give different answers — a fundamental inconsistency. Ampere's Law was incomplete.

Maxwell recognized this problem in 1861. His resolution was both elegant and profound: the changing electric field between the capacitor plates acts as an effective "current" — the displacement current — and it is exactly equal to the conduction current \( I \) in the wire.

Maxwell's Resolution — Displacement Current

The electric field between the plates of a capacitor with charge \( Q \) and plate area \( A \) is:

\[ E = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0 A} \implies \frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0 A}\frac{dQ}{dt} = \frac{I}{\varepsilon_0 A} \]

The electric flux through the gap is \( \Phi_E = EA \), so:

\[ \varepsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} = \varepsilon_0 A \cdot \frac{dE}{dt} = \varepsilon_0 A \cdot \frac{I}{\varepsilon_0 A} = I \]

Maxwell defined the displacement current as:

\[ \boxed{I_d = \varepsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt}} \]

In the capacitor gap, \( I_d = I \) — exactly equal to the conduction current in the wire. Adding \( I_d \) to the right side of Ampere's Law removes the inconsistency: the bulging surface now gives \( \mu_0(0 + I_d) = \mu_0 I \), the same as the flat surface.

The Modified Ampere's Law

\[ \oint \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0(I_c + I_d) = \mu_0 I_c + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} \]

where \( I_c \) is the conduction current (due to moving charges) and \( I_d \) is the displacement current (due to changing electric flux). In free space with no charges, \( I_c = 0 \), and only the displacement current term survives.

Key Points

  • Displacement current is not a physical current of moving charges — it is a mathematical term that represents the effect of a changing electric field.
  • Despite being a fictitious current in that sense, it produces a real magnetic field — just like conduction current.
  • At all times, in all regions of space: \( I_c + I_d = \) constant through any cross-section of a circuit. This restores the consistency of Ampere's Law.
  • The displacement current density is: \( \vec{J}_d = \varepsilon_0 \dfrac{\partial \vec{E}}{\partial t} \).
  • Between capacitor plates: \( I_c = 0 \) and \( I_d = \varepsilon_0 A\, dE/dt \). In the wire: \( I_c = I \) and \( I_d = 0 \).

EM Waves from Displacement Current

The displacement current term is what makes EM waves possible. Consider a region of space with no charges or conduction currents. Maxwell's equations give:

\[ \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \quad \text{(Faraday)} \] \[ \oint \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} \quad \text{(Ampere–Maxwell, no charges)} \]

These two equations together form a coupled system: changing \( \vec{B} \) drives changing \( \vec{E} \), and changing \( \vec{E} \) drives changing \( \vec{B} \). Together they produce a wave that propagates at speed:

\[ c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}} = 3 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s} \]
