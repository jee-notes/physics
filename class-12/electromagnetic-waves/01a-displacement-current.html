Displacement Current

Maxwell's correction to Ampere's Law.

Concept

Electricity and magnetism were originally studied as two separate physical phenomena: electric charges exerted forces on other charges, while magnets and electric currents produced magnetic effects.

Experiments by Oersted and Faraday had already established connections between the two phenomena. An electric current could produce a magnetic effect, and a changing magnetic field could produce an electric effect. However, a unified understanding of these phenomena was still missing.

James Clerk Maxwell approached the problem using the concept of a field. Instead of describing electromagnetic interactions merely as forces between objects, he treated electric and magnetic fields as physical quantities distributed throughout space.

Since both electric and magnetic fields can be represented as vector fields distributed throughout three-dimensional space, they can be studied under a common mathematical framework.

Common mathematical tools used to study vector fields are line integrals and surface integrals, which allow us to examine how vector fields behave along paths and across surfaces.

Expressing the known laws of electricity and magnetism under this field-based mathematical framework revealed deeper relationships between the two fields. A changing magnetic field produces an electric field, while a changing electric field also contributes to producing a magnetic field.

Maxwell recognised that the existing mathematical description of electromagnetism was incomplete. By introducing the concept of displacement current, he completed the relationship and established a consistent theory in which electric and magnetic fields are dynamically coupled.

An extraordinary consequence of this theory was the prediction of electromagnetic waves. A changing electric field and a changing magnetic field are coupled in such a way that an electromagnetic disturbance can propagate through empty space without requiring a material medium.

Maxwell calculated the speed at which these waves should travel using the known electrical and magnetic constants of free space. The result closely matched the experimentally measured speed of light.

Maxwell therefore concluded that light itself is an electromagnetic wave. What appeared to be separate phenomena — electricity, magnetism and light — could now be understood within a single electromagnetic theory.

Thus, Maxwell's achievement was not merely bringing electricity and magnetism under the same set of equations. His approach revealed the relationships between electric and magnetic fields and ultimately connected electromagnetism with light.

The Problem with Ampere's Law

The original Ampere's Law relates the magnetic field around a closed loop to the net current threading through any surface bounded by that loop:

\[ \oint \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}} \]

The surface chosen to apply Ampere's Law is arbitrary — any surface bounded by the loop should give the same answer.

However, consider a parallel plate capacitor being charged by a current \( I \):

  • For a flat surface cutting through the wire: \( I_{\text{enc}} = I \). ✓
  • For a bulging surface passing through the gap between the plates: no conduction current passes through it. \( I_{\text{enc}} = 0 \). ✗

Two surfaces bounded by the same loop give different answers — a fundamental inconsistency. Ampere's Law was incomplete.

Maxwell recognized this problem in 1861.
His resolution was both elegant and profound: the changing electric field between the capacitor plates acts as an effective "current" — the displacement current — and it is exactly equal to the conduction current \( I \) in the wire.

Maxwell's Resolution — Displacement Current

The electric field between the plates of a capacitor with charge \( Q \) and plate area \( A \) is:

\[ E = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0 A} \implies \frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0 A}\frac{dQ}{dt} = \frac{I}{\varepsilon_0 A} \]

The electric flux through the gap is \( \Phi_E = EA \), so:

\[ \varepsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} = \varepsilon_0 A \cdot \frac{dE}{dt} = \varepsilon_0 A \cdot\frac{I}{\varepsilon_0 A} = I \]

Maxwell defined the displacement current as:

\[ \boxed{I_d = \varepsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt}} \]

In the capacitor gap, \( I_d = I \) — exactly equal to the conduction current in the wire.

Adding \( I_d \) to the right side of Ampere's Law removes the inconsistency: the bulging surface now gives \( \mu_0(0 + I_d) = \mu_0 I \), the same as the flat surface.

The Modified Ampere's Law

\[ \oint \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0(I_c + I_d) = \mu_0 I_c + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} \]

where \( I_c \) is the conduction current (due to moving charges) and \( I_d \) is the displacement current (due to changing electric flux).

In free space with no charges, \( I_c = 0 \), and only the displacement current term survives.

Key Points

  • Displacement current is not a physical current of moving charges — it is a mathematical term that represents the effect of a changing electric field.
  • Despite being a fictitious current in that sense, it produces a real magnetic field — just like conduction current.
  • At all times, in all regions of space: \( I_c + I_d = \) constant through any cross-section of a circuit. This restores the consistency of Ampere's Law.
  • The displacement current density is: \( \vec{J}_d = \varepsilon_0 \dfrac{\partial \vec{E}}{\partial t} \).
  • Between capacitor plates: \( I_c = 0 \) and \( I_d = \varepsilon_0 A\, dE/dt \). In the wire: \( I_c= I \) and \( I_d = 0 \).

EM Waves from Displacement Current

The displacement current term is what makes EM waves possible.

Consider a region of space with no charges or conduction currents. Maxwell's equations give:

\[ \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \quad \text{(Faraday)} \] \[ \oint \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} \quad \text{(Ampere–Maxwell, no charges)} \]

These two equations together form a coupled system: changing \( \vec{B} \) drives changing \( \vec{E} \), and changing \( \vec{E} \) drives changing \( \vec{B} \).

Together they produce a wave that propagates at speed:

\[ c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}} = 3 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s} \]
