Magnetic Effects of Current

How moving charges reshape the fabric of space around them.

Conceptual Overview

Magnetism arises from motion. A stationary charge produces only an electric field. But when that charge moves — as current through a wire — it creates a new entity in space: the magnetic field. This is the central insight of this chapter.

The magnetic field \( \vec{B} \) is a vector field, assigning a magnitude and direction to every point in space around a current-carrying conductor. Like the electric field, it is a real physical entity — it stores energy, exerts force, and mediates interactions between currents.

The Magnetic Field as a Field Concept

Just as Coulomb's law describes the electric field of a point charge, Biot-Savart Law describes the magnetic field of a current element. Both follow an inverse-square dependence on distance. Both obey the principle of superposition.

However, unlike the electric field — which points radially away from or toward the source charge — the magnetic field curls around the current. Its direction is always perpendicular to both the current element and the displacement vector to the field point.

This perpendicularity is the defining geometric signature of the magnetic field, and it arises directly from the cross product in the Biot-Savart Law:

\[ d\vec{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{I\, d\vec{l} \times \hat{r}}{r^2} \]

Biot-Savart Law vs Ampere's Circuital Law

This chapter presents two distinct but equivalent approaches to computing magnetic fields — directly analogous to Coulomb's Law and Gauss' Law in electrostatics.

Biot-Savart Law: Field from Current Elements

The Biot-Savart Law builds the magnetic field by integrating contributions from each infinitesimal current element \( I\,d\vec{l} \). It is the most general method and works for any geometry. It is essential when the current distribution lacks symmetry.

Ampere's Circuital Law: Field from Enclosed Current

Ampere's Law relates the line integral of the magnetic field around a closed loop to the current passing through it:

\[ \oint \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}} \]

When strong symmetry exists (infinite straight wire, solenoid, toroid), Ampere's Law allows direct computation of \( \vec{B} \) without integration over current elements. It is the magnetic analogue of Gauss' Law.

Key Relationship

  • Biot-Savart Law builds the field from pieces — it is more general.
  • Ampere's Law uses global symmetry to bypass integration — it is more powerful in symmetric cases.
  • Both describe the same physical field.

The Lorentz Force

A magnetic field acts on matter by exerting force on moving charges. The force on a charge \( q \) moving with velocity \( \vec{v} \) in a field \( \vec{B} \) is given by the Lorentz force:

\[ \vec{F} = q(\vec{v} \times \vec{B}) \]

The magnetic force is always perpendicular to the velocity. It therefore does no work and cannot change the speed of a particle — only its direction. This leads to circular and helical trajectories, the cyclotron, and the velocity selector.
