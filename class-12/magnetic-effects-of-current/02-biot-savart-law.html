Biot-Savart Law

The fundamental law for computing magnetic fields from current distributions.

What is the Biot-Savart Law?

The Biot-Savart Law is to magnetostatics what Coulomb's Law is to electrostatics. It gives the magnetic field contributed by a single infinitesimal current element, and from it — through integration — we can compute the field due to any current distribution.

It was formulated experimentally, shortly after Ørsted's discovery that currents affect compass needles.

The Biot–Savart Formula

Consider a thin wire carrying steady current \( I \). Let \( d\vec{l} \) be an infinitesimal element of the wire directed along the current. Let \( \vec{r} \) be the displacement vector from this current element to a field point \( P \), with magnitude \( r \) and unit vector \( \hat{r} \).

The infinitesimal magnetic field contribution at point \( P \) due to this current element is given by the Biot–Savart law:

\[ d\vec{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{I\, d\vec{l} \times \hat{r}}{r^2} \]

In magnitude form:

\[ dB = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{I\, dl\, \sin\theta}{r^2} \]

where \( \theta \) is the angle between \( d\vec{l} \) and \( \hat{r} \).

The factor \( 1/r^2 \) describes how the contribution of a small current element decreases with distance from that element. However, the total magnetic field due to an extended current distribution depends on geometry and symmetry, since the vector contributions must be integrated over the entire current path.

The \(1/r^2\) dependence applies to the contribution of a current element. The final magnetic field of a complete current configuration may vary as \(1/r\), \(1/r^3\), or even remain approximately constant depending on symmetry.

Unit and Dimensions of Magnetic Field

The SI unit of magnetic field \( \vec{B} \) is the Tesla (T).

From the Lorentz force law, \[ \vec{F} = q(\vec{v} \times \vec{B}), \] we obtain: \[ B = \frac{F}{qv}. \]

Therefore,

\[ 1 \text{ Tesla} = 1 \frac{\text{Newton}}{\text{Ampere} \cdot \text{meter}} \]

Dimensional formula of magnetic field:

\[ [B] = M^1 T^{-2} I^{-1} \]

This shows that magnetic field represents force per unit charge per unit velocity.

\( \mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7} \, \text{T·m/A} \) is the permeability of free space — the magnetic analogue of \( \varepsilon_0 \) in electrostatics.

Key Features of the Biot–Savart Law

  • The elemental contribution decreases as \( 1/r^2 \).
  • The cross product \( d\vec{l} \times \hat{r} \) ensures that the magnetic field is perpendicular to both the current element and the line joining the element to the field point.
  • If \( d\vec{l} \parallel \hat{r} \) (\( \theta = 0^\circ \) or \( 180^\circ \)), then \( d\vec{B} = 0 \).
  • If \( d\vec{l} \perp \hat{r} \) (\( \theta = 90^\circ \)), the contribution is maximum.
  • The direction of \( d\vec{B} \) follows the right-hand rule and forms closed loops around the current.

Direction of the Field — The Right-Hand Rule

The direction of \( d\vec{B} \) is given by the cross product \( d\vec{l} \times \hat{r} \). Physically, you can use the right-hand rule:

  • Point the thumb of your right hand in the direction of current flow.
  • Your curled fingers indicate the direction in which the magnetic field circles around the wire.

This curling nature of the magnetic field — circling around its source — is the geometric signature that distinguishes it from the radial electric field.

Applicability of Gauss Law

When we say Gauss law is applicable to a field, we mean the following:

  1. The field has a source called a pole of strength \( S \), and the field magnitude at distance \( r \) from the pole is of the form \[ |\vec{F}| = \frac{kS}{r^2} \] that is, it follows an inverse-square dependence.
  2. If we evaluate the flux through any closed surface enclosing the pole, \[ \oint \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{A}, \] the result is proportional only to the enclosed pole strength \( S \), and is independent of the size or shape of the surface.

The inverse-square dependence is essential. Only when the field varies as \(1/r^2\) does the surface area factor \(4\pi r^2\) cancel the radial fall-off, making the flux independent of the size of the enclosing surface. This guarantees that the enclosed source strength is well-defined and conserved.

A Gauss-type law is meaningful for a monopole field only when the field has an inverse-square radial dependence.

Application to Magnetism

We may apply the same Gauss-type reasoning to magnetic fields. If magnetic monopoles existed, the magnetic field due to an isolated monopole of strength \( g \) would have the form \[ |\vec{B}| = \frac{k g}{r^2}. \] In that case, the magnetic flux through any closed surface enclosing the monopole would be proportional to \( g \).

However, when we evaluate the magnetic flux experimentally, \[ \oint \vec{B}\cdot d\vec{A}, \] the result is always zero for every closed surface.

\[ \oint \vec{B}\cdot d\vec{A} = 0 \]

This means that the enclosed magnetic pole strength is zero. Hence, isolated magnetic monopoles do not exist in classical electromagnetism. Magnetic fields are therefore fundamentally dipolar in nature, and magnetic field lines always form closed loops.

Principle of Superposition

The Biot-Savart Law, combined with the principle of superposition, allows us to compute the field of any current distribution. The total field at any point is the vector sum of contributions from all current elements:

\[ \vec{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \int \frac{I\, d\vec{l} \times \hat{r}}{r^2} \]

This integral is taken over the entire length of the current path. The challenge in applying the Biot-Savart Law is almost always a geometric one: setting up the integral carefully, exploiting symmetry to simplify components, and evaluating the resulting expression.

Symmetry is your most powerful tool. Before integrating, always identify which components of \( d\vec{B} \) cancel by symmetry — this can reduce a three-dimensional integral to a scalar one.

Biot-Savart vs Ampere's Law

Biot-Savart Law is the more general tool — it works for any current geometry. Ampere's Circuital Law works only when the field distribution has a strong geometrical symmetry (e.g., infinite straight wire, solenoid, toroid). In those special cases, Ampere's Law is far more efficient.

Just as Gauss' Law can be derived from Coulomb's Law, Ampere's Law follows from the Biot-Savart Law through integration. Both describe the same physical field.
