Force on Current-Carrying Conductors

How currents interact with fields and with each other.

Force on a Current Element in a Field

A current-carrying conductor placed in an external magnetic field experiences a force. Microscopically, this is the Lorentz force accumulating over all the drifting charge carriers. Macroscopically, for an infinitesimal element \( Id\vec{l} \) of the conductor:

\[ d\vec{F} = I\, d\vec{l} \times \vec{B} \]

For a straight conductor of length \( L \) in a uniform field \( \vec{B} \):

\[ \vec{F} = I\vec{L} \times \vec{B}, \quad F = BIL\sin\theta \]

where \( \theta \) is the angle between the current direction and \( \vec{B} \). The direction of the force is given by the right-hand rule for the cross product \( \vec{L} \times \vec{B} \).

A conductor carrying current parallel to \( \vec{B} \) experiences no force. Maximum force occurs when the current is perpendicular to \( \vec{B} \). This is the principle behind the electric motor.

Force on a Curved Conductor

For a curved conductor in a uniform field, we integrate \( d\vec{F} = I\, d\vec{l} \times \vec{B} \) along the path. Since \( \vec{B} \) is uniform, it can be pulled out:

\[ \vec{F} = I \left(\int d\vec{l}\right) \times \vec{B} \]

The integral \( \int d\vec{l} \) is simply the net displacement vector from the start to the end of the conductor. Therefore, for any curved conductor in a uniform field, the force is the same as that on a straight conductor connecting the two endpoints.

A closed current loop in a uniform magnetic field experiences zero net force (since the net displacement vector is zero). It does, however, experience a net torque.

Force Between Two Parallel Current-Carrying Wires

Consider two long parallel wires separated by distance \( d \), carrying currents \( I_1 \) and \( I_2 \) respectively. Wire 1 produces a magnetic field at the location of Wire 2:

\[ B_1 = \frac{\mu_0 I_1}{2\pi d} \]

The force per unit length on Wire 2 in this field is:

\[ \frac{F}{L} = I_2 B_1 = \frac{\mu_0 I_1 I_2}{2\pi d} \]
Direction: When currents flow in the same direction, the wires attract each other. When currents flow in opposite directions, they repel. This can be verified using the right-hand rule on each wire's field.

Definition of the Ampere

The SI unit of current, the Ampere (A), was historically defined using the force between parallel wires. One Ampere is defined as that constant current which, when flowing through two straight, infinite parallel conductors of negligible circular cross-section, placed 1 metre apart in vacuum, produces a force per unit length of \( 2 \times 10^{-7} \) N/m between them.

From the formula \( F/L = \mu_0 I^2 / (2\pi d) \), with \( I_1 = I_2 = 1\text{ A} \) and \( d = 1\text{ m} \):

\[ \frac{F}{L} = \frac{4\pi \times 10^{-7} \times 1 \times 1}{2\pi \times 1} = 2 \times 10^{-7} \text{ N/m} \]

This confirms the consistency of the definition. (Note: Since 2019, the SI system redefines the Ampere more fundamentally via a fixed value of the elementary charge \( e \).)

Torque on a Current Loop in a Uniform Field

A rectangular current loop of sides \( a \) and \( b \) carrying current \( I \) is placed in a uniform field \( \vec{B} \). Let \( \theta \) be the angle between the plane of the loop and \( \vec{B} \) (equivalently, \( 90° - \theta \) is the angle between the normal to the loop and \( \vec{B} \)).

The two sides of length \( a \) (parallel to the axis of rotation) each experience forces \( F = BIb \) in opposite directions, forming a couple. The net torque about the central axis is:

\[ \tau = B I a b \cos\theta = B I A \cos\theta \]

where \( A = ab \) is the area of the loop. In vector form, defining the magnetic moment \( \vec{m} = I A\hat{n} \) where \( \hat{n} \) is the normal to the loop:

\[ \vec{\tau} = \vec{m} \times \vec{B} \]

The torque is maximum when the plane of the loop is parallel to \( \vec{B} \) (\( \theta = 0° \)), and zero when the normal to the loop is aligned with \( \vec{B} \) (equilibrium position).
