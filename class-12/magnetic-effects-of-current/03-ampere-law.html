Ampere's Circuital Law

The symmetry-based shortcut for magnetic field computation.

Statement of Ampere's Law

Ampere's Circuital Law states that the line integral of the magnetic field \( \vec{B} \) around any closed loop (called an Amperian loop) is equal to \( \mu_0 \) times the total current enclosed by the loop:

\[ \oint \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}} \]

Here \( I_{\text{enc}} \) is the net current threading through any surface bounded by the chosen loop. The sign of the current is determined by the right-hand rule: if the fingers of the right hand curl in the direction of the integration path, the thumb indicates the positive current direction.

Ampere's Law is to magnetostatics what Gauss' Law is to electrostatics. Both relate a field integral to the source inside — flux of \( \vec{E} \) through a closed surface, vs. circulation of \( \vec{B} \) around a closed loop.

When Can Ampere's Law be Used Directly?

While Ampere's Law is always true, it can be used to directly compute \( \vec{B} \) only when the field is uniform in magnitude along the Amperian loop and either parallel or perpendicular to the path everywhere. This requires strong geometric symmetry.

The three standard symmetries that permit direct application:

  • Cylindrical symmetry — infinite straight wire, coaxial cable, solid cylindrical conductor.
  • Planar symmetry — infinite flat current sheet.
  • Toroidal symmetry — toroid or solenoid (approximated as having cylindrical symmetry on each small cross-section).

Application 1: Infinite Straight Wire

By symmetry, the magnetic field at distance \( r \) from an infinite wire must be tangential (perpendicular to \( \hat{r} \)) and have constant magnitude on any circle of radius \( r \) centred on the wire. Choose a circular Amperian loop of radius \( r \):

\[ \oint \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = B \cdot 2\pi r = \mu_0 I \] \[ \boxed{B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}} \]

This confirms the result from Biot-Savart integration — but is obtained in two lines instead of a full integral.

Application 2: Solid Cylindrical Conductor

A solid cylinder of radius \( R \) carries uniform current \( I \). The current density is \( J = I/(\pi R^2) \).

Outside the conductor (\( r > R \))

\[ B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r} \]

Inside the conductor (\( r < R \))

The enclosed current is \( I_{\text{enc}} = J \cdot \pi r^2 = I \cdot r^2/R^2 \). Applying Ampere's Law:

\[ B \cdot 2\pi r = \mu_0 \cdot \frac{Ir^2}{R^2} \] \[ \boxed{B = \frac{\mu_0 I r}{2\pi R^2}} \quad (r \leq R) \]

The field increases linearly with \( r \) inside, peaks at the surface, then falls off as \( 1/r \) outside. This is directly analogous to the electric field of a uniformly charged solid sphere from Gauss' Law.

Application 3: Ideal Solenoid

Choose a rectangular Amperian loop: one long side of length \( l \) lies inside the solenoid parallel to the axis, and the opposite long side lies outside where \( B = 0 \). The two short sides are perpendicular to \( \vec{B} \) and contribute nothing.

\[ \oint \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = B \cdot l + 0 + 0 + 0 = \mu_0 (nl) I \] \[ \boxed{B = \mu_0 n I} \]

where \( n \) is turns per unit length. The elegance of Ampere's Law here is undeniable: what requires a complex integration via Biot-Savart reduces to one line of algebra.

Application 4: Toroid

For a toroid with \( N \) turns and mean radius \( r \), a circular Amperian loop of radius \( r \) inside the toroid encloses all \( N \) turns:

\[ B \cdot 2\pi r = \mu_0 N I \implies \boxed{B = \frac{\mu_0 N I}{2\pi r}} \]

For a circular loop in the central hole or outside the toroid, the net enclosed current is zero (equal numbers of turns carry current in and out), so \( B = 0 \) in those regions.
