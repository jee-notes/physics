Magnetic Force and the Lorentz Force Law

The force that curves trajectories but never changes speed.

What is Magnetic Force?

A magnetic field exerts a force on any charged particle that is in motion through it. Unlike the electric force — which acts along the field direction — the magnetic force acts perpendicular to both the particle's velocity and the magnetic field. This perpendicularity is the defining geometric character of the magnetic interaction.

A charge at rest in a magnetic field experiences no force. Only motion through a magnetic field generates a magnetic interaction. This immediately distinguishes static electricity from magnetic phenomena.

Magnetism is not an independent force — it is the relativistic consequence of charge in motion. A moving charge in one frame of reference appears as a current in another, generating what we observe as a magnetic field.

The Lorentz Force Law

The complete electromagnetic force on a charge \( q \) moving with velocity \( \vec{v} \) in the presence of both an electric field \( \vec{E} \) and a magnetic field \( \vec{B} \) is given by the Lorentz Force Law:

\[ \vec{F} = q(\vec{E} + \vec{v} \times \vec{B}) \]

The magnetic component of this force is:

\[ \vec{F}_{\text{mag}} = q(\vec{v} \times \vec{B}) \]

The magnitude of the magnetic force is:

\[ F = qvB\sin\theta \]

where \( \theta \) is the angle between the velocity vector \( \vec{v} \) and the magnetic field vector \( \vec{B} \).

When \( \vec{v} \parallel \vec{B} \) (i.e., \( \theta = 0° \) or \( 180° \)), the magnetic force is zero. Maximum force occurs when \( \vec{v} \perp \vec{B} \) (i.e., \( \theta = 90° \)), giving \( F = qvB \).

Direction of the Magnetic Force — The Right-Hand Rule

The direction of \( \vec{v} \times \vec{B} \) is determined by the right-hand rule for the cross product:

  • Point the fingers of your right hand in the direction of \( \vec{v} \).
  • Curl them toward \( \vec{B} \).
  • Your thumb points in the direction of \( \vec{v} \times \vec{B} \).

For a positive charge, the force \( \vec{F} = q(\vec{v} \times \vec{B}) \) is in the direction of \( \vec{v} \times \vec{B} \). For a negative charge, the force is in the opposite direction.

A common JEE approach: use the right-hand rule to find \( \vec{v} \times \vec{B} \), then flip the direction if the charge is negative.

Key Properties of the Magnetic Force

1. Always Perpendicular to Velocity

Since \( \vec{F}_{\text{mag}} = q(\vec{v} \times \vec{B}) \), and cross products are always perpendicular to both input vectors, the magnetic force is always perpendicular to \( \vec{v} \).

2. Does No Work

Work done by a force is \( dW = \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{s} = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{v}\,dt \). Since \( \vec{F}_{\text{mag}} \perp \vec{v} \), their dot product is zero. Therefore:

\[ W_{\text{mag}} = 0 \]

The magnetic force never changes the kinetic energy or speed of a particle. It only changes the direction of motion.

3. Produces Circular and Helical Motion

A charge entering a uniform magnetic field with velocity perpendicular to \( \vec{B} \) undergoes uniform circular motion. The magnetic force provides the centripetal force. If the velocity has a component along \( \vec{B} \), the motion is helical.

4. Depends on the Sign of the Charge

Positive and negative charges moving in the same direction deflect in opposite directions in the same magnetic field. This forms the basis of mass spectrometers and charge-to-mass ratio measurements.

Force on a Current-Carrying Element

A current-carrying conductor is a collection of moving charges. The force on an infinitesimal current element \( Id\vec{l} \) in a magnetic field \( \vec{B} \) is:

\[ d\vec{F} = I\,d\vec{l} \times \vec{B} \]

This is derived directly from the Lorentz force on charge carriers. If \( n \) is the carrier density, \( A \) the cross-sectional area, and \( v_d \) the drift velocity, then \( I = nqv_dA \), and summing over all charges in element \( d\vec{l} \) gives the above expression.

The total force on a straight conductor of length \( L \) in a uniform field is:

\[ \vec{F} = I\vec{L} \times \vec{B} \]
