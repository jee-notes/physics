Energy Bands in Solids

Quantum mechanical origin of electrical classification in condensed matter.

Classification of Solids: Conductors, Insulators, and Semiconductors

Solids can be broadly categorized according to their electrical conductivity (\( \sigma \)) and resistivity (\( \rho = 1/\sigma \)) at room temperature (\( T \approx 300 \text{ K} \)):

  • Metals (Conductors): Possess extremely low resistivity and very high conductivity. \[ \rho \sim 10^{-2} \text{ to } 10^{-8} \ \Omega\cdot\text{m}, \quad \sigma \sim 10^{2} \text{ to } 10^{8} \ \text{S}\cdot\text{m}^{-1} \]
  • Insulators: Possess extremely high resistivity and negligible conductivity. \[ \rho \sim 10^{11} \text{ to } 10^{19} \ \Omega\cdot\text{m}, \quad \sigma \sim 10^{-11} \text{ to } 10^{-19} \ \text{S}\cdot\text{m}^{-1} \]
  • Semiconductors: Possess intermediate resistivity and conductivity that can be finely tuned. \[ \rho \sim 10^{-5} \text{ to } 10^{6} \ \Omega\cdot\text{m}, \quad \sigma \sim 10^{5} \text{ to } 10^{-6} \ \text{S}\cdot\text{m}^{-1} \]
Elemental semiconductors include Silicon (\( \text{Si}, Z=14 \)) and Germanium (\( \text{Ge}, Z=32 \)). Compound semiconductors include inorganic compounds like \( \text{GaAs} \), \( \text{CdS} \), \( \text{InP} \), as well as organic semiconductors and polymers like polyaniline.

Energy Band Theory (Valence Band, Conduction Band, Forbidden Energy Gap)

In an isolated atom, electron energies occupy sharp, discrete quantum states according to the Bohr model. However, when \( N \) atoms come together to form a crystal lattice, the outer electron wavefunctions overlap. In accordance with the Pauli Exclusion Principle, each discrete atomic energy level splits into \( N \) closely spaced sub-levels, forming continuous energy bands.

Key Energy Bands

  • Valence Band (VB): The energy band formed by the outermost valence electrons. It is completely or partially filled at \( 0 \text{ K} \). Electrons in this band do not contribute directly to conduction unless excited.
  • Conduction Band (CB): The lowest unfilled or partially filled energy band above the valence band. Electrons excited into this band are free to drift under an external electric field.
  • Forbidden Energy Gap (\( E_g \)): The energy separation between the top of the valence band (\( E_v \)) and the bottom of the conduction band (\( E_c \)): \[ E_g = E_c - E_v \] No allowed quantum states exist within this gap.

The distinction between the three classes of solids lies directly in their bandgap magnitude:

  1. Conductors: Either the conduction band is partially filled with electrons, or the conduction band overlaps with the valence band (\( E_g \approx 0 \)). A large pool of free electrons is readily available for conduction without thermal activation.
  2. Insulators: A very large forbidden energy gap exists (\( E_g > 3 \text{ eV} \)). For instance, in Diamond, \( E_g \approx 5.4 \text{ eV} \). Thermal energy at room temperature (\( k_B T \approx 0.026 \text{ eV} \)) is insufficient to excite valence electrons across this gap, resulting in zero conductivity.
  3. Semiconductors: Possess a small, finite forbidden energy gap (\( E_g < 3 \text{ eV} \)). At room temperature (\( 300 \text{ K} \)): \[ E_g(\text{Si}) \approx 1.1 \text{ eV}, \quad E_g(\text{Ge}) \approx 0.7 \text{ eV}, \quad E_g(\text{GaAs}) \approx 1.42 \text{ eV} \] Thermal energy excites a noticeable fraction of electrons from VB to CB, creating electrical conductivity.

Temperature Dependence of Conductivity

The electrical resistivity of semiconductors behaves fundamentally differently from that of metals:

Semiconductors possess a Negative Temperature Coefficient of Resistance (\( \alpha < 0 \)):
As temperature \( T \) increases, thermal agitation breaks covalent bonds, exponentially increasing the number density of free carriers \( n(T) \propto e^{-E_g / (2 k_B T)} \). The massive increase in carrier concentration overwhelmingly dominates over increased lattice scattering, causing resistivity to drop and electrical conductivity to surge: \[ \sigma(T) = \sigma_0 e^{-E_g / (2 k_B T)} \]

In contrast, metals have a fixed carrier concentration; rising temperature only increases electron-phonon lattice collisions, causing resistance to increase linearly (\( \alpha > 0 \)).

At absolute zero (\( T = 0 \text{ K} \)), a pure semiconductor has all valence electrons locked in bonds and an empty conduction band. Thus, at \( 0 \text{ K} \), a semiconductor behaves as an ideal insulator.
