Digital Electronics and Logic Gates

Binary logic operations, Boolean algebra, universal gates, and circuit synthesis.

Analog vs Digital Signals and Binary Logic

In electronics, information is processed via electric signals:

  • Analog Signals: Continuous, time-varying voltage or current signals that can assume any value within a continuous range (e.g., audio signals, sine waves).
  • Digital Signals: Discrete signals that have only two allowed voltage levels:
    • Binary 0 (Low / OFF / False): Typically \( 0 \text{ V} \).
    • Binary 1 (High / ON / True): Typically \( +5 \text{ V} \).

A Logic Gate is a digital circuit with one or more binary inputs but only one binary output, operating in accordance with logical rules.

Fundamental Logic Gates: NOT, OR, and AND

1. NOT Gate (Inverter)

Has a single input and single output. It inverts the logic level: \[ Y = \overline{A} \] Truth Table:
Input \( A = 0 \implies Y = 1 \); Input \( A = 1 \implies Y = 0 \).

Universal Logic Gates: NAND and NOR

NAND and NOR gates are called Universal Gates because repeated combinations of either gate alone can synthesize all three fundamental logic operations (NOT, AND, OR), as well as any arbitrary complex digital circuit.

1. NAND Gate (NOT-AND)

\[ Y = \overline{A \cdot B} \] Output is 0 only when both inputs are 1; otherwise output is 1.

Synthesizing Gates using NAND:
  • NOT: Tie inputs together \( \implies Y = \overline{A \cdot A} = \overline{A} \).
  • AND: Follow a NAND with a NAND inverter \( \implies Y = \overline{\overline{A \cdot B}} = A \cdot B \).
  • OR: Invert inputs with NANDs before feeding to a NAND \( \implies Y = \overline{\overline{A} \cdot \overline{B}} = A + B \).

2. NOR Gate (NOT-OR)

\[ Y = \overline{A + B} \] Output is 1 only when both inputs are 0; otherwise output is 0.

Synthesizing Gates using NOR:
  • NOT: Tie inputs together \( \implies Y = \overline{A + A} = \overline{A} \).
  • OR: Follow a NOR with a NOR inverter \( \implies Y = \overline{\overline{A + B}} = A + B \).
  • AND: Invert inputs with NORs before feeding to a NOR \( \implies Y = \overline{\overline{A} + \overline{B}} = A \cdot B \).

De Morgan's Laws and Logic Circuit Simplification

De Morgan's Theorems

  1. First Theorem: The complement of a logical sum equals the product of the individual complements: \[ \overline{A + B} = \overline{A} \cdot \overline{B} \]
  2. Second Theorem: The complement of a logical product equals the sum of the individual complements: \[ \overline{A \cdot B} = \overline{A} + \overline{B} \]

Example: Boolean Simplification

Simplify the Boolean expression: \( Y = \overline{\overline{A} \cdot B + \overline{A \cdot B}} \).

Step 1: Apply De Morgan's First Theorem to the outer sum: \[ Y = \overline{(\overline{A} \cdot B)} \cdot \overline{(\overline{A \cdot B})} \] Step 2: Use double negation \( \overline{\overline{X}} = X \) on the second term: \[ \overline{(\overline{A \cdot B})} = A \cdot B \] Step 3: Apply De Morgan's Second Theorem to the first term: \[ \overline{(\overline{A} \cdot B)} = \overline{\overline{A}} + \overline{B} = A + \overline{B} \] Step 4: Multiply and simplify using Boolean identities: \[ Y = (A + \overline{B}) \cdot (A \cdot B) = (A \cdot A \cdot B) + (\overline{B} \cdot A \cdot B) \] Since \( A \cdot A = A \) and \( \overline{B} \cdot B = 0 \): \[ Y = (A \cdot B) + 0 = A \cdot B \] The entire complex logic network reduces to a simple AND gate.
