p-n Junction Diode

The foundational building block of non-linear semiconductor devices.

Formation of p-n Junction (Diffusion and Drift Current)

A p-n junction is formed when a single semiconductor crystal (like Si) is fabricated such that one side is doped with acceptor impurities (p-type) and the adjacent side is doped with donor impurities (n-type). It cannot be created by simply pressing two separate blocks together, because microscopic surface roughness prevents continuous atomic contact.

Upon junction formation, two opposing transport processes immediately occur:

  1. Diffusion Current (\( I_{\text{diff}} \)): Due to the steep concentration gradient, majority holes from the p-side diffuse across the junction into the n-side, and majority electrons from the n-side diffuse into the p-side. This flow of majority carriers constitutes the diffusion current directed from the p-side to the n-side.
  2. Uncovering of Space Charge: As electrons leave the n-side, they leave behind uncompensated, immobile positive donor ions (\( N_d^+ \)). As holes leave the p-side, they leave behind uncompensated, immobile negative acceptor ions (\( N_a^- \)).
  3. Drift Current (\( I_{\text{drift}} \)): The uncompensated ions create a strong internal electric field \( \vec{E}_{\text{in}} \) directed from the n-side to the p-side. This field sweeps minority carriers (electrons generated in the p-side to n-side, and holes generated in the n-side to p-side). This minority carrier flow is the drift current directed from the n-side to the p-side.

Equilibrium Condition

Diffusion current starts large and decreases as the internal field builds up. Drift current starts at zero and increases until: \[ I_{\text{diff}} = I_{\text{drift}} \] At this point, the net current across the unbiased junction is strictly zero.

Depletion Region and Barrier Potential

Depletion Layer

The region on either side of the junction which is completely depleted of mobile charge carriers (electrons and holes) and contains only immobile space-charge ions is called the depletion region (or space-charge region).

Key characteristics:

  • Depletion Width (\( w \)): Typically of the order of \( 10^{-6} \text{ m} \) (\( \sim 0.5 - 1 \ \mu\text{m} \)). Higher doping levels result in a narrower depletion layer.
  • Barrier Potential (\( V_0 \) or \( V_b \)): The potential difference developed across the depletion layer due to the immobile space-charge ions. It acts as a potential hill preventing further majority carrier diffusion. \[ V_0 \approx 0.7 \text{ V for Silicon}, \quad V_0 \approx 0.3 \text{ V for Germanium} \]
  • Internal Electric Field (\( E_{\text{in}} \)): \[ E_{\text{in}} = \frac{V_0}{w} \approx \frac{0.7 \text{ V}}{10^{-6} \text{ m}} = 7 \times 10^5 \text{ V/m} \sim 10^6 \text{ V/m} \] Even though the barrier voltage is under 1 volt, the microscopic thickness creates an intense electric field.

p-n Junction Under Forward and Reverse Bias

Forward Biasing

When the p-side of the diode is connected to the positive terminal of an external DC voltage source \( V \) and the n-side is connected to the negative terminal:

  • The applied electric field opposes the internal barrier field \( E_{\text{in}} \).
  • The effective barrier height is lowered to \( (V_0 - V) \).
  • The depletion layer width decreases.
  • Majority carriers gain sufficient energy to cross the junction, producing a large forward diffusion current (measured in milliamperes, \( \text{mA} \)).

Reverse Biasing

When the p-side is connected to the negative terminal and the n-side to the positive terminal of the voltage source \( V \):

  • The applied field aids the internal barrier field.
  • The effective barrier height is raised to \( (V_0 + V) \).
  • The depletion layer width increases.
  • Majority carrier diffusion is completely halted. Only thermally generated minority carriers are swept across, resulting in an extremely small, almost constant reverse saturation current \( I_s \) (microamperes \( \mu\text{A} \) for Ge, nanoamperes \( \text{nA} \) for Si).

V-I Characteristics of a p-n Junction Diode

The mathematical relationship describing the current through a p-n diode is given by the Shockley Diode Equation: \[ I = I_s \left( e^{\frac{e V}{\eta k_B T}} - 1 \right) \] where \( \eta \) is the ideality factor (\( \eta=1 \) for Ge, \( \eta \approx 1 \text{ to } 2 \) for Si).

Salient Features of the V-I Curve

  1. Threshold / Cut-in / Knee Voltage (\( V_k \)): In forward bias, current remains negligible until the applied voltage overcomes the barrier potential. Beyond \( V_k \) (0.7 V for Si, 0.3 V for Ge), current increases exponentially.
  2. Dynamic (AC) Resistance (\( r_d \)): Defined as the reciprocal of the slope of the V-I characteristic: \[ r_d = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta I} \] In forward bias, \( r_d \) is very small (\( \sim 1 - 25 \ \Omega \)). In reverse bias, \( r_d \) is extremely high (\( \sim \text{M}\Omega \)).
  3. Reverse Breakdown Voltage (\( V_{\text{BR}} \)): If the reverse voltage is increased to a critical threshold, reverse current suddenly rises sharply. Operating an ordinary diode in this region causes thermal destruction.
