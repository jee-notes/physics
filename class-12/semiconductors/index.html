Semiconductors

The foundation of modern solid-state electronics and quantum band theory.

Conceptual Overview

So far in the Current Electricity chapter, we studied conductors and how they obey Ohm's law, \( V = IR \).

We examined how electric current flows through conducting materials and studied electric circuits, which are just specific configurations of electrical components connected to transmit and control electrical energy.

An important aspect of an electric circuit is therefore not just the transmission of electrical energy, but also its control. We should be able to decide when and how much current flows, according to our requirements.

For this, we need materials and devices with contrasting electrical properties. We need conducting materials to provide paths for electric current and insulating materials to prevent current from flowing through unwanted paths. Switches allow us to connect or interrupt these conducting paths, turning current ON or OFF.

This is somewhat similar to mechanical machines, where materials with different mechanical properties are used for different purposes.
For example, rigid bodies are used to maintain shape and transmit forces, whereas elastic or plastic materials are used wherever deformation is required.

However, instead of relying only on physically opening and closing a switch, imagine being able to control the flow of current by changing the electrical conditions of a material or device.

This would allow us to build circuits that can automatically control electrical signals and perform much more complex operations. This is where semiconductors become important.

Semiconductors are materials whose electrical behaviour is quite different from that of ordinary conductors and insulators.

Their \( V\text{-}I \) characteristics exhibit distinctive behaviour, and their electrical behaviour can be controlled by changing external conditions such as temperature and applied voltage, or by introducing suitable impurities.

Consequently, semiconductor devices can be designed to control the flow of electric current.

Depending on their configuration and operating conditions, they can allow current to flow, restrict its flow, or even amplify electrical signals.

Thus, semiconductor devices provide the foundation for modern electronic circuits, where electrical signals can be controlled and processed.

They form the backbone of a wide range of applications, from simple switching and signal amplification to communication systems, computers and mobile phones.

Microscopic Origin

In the Current Electricity chapter, we studied the microscopic form of Ohm's law to understand why metals behave the way they do. We saw that electrical conductivity is given by \( \sigma = \frac{ne^2\tau}{m} \), where \( n \) is the number density of free electrons and \( \tau \) is the average relaxation time between collisions.

In metals, \( n \) is extraordinarily large and essentially fixed by the atomic structure. When temperature increases, \( n \) does not change noticeably; instead, increased lattice vibrations reduce \( \tau \), causing the conductivity of a metal to decrease.

To understand semiconductors, we must similarly look at their microscopic crystal structure and electronic configuration—specifically, how atoms share valence electrons in covalent bonds and how much energy is required to free those electrons for conduction.

Historically, before the discovery of semiconductor physics, controlling a flow of electrons meant literally boiling them off a heated metal filament into an evacuated glass tube. Electronic circuits relied heavily on such vacuum tubes (valves like diodes, triodes, and tetrodes).

While functional, vacuum tubes had severe practical limitations: they were bulky, fragile, required high operating voltages, wasted large amounts of power simply heating filaments, and suffered from short lifespans.

The breakthrough of solid-state physics was realizing that we do not need a vacuum to control charge carriers—we can control them directly inside a solid crystal lattice at room temperature, using negligible power and microscopic dimensions.

Semiconductors (such as Silicon and Germanium) are crystalline solids whose electrical conductivity lies between that of conductors and insulators. At absolute zero, their valence electrons are tightly locked in covalent bonds, making them behave like insulators.

However, unlike true insulators, the energy required to break these bonds is relatively small. As a result, semiconductors offer a unique microscopic advantage: the concentration of charge carriers is not fixed.

Instead, the density, polarity, and transport of charge carriers can be dynamically engineered through thermal excitation, exposure to light, applied electric fields, or the controlled addition of specific impurities (a process known as doping).

Even more remarkably, electrical conduction in semiconductors involves two distinct types of mobile charge carriers: negatively charged free electrons and positively charged vacancies called holes.

This ability to precisely tailor charge carriers at the atomic level revolutionized science and technology—paving the way for diodes, transistors, integrated circuits, microprocessors, and the entire modern digital era.

Microscopic Origin

In conductors, we study the microscopic form of Ohm's Law to understand why a metal behaves the way it does electrically. We try to connect the macroscopic electrical properties of a material with the microscopic behaviour of its charge carriers.

We take a similar approach with semiconductors. To understand their distinctive electrical behaviour, we need to examine their microscopic structure, particularly their crystalline arrangement and electronic configuration.

Historically, electronic circuits relied heavily on vacuum tubes (also called valves), such as diodes, triodes and tetrodes. These devices could control and amplify electrical signals and were widely used in radios, communication systems and early computers.

However, vacuum tubes had several limitations. They were bulky and fragile, required considerable electrical power for heating their filaments, generally operated at high voltages and had limited lifespans.

Scientists therefore explored ways to achieve similar electronic control using solid materials rather than vacuum tubes. The development of semiconductor physics and the invention of the transistor marked a major breakthrough in this direction. Transistors could perform many of the functions of vacuum tubes while being considerably smaller, more energy-efficient and more reliable.

The development of solid-state semiconductor devices revolutionized science and technology, paving the way for integrated circuits, microprocessors, optoelectronics and the modern digital era.

Semiconductors are materials whose electrical conductivity generally lies between that of good conductors and insulators. Unlike metals, where the concentration of free charge carriers remains approximately constant over ordinary temperature ranges, the concentration of mobile charge carriers in semiconductors can change significantly with external conditions.

For example, thermal energy can excite electrons and generate additional mobile charge carriers. We can also deliberately modify the concentration and type of charge carriers by introducing suitable impurities into the crystal. This process is called doping.

Furthermore, an applied electric field can drive the motion of these charge carriers, producing an electric current. Thus, by controlling the concentration, type and transport of charge carriers, we can control the electrical behaviour of semiconductor materials and devices.

Doping - The p-n Junction

At the heart of semiconductor physics is the concept of energy bandgaps and two independent mobile charge carriers: negatively charged conduction electrons and positively charged valence holes.

In a pure (intrinsic) semiconductor, electrons and holes exist in equal concentrations. By intentionally introducing trace impurities—a process called doping—we create extrinsic semiconductors: n-type (electron-rich) and p-type (hole-rich).

Joining p-type and n-type semiconductors forms a p-n junction—a non-linear device capable of directional conduction (rectification), light emission (LEDs), photovoltaic power generation (solar cells), and high-speed electronic switching.

Logic Gates

Beyond analog amplification and rectification, semiconductor switches operate between two discrete voltage states—0 (LOW) and 1 (HIGH)—forming the physical basis of the binary number system.

Combining diodes and transistors into fundamental building blocks called Logic Gates (NOT, OR, AND, NAND, NOR) enables digital circuits to execute Boolean algebra, make logical decisions, and power modern computing hardware.
