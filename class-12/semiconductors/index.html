Semiconductors

The foundation of modern solid-state electronics and quantum band theory.

Conceptual Overview

Before the discovery of semiconductors, electronic circuits relied heavily on vacuum tubes (valves like diodes, triodes, and tetrodes). While functional, vacuum tubes were bulky, fragile, consumed large amounts of power for filament heating, operated at high voltages, and suffered from limited lifespans. The birth of solid-state semiconductor physics revolutionized science and technology, paving the way for integrated circuits, microprocessors, optoelectronics, and the modern digital era.

Semiconductors are crystalline materials whose electrical conductivity lies between that of conductors and insulators. Unlike metals—where carrier concentration is fixed and conductivity decreases with temperature—semiconductors provide a unique physical platform where the density, polarity, and transport of charge carriers (electrons and holes) can be dynamically engineered through controlled impurity addition (doping), thermal excitation, and applied electromagnetic fields.

At the heart of semiconductor physics is the concept of energy bandgaps and two independent mobile charge carriers: negatively charged conduction electrons and positively charged valence holes.

By joining p-type and n-type semiconductors, one creates a p-n junction—a non-linear device that exhibits directional electrical conduction, light emission, photovoltaic generation, and high-speed switching capabilities.
