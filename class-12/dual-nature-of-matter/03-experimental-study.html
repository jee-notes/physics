Experimental Study of Photoelectric Effect

Investigating how light intensity, frequency, and electrical potential affect photoelectron emission.

Experimental Setup and Instrumentation

To systematically study the photoelectric effect, Physicists used a standardized experimental arrangement. It consists of an evacuated glass or quartz tube containing a photosensitive plate (C, the emitter cathode) and another metal plate (A, the collector anode).

A transparent window, usually made of quartz (to allow UV light passage unattenuated), lets monochromatic light fall onto plate C. The plates are connected to an external circuit containing a microammeter, a variable voltage source, and a commutator switch. The varying voltage source allows the experimenter to independently control the potential difference between the plates, while the commutator can reverse the polarity, making plate A either positive or negative with respect to plate C.

Summary of Photoelectric Observations

By manipulating the experimental variables—specifically the light's intensity, the light's frequency \( \nu \), and the potential difference \( V \) between the electrodes—researchers documented distinct laws governing photoelectric emission:

  1. For a given substance, there exists a minimum threshold frequency \( \nu_0 \) below which no emission occurs, regardless of light intensity.
  2. Above the threshold frequency, the number of photoelectrons emitted per second (photocurrent) is directly proportional to the intensity of the incident light.
  3. The maximum kinetic energy of the emitted photoelectrons depends linearly on the frequency of the incident light, but is entirely independent of its intensity.
  4. The photoelectric emission is an instantaneous process. The time lag between the incidence of light and electron emission is incredibly small, less than \( 10^{-9} \) seconds, even if the light intensity is extremely dim.

Failure of Wave Theory of Light

The classical electromagnetic wave theory of light completely failed to explain these experimental observations. According to wave theory:

Intensity Failure: A more intense light wave carries more energy. Hence, the kinetic energy of emitted electrons should increase with light intensity. But experiment showed kinetic energy is independent of intensity.

Frequency Failure: Wave theory says light of *any* frequency should eventually emit electrons if it is intense enough and shone for long enough. It cannot explain the existence of a sharp threshold frequency \( \nu_0 \) below which emission is strictly zero.

Instantaneous Effect Failure: According to wave theory, a wave front's energy is spread continuously over space. A single electron would require hours to accumulate enough energy from a dim light wave to escape the metal surface. Yet, emission is observed to be instantaneous.
