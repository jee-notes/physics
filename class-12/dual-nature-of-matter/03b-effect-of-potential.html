Effect of Potential

Observing the varying kinetic energy of emitted photoelectrons.

Retarding and Accelerating Potentials

Consider keeping the frequency and intensity of incident light constant while we vary the potential applied to the collector plate A relative to emitter plate C.

If we apply a positive (accelerating) potential to plate A, it attracts the emitted photoelectrons. As we increase the positive potential, the photocurrent gradually increases until it reaches a maximum saturation value. At this saturation current, all electrons emitted by the cathode are successfully reaching the anode.

Conversely, if we apply a negative (retarding) potential to plate A, it repels the electrons. Only those electrons with enough initial kinetic energy can traverse the opposing electric field to reach the anode. As the retarding potential is increased (made more negative), the photocurrent decreases rapidly because fewer and fewer electrons have sufficient kinetic energy to overcome the repulsion.

Stopping Potential (Cut-off Potential)

At a certain critical negative potential, even the most energetic photoelectron is stopped and turned back just before reaching the collector plate. The photocurrent becomes strictly zero.

Stopping Potential (\( V_0 \))

Definition: The minimum negative (retarding) potential applied to the anode at which the photoelectric current becomes totally zero is called the stopping potential or cut-off potential.

Significance: The work done by the retarding electric field precisely equals the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted photoelectrons.

\[ K_{\text{max}} = eV_0 \]

Where \( K_{\text{max}} \) is the maximum kinetic energy, \( e \) is the charge of an electron, and \( V_0 \) is the magnitude of the stopping potential.

Stopping Potential vs Intensity relationship

If we perform this experiment repeatedly, using light beams of the exact same frequency but different intensities, we find an important result:

The saturation photocurrent increases with higher intensity, but the stopping potential \( V_0 \) remains absolutely identical for all intensities.

This indicates that the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted photoelectrons depends *only* on the frequency of the light and the nature of the material, and is completely independent of the intensity of the incident light. Overcoming the severe failure of wave theory, this demonstrates that increasing light intensity does not give more energetic electrons, it merely produces *more* electrons.

Saturation current

Common doubt is that why saturation current is obtained because increasing voltage increases kinetic energy of emitted photo-electrons.

In conductor, we know that \( I = nAev_d \). So, higher speed indicates higher current. And so, as voltage increases, KE increases, speed increases - current should increase.

The answer lies in the fact that intensity controls the no. of electrons emitted / second. Lets say 100 photo-electrons are emitted per second. Then even if the speed is so fast that the electrons reach collector instantaneously, still only 100 electrons / second would reach the collector.
