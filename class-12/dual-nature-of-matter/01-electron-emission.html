Electron Emission

How electrons are liberated from the surface of a metal.

Metals and Free Electrons

In metals, the outer electrons of the constituent atoms form a "sea" of free electrons. These electrons are free to move throughout the entire volume of the metal, responsible for the high electrical and thermal conductivity of metals. However, these free electrons cannot casually escape the surface of the metal at room temperature.

If an electron attempts to cross the surface bound, the positive background lattice pulls it back. It experiences a net attractive force into the bulk of the material. A certain minimum amount of potential energy barrier must be overcome for an electron to break free completely.

The Work Function

The minimum external energy required by an electron to just escape from the metal surface is known as the work function of that metal.

Work Function (\( \Phi_0 \) or \( W \))

Definition: The minimum amount of energy required by an electron to just escape from the metal surface.

SI Unit: Joule (J), but practically measured in Electron Volts (eV). \( 1\text{ eV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J} \).

Dimensional Formula: \( [ML^2T^{-2}] \)

The work function depends primarily on:

  • The nature of the metal (its atomic structure).
  • The conditions of its surface (impurities, oxidation, etc).
Alkali metals like Cesium (Cs) have the lowest work function (about 2.14 eV), whereas Platinum (Pt) has the highest (about 5.65 eV).

Thermionic and Field Emission

Thermionic Emission

When a metal is heated to a very high temperature, the thermal energy supplied to the free electrons enables them to overcome the surface barrier and leave the metal. This process is called thermionic emission. The emitted electrons are called thermions.

Field Emission

If an extremely strong electric field (on the order of \( 10^8\text{ V/m} \)) is applied across a metal surface, it physically pulls the electrons out of the metal. Spark plugs and cold cathode emission rely on this principle.

Photoelectric and Secondary Emission

Photoelectric Emission

When electromagnetic radiation (light) of suitable high frequency (like ultraviolet light) is incident on a metal surface, electrons absorb the photon energy and are ejected. This is the photoelectric emission, and the ejected electrons are known as photoelectrons.

Secondary Emission

When fast-moving primary electrons strike a metal surface, they can transfer their kinetic energy to the free electrons within the metal. If this energy exceeds the work function, the original free electrons are ejected. These are called secondary electrons.
