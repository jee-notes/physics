Electron Emission

How electrons are forcefully ejected from the surface of a metal.

Metals and Free Electrons

In metals, the outer electrons of the constituent atoms form a "sea" of free electrons. These electrons are free to move throughout the entire volume of the metal, responsible for the high electrical and thermal conductivity of metals. However, these free electrons cannot casually escape the surface of the metal at room temperature.

Free electrons in a metal are not bound to individual atoms, but they are still confined within the metal due to an overall potential well created by the positive ion lattice.
They can move freely inside the metal, but need additional energy to escape outside it.

If an electron attempts to cross the surface bound, the positive background lattice pulls it back.

It experiences a net attractive force into the bulk of the material.

A certain minimum amount of potential energy barrier must be overcome for an electron to break free completely.

The Work Function

Work Function (\(\Phi\))

Definition: The minimum amount of external energy required by an electron to just escape from the metallic surface.

SI Unit: Joule (J), but practically measured in Electron Volts (eV). \( 1\text{ eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J} \).

Dimensional Formula: \( [ML^2T^{-2}] \)

Inside the metal, electrons are at a lower potential energy level and to escape the surface, they must climb an energy barrier equal to the work function.
However, the key concept here is - "minimum" energy required.

The work function depends primarily on:

  • The nature (material) of the metal (its atomic structure).
  • The conditions of its surface (impurities, oxidation, etc).

Alkali metals like Cesium (Cs) have the lowest work function (about 2.14 eV), whereas Platinum (Pt) has the highest (about 5.65 eV).

How the work function changes with other factors is not part of exam syllabus.
So, we will be treating work function to be constant for a given metal unless stated.
Purposefully, exams try to confuse by varying temperature, pressure etc. but we treat work function as constant.

Electron Emission

There are different mechanisms to forcefully eject free electrons out of the metal body. These mechanisms are called as electron emissions.

The emitted electrons are studied in a common framework like considering them as particles and measuring their mechanical energy, momentum, mass, charge etc.

These emissions are listed below and studied:

Thermionic Emission

When a metal is heated to a very high temperature, the thermal energy supplied to the free electrons enables them to overcome the surface barrier and leave the metal. This process is called thermionic emission. The emitted electrons are called thermions.

Field Emission

If an extremely strong electric field (on the order of \( 10^8\text{ V/m} \)) is applied to metallic surface, it lowers the potential barrier and ejects the surface electrons out of the metal. This is called field emission.

Photoelectric Emission

When electromagnetic radiation of sufficiently high frequency is incident on a metal surface, electrons absorb energy from the radiation and are emitted.

This phenomenon cannot be explained using classical wave theory of light and becomes the central focus of this chapter.

Secondary Emission

When fast-moving particles (like \(\alpha\) particles) strike a metal surface, they can transfer their kinetic energy to the free electrons within the metal. If this energy exceeds the work function, the original free electrons are ejected. These are called secondary electrons.

We have only photoelectric emission as part of exam syllabus.
Other emissions are mentioned because some aspects of photoelectric emission are similar to other emissions and so conclusions were made considering the entire body of knowledge.
