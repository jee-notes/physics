Photo Electric Emission

Forcefully ejecting electrons from the surface of a metal using light.

Metals and Free Electrons

In metals, the outermost electrons of the constituent atoms form a "sea" of free electrons.

These electrons are free to move throughout the entire volume of the metal, responsible for the high electrical and thermal conductivity of metals.

However, these "free" electrons cannot casually escape the surface of the metal at room temperature.

If an electron attempts to cross the surface boundary, the positive lattice background pulls it back. It experiences a net attractive force into the bulk of the material. This is known as the surface barrier.

Free electrons in a metal are not bound to individual atoms, but they are still confined within the metal due to an overall potential well created by the positive ion lattice.
They can move freely inside the metal, but need additional energy to escape outside it.

A certain minimum amount of potential energy barrier must be overcome for an electron to break free completely. The energy of an electron inside the metal is less than the energy of an electron at rest outside in the vacuum.

If electrons could escape easily at room temperature, metals would spontaneously become positively charged and "glow" with emitted charge.
The existence of the Work Function is what keeps matter stable.

The Work Function

Work Function (\(\Phi\))

Definition: The minimum amount of external energy required by an electron to just escape from the metallic surface.

SI Unit: Joule (J), but practically measured in Electron Volts (eV).

Dimensional Formula: \( [ML^2T^{-2}] \)

Inside the metal, electrons are at a lower potential energy level and to escape the surface, they must climb an energy barrier equal to the work function.
The key concept to note here is that, work function is associated with "minimum energy" requirement.
Minimum - indicates that if lesser energy is provided then the electron will certainly not escape.
However, it does not guarantee that if this much minimum energy is provided, the electron will certainly escape.
This is because the electron may lose energy by colliding with other electrons and in dissipation.

The work function depends primarily on:

  • The nature (material) of the metal (its atomic structure).
  • The conditions of its surface (impurities, oxidation, etc).

Alkali metals like Cesium (Cs) have the lowest work function (about 2.14 eV), whereas Platinum (Pt) has the highest (about 5.65 eV).

How the work function changes with other factors is not part of exam syllabus.
So, we will be treating work function to be constant for a given metal unless stated.
Purposefully, exams try to confuse by varying temperature, pressure etc. but we treat work function as constant.

Work Function is generally measured in eV (electron volt). One electron volt is the energy gained by an electron when it has been accelerated by a potential difference of 1 volt, so that :

\[ 1\text{ eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J} \]

Electron Emission

There are different mechanisms to forcefully eject free electrons out of the metal body. These mechanisms are called as electron emissions.

The emitted electrons are studied under a common framework like measuring their mechanical energy, momentum, mass, charge, e/m etc. These emission experiments were conducted before the discovery of electron itself by J.J.Thomson in 1897.

These emissions are listed below and studied:

Thermionic Emission

When a metal is heated to a very high temperature, the thermal energy supplied to the free electrons enables them to overcome the surface barrier and leave the metal. This process is called thermionic emission. The emitted electrons are called thermions.

Field Emission

If an extremely strong electric field (on the order of \( 10^8\text{ V/m} \)) is applied to metallic surface, it lowers the potential barrier and ejects the surface electrons out of the metal as in a Spark Plug. This is called field emission.

Photoelectric Emission

When electromagnetic radiation of sufficiently high frequency is incident on a metal surface, electrons absorb energy from the radiation and are emitted.

This phenomenon cannot be explained using classical wave theory of light and so, it is the central focus of this chapter.

Secondary Emission

When fast-moving particles (like \(\alpha\) particles) strike a metal surface, they can transfer their kinetic energy to the free electrons within the metal. If this energy exceeds the work function, the original free electrons are ejected. These are called secondary electrons.

We have only photoelectric emission as part of exam syllabus.
Other emissions are mentioned because some aspects of photoelectric emission are similar to other emissions and so conclusions were made considering the entire body of knowledge.

Historical Context: Photoelectric emission

The photoelectric effect was first discovered almost accidentally. In 1887, Heinrich Hertz was experimentally proving the existence of electromagnetic waves (the very discovery that cemented Maxwell's wave theory of light). He used an apparatus equipped with a spark gap.

Hertz observed that high-voltage sparks passed across the detector gap more readily when the emitter plate was illuminated by ultraviolet light from an arc lamp. The incident ultraviolet light facilitated the escape of free electrons from the metal surface, which allowed the spark discharge to occur more easily. This was the first empirical observation of photoelectric emission.

Hallwachs' and Lenard's Observations

Between 1888 and 1902, Wilhelm Hallwachs and Philipp Lenard conducted detailed investigations into this phenomenon.

Hallwachs' Experiment: He connected a negatively charged zinc plate to an electroscope. When illuminated by UV light, the plate quickly lost its charge. If initially uncharged, it gained a positive charge. If initially positively charged, it became more positive. He concluded that the light caused negative particles to be emitted from the zinc plate.

Lenard's Experiment: Lenard constructed an evacuated glass tube containing two metal electrodes (emitter and collector). When UV light struck the emitter, a current flowed in the external circuit. The current ceased as soon as the light was turned off. Lenard noted that this current only flowed if the light's frequency was sufficiently high.

Defining the Photoelectric Effect

From these early observations, the core phenomenon was clearly defined.

The Photoelectric Effect

Definition: The phenomenon of emission of electrons from the surface of a metal when light of a suitable frequency falls upon it is called the photoelectric effect.

Remember: The emitted particles are standard electrons. They are termed "photoelectrons" solely to indicate their origin—they were emitted due to incident light.

Basic Circuit Components

The core apparatus for studying the photoelectric effect involves:

  • Evacuated Glass Tube: Contains the electrodes in a vacuum to prevent electrons colliding with gas molecules.
  • Photosensitive Cathode (Emitter): The surface that emits electrons when illuminated.
  • Anode (Collector): The electrode that collects the emitted electrons, allowing current to flow.
  • Variable Voltage Source: To change the potential difference between the anode and cathode.
  • Microammeter: To detect the tiny photoelectric current propagating through the circuit.
