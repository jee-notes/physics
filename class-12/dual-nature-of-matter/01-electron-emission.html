Photo Electric Emission

Forcefully ejecting electrons from the surface of a metal using light.

Metals and Free Electrons

In metals, the electrons of the outermost shell of the constituent atoms form a "sea" of free electrons.

These electrons are free to move within the bulk volume of metal and this free nature is responsible for the high electrical and thermal conductivity of metals.

However, these "free" electrons cannot casually escape the surface of the metal at room temperature.

The Surface Barrier

Consider an electron in the metal near its surface attempting to cross the surface boundary. The remaining bulk metal in the background becomes relatively positive and pulls the escaping electron back. The electron must overcome this attractive interaction to leave the metal completely..

The potential energy of an electron inside the metal is lower than the potential energy of an electron at rest outside in vacuum. The transition from the bulk to the vacuum therefore involves an increase in potential energy. This energy barrier at the surface is called the surface barrier.

Inside the metal, the potential energy of a free electron is constant throughout the bulk. As the electron approaches the surface and moves outside into vacuum, its potential energy rises.

We can visualise this as an idealised step-like potential-energy profile:

\[ U(x) = \begin{cases} U_{\text{bulk}}, & \text{inside the metal}\\ U_{\text{vacuum}}, & \text{outside the metal} \end{cases} \]

where \(U_{\text{vacuum}}\) is higher than \(U_{\text{bulk}}\) .

Inside the bulk, the potential energy of the entire region is uniform. Therefore, an electron can move throughout this equipotential region without any change requirement on energy interaction.

Just outside the surface, there is sudden jump in potential energy. This jump forms a barrier that must be overcome for escaping the surface. Its like a flat ground with \(5\text{ m}\) high walls at the boundary.

If electrons could escape easily at room temperature, metals would spontaneously become positively charged and "glow" with emitted charge.
The existence of Surface Barrier is what keeps matter stable.

If any electron is outside the surface, it means that it has paid the surface-barrier "toll". We can conclude that energy was provided to that electron and it used it up to eject itself out from the metal body.

The Work Function

Definition: The Work function (\(\Phi\)) of a metallic surface is the minimum amount of external energy required by an electron to just escape from the metallic surface.

  • SI Unit: Joule (J), but practically measured in Electron Volts (eV).
  • Dimensional Formula: \( [ML^2T^{-2}] \)

The work function is the energy requirement associated with a single electron, not the total energy required to remove all the free electrons from a metal. It is also important to remember that it represents the minimum energy requirement among the occupied electron states.

Minimum is the key concept associated with the work function. It has several important implications. And to understand them we need to imagine having some mechanism through which we can provide energy to an individual electron.

  1. Minimum Energy Threshold: This is the common and correct interpretation. The work function represents the minimum energy requirement for electron emission. If an energy smaller than the work function is provided to any electron, it certainly cannot be ejected out. The electron will absorb the energy and lose it in collisions within the metal body, thus increasing the temperature of the bulk.
  2. However, even if we impart energy equal to the work function to any arbitrary electron , it doesn't guarantee emission of that electron.
  3. This is because the work function represents the minimum escape-energy requirement. Electrons that are bound more tightly require more energy to escape. Its same like that flat ground. The wall height is \(5\text{ m}\) but different people in the ground have different heights and taller people need lesser energy. Work function represents the energy required by the tallest person.
  4. In terms of Surface Barrier, Work function is the surface barrier of the most loosely bound electron.
  5. In Photo-electric Effect Experiment, we do not transfer energy on single electron basis but a huge population of electrons collectively interact. In such setting, we are certain to eject certain electrons that are most loosely bound.
  6. This concept is used later to formulate Einstein's photo-electric effect equation of energy conservation. The least-bound electron, which has the minimum energy requirement represented by the work function, emerges with the maximum kinetic energy.

Factors Affecting Work Function

The work function is primarily a property of the metallic material and its surface conditions. Different metals have different electronic structures and consequently different work functions.

The work function depends primarily on:

  • Nature of the Metal: Different metals have different electronic structures and different energy requirements for removing their electrons.
  • Surface Conditions: Impurities, oxidation, adsorbed particles and other surface conditions can alter the work function.

Therefore, when comparing work functions of different metals, we must consider their surface conditions. For standard comparisons, clean surfaces under specified conditions are assumed.

Alkali metals such as Cesium (Cs) have among the lowest work functions (approximately \(2.14\text{ eV}\)), whereas metals such as Platinum (Pt) have relatively high work functions (approximately \(5.65\text{ eV}\)).

The work function can depend on factors such as surface condition and temperature.
However, unless specified in exam questions, we treat the work function as constant for a given metal surface.
Some questions try to confuse on this point.

Electron Volt as a Unit of Work Function

The work function is commonly expressed in electron volts (eV) because it represents an energy requirement for an individual electron, and electron volts provide a convenient unit for microscopic energy scales.

One electron volt is the energy gained by an electron when it is accelerated through a potential difference of one volt.

\[ 1\text{ eV}=1.6\times10^{-19}\text{ J} \]

Thus, work function can be expressed either in joules or electron volts, depending on convenience.

Electron Emission

There are different mechanisms through which free electrons can be forcefully ejected from the surface of a metal. These mechanisms are collectively known as electron emission.

Electron Emission vs Atomic Ionisation
Students often confuse the work function with ionisation energy, which they study in Chemistry.
Although both involve removing an electron, they describe different situations.
Work Function: The minimum energy required to remove an electron from a metallic solid surface into vacuum.
Ionisation Energy: The energy required to remove an electron from an isolated gaseous atom or ion.
In the electron-emission processes studied here, we are concerned with the ejection of free or conduction electrons of the metal.
These electrons are already delocalised and not associated with a particular atom.
We are not removing an electron by ionising an individual constituent atom or chemically decomposing the material.
Thus, electron emission is treated as a physical process involving the escape of electrons from a metallic surface.

The emitted electrons are identical irrespective of the mechanism through which they are emitted. They possess the same fundamental properties, such as mass and charge. Their mechanical energy, momentum and charge-to-mass ratio (\(e/m\)) can be studied using suitable experimental arrangements.

There are several mechanisms of electron emission. We just introduce them briefly, although photoelectric emission is the mechanism studied in detail in this chapter.

Thermionic Emission

When a metal is heated to a sufficiently high temperature, energy is supplied to the free electrons within the metal. As a result, the electrons acquire different amounts of kinetic energy through their interactions within the bulk.

Some electrons acquire sufficient energy to overcome the surface barrier and escape from the metal into vacuum. This process is called thermionic emission. The emitted electrons are called thermions.

Thus, thermionic emission uses thermal energy to facilitate the escape of free electrons from a metallic surface.

Field Emission

When an extremely strong electric field is applied to a metallic surface, it modifies the surface potential barrier. In particular, the barrier becomes sufficiently lower and thinner to allow electrons to escape.

This phenomenon is called field emission. The electric field facilitates the escape of electrons without requiring them to acquire the full barrier energy through conventional thermal excitation.

Photoelectric Emission

When electromagnetic radiation of sufficiently high frequency is incident on a metal surface, electrons absorb energy from the incident radiation. If an electron acquires sufficient energy to overcome its escape-energy requirement, it can leave the metal surface.

This process is called photoelectric emission. The emitted electrons are called photoelectrons.

The experimentally observed features of photoelectric emission cannot be explained using wave model of light.

Secondary Emission

When fast-moving particles, such as alpha particles, strike a metallic surface, they can transfer some of their kinetic energy to the electrons within the metal.

If an electron receives sufficient energy to overcome its escape-energy requirement, it can be ejected from the metal surface. These emitted electrons are called secondary electrons, and the process is known as secondary emission.

The incident particle supplies the energy, while the electron emitted from the metal is the secondary electron. Thus, the name describes the origin of the emitted electron rather than a different type of electron.

Historical Context: Photoelectric Emission

The photoelectric effect was first observed almost accidentally. In 1887, Heinrich Hertz was conducting experiments to establish the existence of electromagnetic waves predicted by Maxwell's electromagnetic theory.

Hertz used a spark transmitter to generate electromagnetic waves and a separate receiver apparatus equipped with a small spark gap to detect them. The detection of electromagnetic waves was indicated by a spark appearing across the receiver gap.

During these experiments, Hertz noticed something unexpected: the sparks in the receiver gap became stronger when ultraviolet light from the transmitter spark was allowed to reach the receiver. When the ultraviolet radiation was blocked, the detected sparks became weaker.

Further investigation established that it was specifically the ultraviolet component of the radiation that produced this effect. Ordinary glass could block the ultraviolet radiation, whereas quartz allowed it to pass.

The observation suggested that ultraviolet radiation was facilitating the electrical discharge across the receiver gap. We now understand this phenomenon in terms of electrons being emitted from the metal surfaces under the influence of incident radiation.

An Interesting Historical Connection
Hertz was investigating electromagnetic waves to establish Maxwell's theory. During these experiments, he accidentally observed a phenomenon that later became one of the important experimental foundations of quantum physics.
The irony is that an experiment intended to establish the wave description of light led to the discovery of a phenomenon that classical wave theory could not explain satisfactorily.

Hallwachs' and Lenard's Observations

Following Hertz's observation, Wilhelm Hallwachs and Philipp Lenard investigated photoelectric emission in greater detail between 1888 and 1902.

Hallwachs' Experiment: Hallwachs connected a negatively charged zinc plate to an electroscope. When the plate was illuminated with ultraviolet light, it quickly lost its negative charge.

He also observed that an initially uncharged zinc plate became positively charged when illuminated with ultraviolet light. An initially positively charged plate became more positively charged.

From these observations, Hallwachs concluded that ultraviolet light caused negative particles to leave the zinc plate.

At that time, the electron had not yet been identified. After Thomson's discovery of the electron in 1897, these emitted negative particles could be identified as electrons.

Lenard's Experiment: Lenard developed an evacuated glass tube containing two metallic electrodes: an emitter and a collector. When ultraviolet light fell on the emitter, electrons were emitted from its surface and travelled towards the collector.

The collection of these electrons produced an electric current in the external circuit. Lenard observed that the current ceased when the incident light was switched off.

He also investigated the dependence of electron emission on the frequency of incident radiation and observed that emission occurred only when the frequency was sufficiently high.

These investigations transformed the initial observation into a phenomenon that could be studied systematically through electrical measurements.

Photoelectric Effect Experiment

The observations of Hertz, Hallwachs and Lenard led to systematic experimental investigations of photoelectric emission. The phenomenon could now be studied through carefully controlled experiments and quantitative measurements.

Definition: The phenomenon of emission of electrons from the surface of a material when electromagnetic radiation of suitable frequency falls upon it is called the photoelectric effect.

Detecting Emitted Electrons

An electron is an individual microscopic particle. In an ordinary laboratory experiment, we do not directly track every emitted electron. Instead, we detect their emission through their collective electrical effect.

When electrons are emitted from a metallic surface, they can be collected by another electrode. Their movement through the external circuit produces an electric current that can be measured.

However, current can also flow through an ordinary conductor when a potential difference is applied across it. Therefore, we need an experimental arrangement that allows us to distinguish the current resulting from photoelectric emission.

Scientists combined two physical phenomena to achieve this:

  • Photoelectric Emission: Incident light causes electrons to escape from the photosensitive metal surface.
  • Electrical Conduction: An applied potential difference enables the emitted electrons to move through the external circuit, producing a measurable current.

By combining these phenomena in a controlled experimental arrangement, the emission of electrons can be investigated by measuring the resulting photoelectric current.

Experimental Apparatus

The basic apparatus for studying the photoelectric effect consists of the following components:

  • Evacuated Glass Tube: Contains the electrodes in a vacuum so that emitted electrons can travel between them without significant collisions with gas molecules.
  • Photosensitive Cathode (Emitter): The metallic surface from which electrons are emitted when illuminated by suitable radiation.
  • Anode (Collector): Collects the photoelectrons emitted by the cathode. Their arrival produces current in the external circuit.
  • Variable Voltage Source: Provides a controllable potential difference between the cathode and anode. This allows us to control the motion and collection of emitted electrons.
  • Microammeter: Measures the small photoelectric current flowing through the external circuit.

Using this arrangement, we can investigate how photoelectric current and the kinetic energy of emitted electrons depend on the frequency and intensity of incident radiation.
