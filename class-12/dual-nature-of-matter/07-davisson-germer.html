HCV Conceptual Questions

It is difficult to get HCV conceptual questions solved for this chapter as they are mixed with relativity chapter. Relativity chapter is outside the scope of JEE and so it becomes difficult to comprehend the questions.

1. Can we find the mass of a photon by the definition \(p = mv\)?

No. The equation \(p = mv\) applies strictly to particles with a non-zero rest mass because, here "\(m\)" is mass of the particle when it is at rest.

A photon is a quantum of electromagnetic radiation and has a rest mass exactly equal to zero.

Its momentum is defined fundamentally by its wave properties using the de Broglie relation \(p = \frac{h}{\lambda}\) or from its energy \(p = \frac{E}{c}\).

Even when we attempt to calculate a "mass" using \(\left(mc = \frac{h}{\lambda}\right)\), we are calculating an "equivalent relativistic mass", not a true rest mass.

2. Is it always true that for two sources of equal intensity, the number of photons emitted in a given time are equal?

No. Intensity is the total energy crossing a unit area per unit time (\(I = \frac{n \cdot E}{A \cdot t}\)), where \(n/t\) is the number of photons per second and \(E = h\nu\) is the energy of a single photon.

If the two sources emit light of different frequencies, their individual photons carry different amounts of energy.

To maintain the same overall intensity, the source with the lower frequency (lower energy per photon) must emit a greater number of photons per given time.

3. What is the speed of a photon with respect to another photon if
(a) the two photons are going in the same direction and
(b) they are going in opposite directions?

In both cases (a) and (b), the relative speed is \(c\) (the speed of light in a vacuum).

According to the postulates of the Special Theory of Relativity, the speed of light is invariant and measures as \(c\) in all inertial frames of reference.

Classical Galilean relative velocity formulas (like \(c - c = 0\) or \(c + c = 2c\)) fail completely for light.

4. Can a photon be deflected by an electric field? By a magnetic field?

No to both. Photons are quanta of electromagnetic energy and carry zero net electric charge.

The Lorentz force (\(F = qE + qv \times B\)) requires a non-zero charge (\(q\)) to act upon, photons travel entirely undeflected through both electric and magnetic fields.

5. A hot body is placed in a closed room maintained at a lower temperature. Is the number of photons in the room increasing?

Yes. The hot body will continuously radiate high-energy photons (e.g., infrared) to cool down, while the colder room absorbs them.

Because the total energy is conserved, when the colder room walls absorb a single high-energy photon, they will eventually re-radiate that energy as multiple lower-energy photons to reach thermal equilibrium.

Thus, the total count of photons in the system increases as entropy increases.

6. Should the energy of a photon be called its kinetic energy or its internal energy?

This is a common doubt because when we learn about photon, we imagine it to be a particle that can hold (contain) kinetic energy or internal energy like particle matter.

But, photon is a packet of energy itself and its interactions with quantum particles is analogous to mechanical interactions involving energy and momentum exchange. A photon represents kinetic energy of electromagnetic field; it cannot exist at rest.

All of its energy (\(E = h\nu\)) is tied to its propagation through space at the speed \(c\). It lacks a rest mass or any internal structure that could store "internal energy" in the classical thermodynamic sense.

7. In an experiment on photoelectric effect, a photon is incident on an electron from one direction and the photoelectron is emitted almost in the opposite direction. Does this violate conservation of momentum?

No, it does not. The apparent paradox arises from treating the incident photon and the single ejected electron as the entire system.

The law of conservation of momentum applies strictly to an entire isolated system. In the photoelectric effect, the system includes the massive, macroscopic metal lattice (the crystal) from which the electron is bound.

When the electron is ejected backward, the massive metal lattice recoils in the direction of the incident photon to perfectly balance the momentum.

Because the mass of the lattice is enormously large compared to an electron, its recoil velocity is practically zero, making it unnoticeable, but it successfully absorbs the required momentum to satisfy conservation laws.

8. It is found that yellow light does not eject photoelectrons from a metal. Is it advisable to try with orange light? With green light?

If yellow light fails, it means the energy of a yellow photon (\(E = \frac{hc}{\lambda}\)) is less than the work function (\(\Phi\)) of that specific metal. We need photons with higher energy.

Orange light: It is not advisable. Orange light has a longer wavelength (and thus a lower frequency) than yellow light. Its photons carry even less energy than yellow photons and will certainly fail to cause emission.

Green light: It is advisable. Green light has a shorter wavelength (and a higher frequency) than yellow light. Its photons carry more energy, which might be sufficient to exceed the threshold and trigger the photoelectric effect.

9. It is found that photosynthesis starts in certain plants when exposed to the sunlight but it does not start if the plant is exposed only to infrared light. Explain.

Photosynthesis is a photochemical process that behaves much like the photoelectric effect. It requires a specific minimum "activation energy" per photon to successfully break and form the necessary chemical bonds in chlorophyll molecules.

Sunlight contains a broad spectrum, including visible light, which possesses high-frequency, high-energy photons capable of triggering this complex chemical reaction.

Infrared light, on the other hand, consists of low-frequency, low-energy photons. No matter how intense the infrared light source is (how many photons hit the plant per second), the individual photons lack the threshold energy required to initiate the reaction.

10. The threshold wavelength of a metal is \(\lambda_0\). Light of wavelength slightly less than \(\lambda_0\) is incident on an insulated plate made of this metal. It is found that photoelectrons are emitted for sometime and after that the emission stops. Explain.

Initially, because the incident wavelength \(\lambda \lt \lambda_0\), the incoming photons have enough energy to overcome the work function and successfully eject electrons from the metal.

However, the key detail is that the plate is insulated. This means it has no connection to the ground and cannot replenish the electrons it is losing to the surrounding space.

As electrons are continuously ejected, the insulated plate develops a growing net positive charge. This accumulating positive charge creates a strong, attractive electric field (a retarding potential) that pulls back on the escaping electrons.

Eventually, this positive potential becomes equal in magnitude to the stopping potential of the fastest emitted electrons. At this point, even the most energetic photoelectrons are pulled back into the metal, completely halting any further net emission.

11. Is \(p = E/c\) valid for electrons?

No. The relationship \(p = E/c\) is derived specifically for particles with a rest mass exactly equal to zero, such as photons.

For a particle with a non-zero rest mass \(m_0\) like an electron, and also moving with speed \(v\) comparable to \(c\), the general relativistic energy-momentum relationship is used: \(E^2 = (pc)^2 + (m_0c^2)^2\).

This \(E\) is not just the kinetic energy, but the total energy on account of its motion and mass-equivalence.

We can use equivalent relativistic mass \(m'\) where \(E = m'c^2\) and moving with velocity \(v\) where momentum \(p = m'v\).

We get \((m'c^2)^2 = (m'vc)^2 + (m_0c^2)^2\).

\[ m'^2(c^2 - v^2) = m_0^2 c^2 \Rightarrow m' = \frac{m_0}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}} \]

For standard, non-relativistic physics problems where the electron is moving significantly slower than the speed of light, its momentum is related to its kinetic energy (\(K\)) via classical mechanics: \(p = \sqrt{2m_0K}\).

12. Consider the de Broglie wavelength of an electron and a proton. Which wavelength is smaller if the two particles have
(a) the same speed
(b) the same momentum
(c) the same energy?

The core principle here is the de Broglie relation \(\lambda = \frac{h}{p}\), combined with the fact that the mass of a proton is significantly greater than the mass of an electron (\(m_p \gg m_e\)).

(a) Same speed (\(v\)): Momentum is defined as \(p = mv\). Since the proton has a much larger mass, it possesses a much larger momentum (\(p_p > p_e\)). A larger momentum results in a smaller wavelength. Therefore, the proton has the smaller wavelength.

(b) Same momentum (\(p\)): The de Broglie wavelength depends solely on momentum. Since their momentums are identical, their wavelengths are exactly equal.

(c) Same kinetic energy (\(K\)): Momentum can be expressed in terms of kinetic energy as \(p = \sqrt{2mK}\). Since \(K\) is constant for both, the particle with the larger mass will have the larger momentum (\(p_p > p_e\)). Therefore, the proton has the smaller wavelength.

13. If an electron has a wavelength, does it also have a colour?

No. The concept of "colour" is a biological and physiological perception. It is created by our brains when the photoreceptors in our retina interact with specific wavelengths of electromagnetic waves (photons) in the visible spectrum.

The de Broglie wavelength of an electron does not represent an electromagnetic wave. Instead, it represents a matter wave—a quantum mechanical probability amplitude that describes the likelihood of finding the particle at a certain point in space.

Because an electron's matter wave is fundamentally different from light and cannot interact with the eye's retinal chemistry to produce visual sensations, the concept of color simply does not apply to it.
