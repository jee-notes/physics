Light Energy Parameters

Intensity and Frequency of light.

Waves Chapter Revision

This section is a targeted revision of Mechanical and EM Waves. To understand why the Photoelectric effect is "revolutionary", we must first be firm on the classical parameters of a wave.

The Fundamental Wave Parameters

  • Frequency (\(\nu\)): Dictated by the source. This determines the color of the wave.
  • Wavelength (\(\lambda\)): The distance between consecutive peaks. This determines the speed of the wave in a medium.
  • Amplitude (\(A\)): The maximum displacement of the oscillation. This determines the energy carried by the wave.

Wave Propagation

Waves are a form of energy propagation. In mechanical string waves, we studied 1D propagation over a string. However, light waves and sound waves propagate in space according to Huygens' wavefront model.

All electromagnetic waves propagate in vacuum with constant speed \(c = 3 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}\). The wavelength and frequency of EM waves are related by \(c = \nu\lambda\).

In different media, the speed of light changes, while frequency remains the same as it is determined by the source. As a result, the wavelength changes accordingly.

Intensity: A Universal Wave Property

Intensity is a physical quantity applicable to all wave phenomena (Sound, Water, Light). It quantifies the "Energy Flux" of the wave.

Physical Definition:
Intensity (\(I\)) is the energy crossing per unit area per unit time perpendicular to the direction of propagation. \[ I = \frac{\text{Energy}}{\text{Area} \times \text{Time}} = \frac{\text{Power}}{\text{Area}} \]

If we consider a cylindrical volume with area of cross-section A and length \(c\Delta t\) along the X-axis. Then, the energy contained in this cylinder crosses the area A in time \(\Delta t\) as the wave propagates at speed c. The energy contained is:

\[ U = u_{\text{av}}(c\Delta t)A \] \[ I = \frac{U}{A\Delta t} = u_{\text{av}}(c) = \frac{1}{2} \epsilon_0 E_0^2 c \]

In the context of wave model, if we want to increase the energy delivered to a surface, we increase the Amplitude (Intensity).
This corresponds to increasing the energy incident on the surface per unit time.
The frequency is merely a characteristic of oscillation and does not determine the incident energy on the surface per unit time.
This is because energy is continuously distributed in the wave field, and not carried per cycle.
If we increase frequency, the number of oscillations per second increases, but each oscillation occurs over a shorter time interval, so the total energy incident per unit time remains unchanged.

Frequency of EM Waves

The EM Spectrum is a continuous range of frequencies all propagating with speed \(c = 3 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}\) in vacuum. We divide this spectrum into regions based on their interaction and applications.

Outside the Visible Spectrum:
Frequencies are primarily divided based on their practical usage. For example:
  • Radio Waves: Low frequency, long wavelength; used for signal transmission.
  • X-Rays: High frequency, high penetration; used for medical imaging.
Within the Visible Spectrum:
Frequencies are divided based on Colour. The human brain translates a frequency of roughly \(4 \times 10^{14}\text{ Hz}\) as Red and \(7.5 \times 10^{14}\text{ Hz}\) as Violet.
The "Frequency Rule" for this Chapter:
In Dual Nature, Frequency is the most important parameter.
  • High Frequency (\(\nu\)) = High Energy Photons = Potential for Photoelectric emission.
  • Low Frequency (\(\nu\)) = Low Energy Photons = No emission, regardless of Intensity.

It is vital to remember that while Wavelength (\(\lambda\)) changes when light enters a medium, Frequency (\(\nu\)) remains constant. Thus, the behaviour of light in this chapter is determined by its frequency.

Source

We first need to study the sources of light to understand the energy distribution of the radiation we use in experiments.

Polychromatic Source:
An Incandescent bulb is polychromatic. It produces light through thermal radiation. The tungsten filament is heated until it glows, emitting a continuous "smear" of many different frequencies/colors simultaneously.

Monochromatic Source:
A Sodium lamp is monochromatic. It produces light through atomic excitation. Electrons in the sodium atoms jump between specific energy levels, emitting light of a very specific frequency (dominantly the yellow D-lines).
To study the Photoelectric Effect, we need a Monochromatic source.
If we used a polychromatic source, we wouldn't know which frequency was responsible for ejecting the electrons.
By using a monochromatic source, we ensure that every photon hitting the metal has the exact same energy (\(E = h\nu\)).

Chromaticity is essentially a measure of the "purity" of the light's color.

A laser has the highest chromatic purity, while the sun or a bulb has low purity because they are mixtures of the entire EM spectrum.

Intensity: Human Perspective

Just like loudness is a measure of intensity for sound waves, brightness is a measure of intensity for light waves.

But, as there is a range of frequencies that are audible or visible, they are not used to measure intensity in general.

Hence, we have decibels to measure loudness and luminosity to measure brightness.

Both are measures of intensity, but calibrated to human senses.

The "Invisible" High Intensity:
It could be that UV-light or Infrared-light of very high intensity is incident, but not detected by human eyes as it falls outside the visible spectrum.
To a human, this would be "total darkness," but to a metal surface in this chapter, it could be a massive energy bombardment.

In exam problems, we must ignore "brightness" (how it looks) and focus on "Intensity" (how much energy hits the area).

In the Photoelectric Effect, an "invisible" UV light can do what a "blinding" Red light cannot.

Variable Control in Experiments

There is a contradiction between the wave model and the photon model in how intensity and frequency are controlled.

Photon Model:
In the photon model, intensity is a function of both frequency and photon flux.
\(I = \Phi_{P}\cdot h\nu\)
Wave Model:
In the wave model, intensity depends only on amplitude and not on frequency.
\(I = \frac{1}{2} \epsilon_0 E_0^2 c\)

In the photoelectric experiment, we study the effects of changing frequency and intensity.

This creates a conflict because, as per the photon model, intensity can be changed by varying either frequency or photon flux. In the wave model, we can change frequency while keeping amplitude \(E_0\) the same, and thus keep intensity unchanged.

To resolve this, variables are assumed to be carefully controlled unless explicitly mentioned:

  • Intensity Control: Change the brightness (number of photons) while keeping frequency fixed.
  • Frequency Control: Change the color (energy per photon) while keeping photon flux (and hence intensity) fixed.
