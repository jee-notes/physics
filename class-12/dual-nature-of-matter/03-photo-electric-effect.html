Photo-Electric Effect Experiment

Investigating how light intensity and frequency affect photoelectron emission.

Experiment Setup

The idea behind the photoelectric effect experiment is to combine photoelectric emission with electrical conduction in an external circuit. This allows us to investigate the emission of electrons by measuring the resulting photoelectric current.

The standardised experimental arrangement consists of an evacuated glass tube fitted with a quartz window.

The quartz window is required because ultraviolet radiation is used in this experiment. Ordinary glass is opaque to UV radiation, whereas quartz is transparent to it thus allowing it to reach the metal plate.

Inside the evacuated tube, two electrodes are fitted:

  • Cathode (C) — Emitter: A photosensitive metallic plate from which electrons are emitted when suitable monochromatic light falls on it.
  • Anode (A) — Collector: The second electrode, which collects the photoelectrons emitted from the cathode.

Monochromatic light is directed through the quartz window onto the photosensitive cathode C. When photoelectrons are emitted, they can travel through the evacuated space towards the collector plate A.

The two electrodes are connected to an external electrical circuit containing a microammeter, a variable DC voltage source and a commutator switch.

Role of the External Circuit

When photoelectrons travel from the cathode towards the anode, their collection completes the electrical circuit and produces a measurable current. The microammeter detects this small photoelectric current.

The variable DC voltage source allows the experimenter to control the potential difference between the two electrodes. This is important because the applied potential influences the motion and collection of photoelectrons.

The commutator switch allows us to reverse the polarity of the applied voltage. Consequently, the collector plate A can be made either positive or negative with respect to the emitter plate C.

Thus, by controlling both the magnitude and polarity of the applied potential difference, we can study how the photoelectric current changes under different electrical conditions.

Photoelectric Experiment Observations

The photoelectric effect experiment is based on measuring the photoelectric current using a microammeter and studying how it changes when we manipulate different experimental variables.

The three variables involved in the experiment are:

  1. Intensity of Incident Light: \(I\).
  2. Frequency of Incident Light: \(\nu\).
  3. Potential Difference Between the Electrodes: \(V_c-V_e=V\), also called the tube voltage.

Establishing the V-i Characteristic

We first keep the light-related parameters fixed and investigate how the photoelectric current varies with the applied tube voltage. For this purpose, we illuminate the photosensitive emitter with monochromatic light of constant intensity \(I\) and frequency \(\nu\), such that \(\nu>\nu_0\)

Here, \(\nu_0\) is the threshold frequency of the emitter material. We now vary only the tube voltage \(V\) and observe the resulting photoelectric current \(i\).

Initially, we maintain zero potential difference between the collector and emitter plates: \(V=V_c-V_e=0\)

Monochromatic light is directed onto the emitter cathode C, causing photoelectrons to be emitted. We measure the resulting photoelectric current using the microammeter.

We then gradually increase the tube voltage in the positive direction, making the collector anode A increasingly positive with respect to the emitter C. We observe how the photocurrent changes.

Next, we reverse the polarity of the applied voltage using the commutator. The collector is now made negative with respect to the emitter. We gradually increase the magnitude of this negative voltage and observe how the photocurrent changes.

Experimental Observations

The following observations are obtained from the experiment:

  1. Non-zero Current at Zero Applied Voltage:

    Even when the tube voltage is zero (\(V=0\)), a non-zero photoelectric current is detected.

    \[ V=0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad i\neq 0 \]

    This is because emitted photoelectrons already possess initial kinetic energy. Some of them can reach the collector even without an externally applied accelerating potential.

  2. Increasing Current with Positive Voltage:

    As we increase the positive tube voltage, the collector attracts photoelectrons more strongly. Consequently, a greater fraction of the emitted electrons reaches the collector, and the photoelectric current increases.

  3. Current Becomes Independent of Further Voltage Increase:

    After a certain positive voltage is reached, the photoelectric current stops increasing significantly, even when the tube voltage is increased further.

  4. Saturation Current:

    At sufficiently high positive voltage, essentially all the emitted photoelectrons are collected by the anode. The photocurrent reaches its maximum value and the V-i graph becomes approximately flat.

    This maximum value of photocurrent is called the saturation current, denoted by \(i_s\).

  5. Decreasing Current with Negative Voltage:

    We now make the collector negative with respect to the emitter (\(V<0\)). The electric field opposes the motion of photoelectrons towards the collector. Consequently, fewer electrons reach the collector and the photocurrent decreases.

  6. Stopping Potential:

    As the magnitude of the negative voltage increases, a stage is reached when even the most energetic photoelectrons cannot reach the collector. The photoelectric current becomes zero.

    The magnitude of this minimum retarding potential required to stop the most energetic photoelectrons is called the stopping potential, denoted by \(V_0\).

    Since our tube-voltage convention is \(V=V_c-V_e\), the actual applied voltage at the stopping condition is:

    \[ \boxed{V=-V_0} \]

    The stopping potential is related to the maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons:

    \[ \boxed{K_{\max}=eV_0} \]
Understanding the V-i Characteristic

The experiment gives us a complete V-i profile for one particular combination of light intensity and frequency.

Throughout this investigation, both light parameters are kept constant:

\[ I=\text{constant}, \qquad \nu=\text{constant}>\nu_0 \]

Only the tube voltage is varied. We obtain a current that first increases, eventually saturates, and then decreases under retarding potential until it becomes zero at the stopping potential.

We shall now repeat this investigation for different values of light intensity and frequency and study how these changes affect the V-i characteristic, particularly its two important quantities: saturation current (\(i_s\)) and stopping potential (\(V_0\)).

Changing Light Parameters

We have established the V-i characteristic for a particular combination of light intensity and frequency. We now investigate how this characteristic changes when we modify the properties of the incident light.

Before proceeding, let us establish the notation used throughout this investigation. We have two experimentally measured quantities and three independent experimental variables.

  • Light Intensity: \(I\)
  • Light Frequency: \(\nu\)
  • Tube Voltage: \(V=V_c-V_e\)
  • Photocurrent: \(i\)
  • Saturation Current: \(i_s\)
  • Stopping Potential: \(V_0\), expressed as a positive magnitude.

For a particular combination of light intensity and frequency \((I,\nu)\), we obtain one complete V-i profile. We have already studied how this profile changes when the tube voltage is varied.

Now we investigate how changing the two light parameters, intensity (\(I\)) and frequency (\(\nu\)), affects the V-i profile. In particular, we want to understand how the two important quantities, saturation current (\(i_s\)) and stopping potential (\(V_0\)), respond to these changes.

Changing Light Intensity

First, we change only the intensity of incident light (\(I\)), while keeping its frequency (\(\nu\)) constant and above the threshold frequency: (\(\nu>\nu_0\))

This is achieved by changing the distance between the same monochromatic point source and the photosensitive surface, while keeping the frequency unchanged.

We now obtain two V-i profiles corresponding to two different intensities, \(I_1\) and \(I_2\), where: \(I_2 \gt I_1\) . The following observations are made:

  1. Saturation Current:

    The saturation current increases with the intensity of incident light. In the idealised experimental conditions, it is directly proportional to intensity.

    \[ \boxed{i_s\propto I} \]

    Therefore, a graph of saturation current against light intensity is a straight line passing through the origin.

  2. Stopping Potential:

    The stopping potential remains unchanged when the intensity of incident light is increased.

    \[ \boxed{V_0=\text{constant}} \]

    Thus, increasing light intensity increases the photoelectric current but does not increase the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electrons.

Changing Light Frequency

Next, we change the frequency of incident light while keeping its intensity constant.

Important: What does constant intensity mean here?

This comparison requires us to keep the photon flux constant rather than the incident radiant power.

Photon flux is the number of photons incident per unit area per unit time:

\[ \text{Photon Flux}=\frac{N}{A\Delta t} \]

As per convention, in photo electric experiment, constant intensity implies constant photon flux and not constant incident radiant power. We shall discuss this distinction in greater detail later.

We now obtain two V-i profiles corresponding to two different frequencies, \(\nu_1\) and \(\nu_2\), where:

\[ \nu_2>\nu_1>\nu_0 \]

The following observations are made:

  1. Saturation Current:

    The saturation current remains constant when the frequency is changed, provided the photon flux is maintained constant under the idealised experimental conditions.

    \[ \boxed{i_s=\text{constant}} \]
  2. Stopping Potential:

    The stopping potential increases with the frequency of incident light. The relationship is linear.

    \[ \boxed{V_0\propto \nu-\nu_0} \]

    Thus, higher-frequency photons produce photoelectrons with greater maximum kinetic energy, requiring a larger retarding potential to stop them.

Changing the Emitter Plate Material

Lastly, we change the material of the emitter plate. Different metals have different work functions (\(\Phi\)) and therefore different threshold frequencies.

We repeat the frequency experiment using different emitter materials and observe that the general pattern remains the same. This indicates that the observed relationship between photon frequency and electron kinetic energy is not restricted to a particular metal. It reflects a general property of the interaction between light and matter.

However, the V-i profiles and the stopping-potential versus frequency graphs are shifted because the work function differs between materials.

The stopping-potential relation can be expressed as:

\[ V_0=\frac{h}{e}\nu-\frac{\Phi}{e} \]

Therefore, different metals produce different stopping-potential versus frequency lines, but all the lines have the same slope:

\[ \boxed{\text{Slope}=\frac{h}{e}} \]

The difference lies in the intercept, which depends on the work function of the emitter material.

Experimental Results: Summary

We have now investigated how the two measured quantities, saturation current and stopping potential, depend on the two properties of incident light, intensity and frequency.

The Two-Parameter, Two-Observable Framework
Measured Quantity Changing Intensity (\(I\)) Changing Frequency (\(\nu\))
Saturation Current (\(i_s\)) Changes proportionally Constant
Stopping Potential (\(V_0\)) Constant Changes linearly
*For the frequency comparison, photon flux is maintained constant.

These observations establish two important distinctions:

  1. Saturation Current: It depends on the number of photoelectrons collected per unit time and therefore responds to the photon flux.
  2. Stopping Potential: It depends on the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electrons and therefore responds to the frequency of incident light.

These experimental relationships will help us understand why classical wave theory fails to explain photoelectric emission and why Einstein's photon theory becomes necessary.
