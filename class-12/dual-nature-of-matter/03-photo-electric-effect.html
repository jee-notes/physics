Experimental Study of Photoelectric Effect

Investigating how light intensity, frequency and electrical potential affect photoelectron emission.

Experimental Setup and Instrumentation

To systematically study the photoelectric effect, Physicists used a standardized experimental arrangement. It consists of an evacuated glass or quartz tube containing a photosensitive plate (C, the emitter cathode) and another metal plate (A, the collector anode).

A transparent window, usually made of quartz (to allow UV light passage unattenuated), lets monochromatic light fall onto plate C. The plates are connected to an external circuit containing a microammeter, a variable voltage source, and a commutator switch. The varying voltage source allows the experimenter to independently control the potential difference between the plates, while the commutator can reverse the polarity, making plate A either positive or negative with respect to plate C.

Photoelectric Experiment Observations

The experiment of Photo Electric Effect is based on measuring the photocurrent using micro-ammeter and studying how is affected by manipulating the experimental variables — specifically:

  1. Intensity of light (\( I \)).
  2. Frequency of light (\( \nu \)).
  3. Potential difference between the collector and emitter plates (\(V_c - V_e = V \)).

First, we maintain tube voltage = \(V_c - V_e = V = 0\). And, we throw incident monochromatic light on collector plate C. There are two observations found to defy wave theory:

  1. It was found that there exists a minimum threshold frequency \( \nu_0 \) below which no emission occurs, regardless of light intensity.
  2. It was found that photoelectric emission is an instantaneous process. The time lag between the incidence of light and electron emission is incredibly small, less than \( 10^{-9} \) seconds, even if the light intensity is extremely dim.

As it will be explained later, these observations were found to be contradicting the commonly accepted wave theory of light. But, we proceed with the experiment to uncover further contradicting observations. We do this by attempting to plot the V-I graph keeping Intensity (\( I \)) and Frequency (\( \nu \gt \nu_0 \)) of light constant.

Below observations are found:

  1. We keep \(V_c - V_e = V = 0\) and throw light on the emitter plate. Non-zero photocurrent (\( i \)) is detected. Thus, when \( V = 0 \) , \( i \neq 0 \)
  2. We keep increasing \( V \) and \( i \) is observed to increase as well.
  3. However, after a certain value of \( V \) , \( i \) does not increase even when \( V \) is increased further.
  4. The value of \( i \) saturates and the V-I graph goes flat. This value of photocurrent is called saturation current denoted by \( i_s \).
  5. Now we change \( V \) such that \(V_c - V_e = V \lt 0\) . It is observed that as we increase the retarding potential, photocurrent \( i \) decreases and at a particular value of \( V \) , it becomes exactly zero. This value of potential is called stopping potential and it's denoted by \( V_s \).

This is entirely the V-I profile keeping Intensity (\( I \)) and Frequency (\( \nu \gt \nu_0 \)) of incident light at a particular constant value.

Changing Light Parameters

The entire experiment was further performed by manipulating parameters of incident light and observing its impact on V-I profile.

Changing Light Intensity

First we change only The Light Intensity (\( I \)) and keeping Frequency (\( \nu \gt \nu_0 \)) of light constant. This is physically done just by changing the distance of the same point source of monochromatic light. Entire V-I profile is plotted for 2 different intensities \( I_1 \) and \( I_2 \) where \( I_2 \gt I_1 \) . It is observed that:

  1. Saturation current (\( i_s \)) is proportional to intensity.
  2. Stopping Potential (\( V_s \)) is constant and does not change with intensity.

Changing Light Frequency

The Light Frequency (\( \nu \)) is changed keeping Intensity constant. Note that this is a major source of confusion. Only for this particular part of experiment, intensity does not mean the incident power but photon intensity - as in the (no. of photons incident / second) is kept constant. It is explained later. Entire V-I profile is plotted for 2 different frequencies \( \nu_1 \) and \( \nu_2 \) where \( \nu_2 \gt \nu_1 \gt \nu_0 \) . It is observed that:

  1. Saturation current (\( i_s \)) is constant and does not change with frequency.
  2. Stopping Potential (\( V_s \)) varies linearly with frequency.

Changing Emitter Plate Metal

Lastly, we change the emitter plate metal that has a different work function \( \Phi \) . Similar patterns were observed. Only that the \( V_s \) vs \( \nu \) graph is different due to different \( \Phi \) . But it has the same slope.

Common Mechanism Framework

Alongwith Photo - Electric Emission, other Electronic Emissions were studied and a common framework was devised to study those.

Stopping Potential \( V_s \) existsted in other emissions. But, in case of photo - electric emission, it was found to be sharply varying with frequency only and not with intensity.

Stopping Potential is linked with the maximum Kinetic Energy of electron. When \( V = V_s \) , the electron with maximum KE is decelerated just enough to not reach the collector plate.

The fixed value of Stopping Potential independent of intensity was a major contradiction to wave theory of light.

The Collapse of Classical Wave Theory

Maxwell’s wave theory, while perfect for Interference and Diffraction, could not explain the "point-to-point" energy transfer seen here.

1. The Intensity Paradox
  • Wave Prediction: High intensity = high amplitude = more energy. Electrons should come out with more Kinetic Energy.
  • Experimental Reality: Intensity only increases the number of electrons; their individual Kinetic Energy stays the same.
2. The Frequency Paradox
  • Wave Prediction: Energy is continuous. Even low-frequency light should eventually "boil" off an electron if given enough time.
  • Experimental Reality: Below a specific Threshold Frequency (\(\nu_0\)), no emission occurs, even if the light is blindingly bright.
3. The Time-Lag Paradox (The "Soaking" Problem)
  • Wave Prediction: A wave spreads energy over the entire surface. A single electron would need to "soak up" energy for minutes or hours to reach the Work Function.
  • Experimental Reality: Emission is instantaneous (\(\lt 10^{-9}\) s). The energy is delivered in a single, localized hit.
