Properties of the Photon

Understanding the basic "particle" of electromagnetic radiation.

The Particle Nature of Light

The success of Einstein's photoelectric theory permanently established the photon model. In interaction with matter, radiation behaves as if it is made up of particles called photons.

Photons are fundamental gauge bosons. They travel through a vacuum at exactly the speed of light (\( c = 3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s} \)). Unlike classical particles, photons cannot be brought to rest; their rest mass is strictly zero. They only exist while moving.

Photon Energy and Momentum

Energy of a Photon

All photons of light of a particular frequency \( \nu \), or wavelength \( \lambda \), have the same energy:

\[ E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda} \]

Momentum of a Photon

Though massless, photons transport momentum. The momentum \( p \) of a photon is given by:

\[ p = \frac{E}{c} = \frac{h}{\lambda} \]

Because energy and momentum depend solely on frequency (or wavelength), all photons of a specific color are identical physical entities.

Radiation Pressure and Intensity

Because photons carry momentum, when they strike a material surface and are absorbed or reflected, they exert a force. The force per unit area is called Radiation Pressure.

In the photon picture, the intensity of light is determined by the *number* of photons.

Increasing the intensity of light of a given wavelength increases the number of photons crossing a given area per second. It does *not* increase the energy of the individual photons.

Wave-Particle Duality of Radiation

This leads us to the heart of wave-particle duality. Light poses a semantic problem: it exhibits properties of both waves and particles depending on the nature of the experiment.

In phenomena involving propagation, such as interference, diffraction, and polarization, light unmistakably behaves as a wave. However, in phenomena involving interaction with matter, such as the photoelectric effect or Compton scattering, light unmistakably behaves as a stream of particles (photons).

Both descriptions are necessary for a complete understanding of light. The wave theory predicts the probability of finding a photon at a particular point, linking the two seemingly contradictory models.
