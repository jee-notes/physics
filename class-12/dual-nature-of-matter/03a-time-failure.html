Soaking Model

Time Lag in Wave Model.

Wave model is Soaking model

To understand how the observations of photo electric effect contradict the wave model, we need to first understand what we expect from wave model.

Students think that the fact that Metal has characteristic work function \( \Phi \) is a contradiction to wave model of light.
This is clearly wrong way of thinking.
Metal having a characteristic work function is the property of metal and its internal design.
It does not indicate anything directly about nature of light.
Nature of light is determined by studying interaction of light with metal and knowing about characteristics of metal that are independent of nature of light.

The three failures frequency, intensity and time.

Soaking model

The soaking model of wave nature creates below expectations:

  1. There should be no threshold frequency : Wave model predicts continuous incidence of energy. And so, light of any frequency but sufficient intensity and in sufficient time should ultimately eject electrons.
  2. There should be time lag depending upon incident intensity : Wave model resembles heating a pot of water on a gas burner. Water has fixed boiling point and heat capacity similar to metal having fixed work function. If we heat water on a larger burner, we will take less time to boil.
  3. Stopping potential should depend on intensity : Stopping Potential depends sharply on frequency only. This is again a big blow because a continuous energy distribution is expected depending on intensity and time of incidence.
Basically, wave model predicts: Non- Impulsive Energy and Momentum Transfer.

The "Soaking" Analogy

In the classical wave model, light energy is distributed continuously across the wavefront. Imagine a wave washing over a beach; the energy is spread over every grain of sand. For an electron to escape, it must "soak up" this distributed energy until it accumulates enough to overcome the Work Function (\(\Phi\)).

The Classical Prediction:
  • Continuous Accumulation: Energy transfer is a slow, additive process.
  • Intensity Dependency: A brighter light (larger amplitude) should reduce the "soaking time."
  • Frequency Irrelevance: Even low-frequency light should eventually work if you wait long enough.

The Mathematical Contradiction

Let's perform a "Back-of-the-Envelope" calculation to see how long a wave would take to eject an electron.

Sample Calculation:
Suppose we shine a dim light of intensity \(I = 10^{-10} \text{ W/m}^2\) on a metal with \(\Phi = 2 \text{ eV}\).
1. Assume the target area of an electron is roughly the size of an atom (\(r \approx 10^{-10} \text{ m}\)).
2. Area \(A \approx \pi r^2 \approx 3 \times 10^{-20} \text{ m}^2\).
3. Energy absorbed per second \(P = I \times A = 3 \times 10^{-30} \text{ J/s}\).
4. Energy required (\(\Phi\)) \(\approx 3.2 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J}\).

Time Required: \[ t = \frac{\text{Energy required}}{\text{Energy per second}} \approx 10^8 \text{ seconds} \approx \mathbf{3 \text{ years!}} \]

The Reality: In experiments, the emission is instantaneous (\( \lt 10^{-9} \text{ s} \)). The wave model isn't just slightly off; it is off by a factor of trillions.

Impulsive vs. Non-Impulsive Transfer

The fundamental difference lies in how energy is delivered:

Model Mechanism Analogy
Wave (Soaking) Non-Impulsive: Energy spreads over the surface. A gentle rain filling up a bucket.
Particle (Quantum) Impulsive: Energy is delivered in a single "gulp." A heavy brick falling into a bucket.
