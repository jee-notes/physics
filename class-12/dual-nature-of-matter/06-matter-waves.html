Matter Waves

Louis de Broglie's bold hypothesis suggesting particles exhibit wave properties.

de Broglie Hypothesis

In 1924, a French physics student named Louis de Broglie proposed a revolutionary idea based on symmetry. He noted that nature loves symmetry. If radiation (waves) possesses a particle nature under certain conditions, then perhaps matter (particles) should possess a wave nature under certain conditions.

He hypothesized that a moving material particle—like an electron, proton, or even a baseball—has a wave associated with it. These are called matter waves or de Broglie waves.

Wavelength of Material Particles

de Broglie proposed that the wavelength \( \lambda \) associated with a particle of mass \( m \) moving with velocity \( v \) (and thus momentum \( p = mv \)) is given by the same relationship that applies to photons.

de Broglie Wavelength (\( \lambda \))

\[ \lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{mv} \]

Where \( h \) is Planck's constant.

This brilliantly simple equation connects a pure wave property (\( \lambda \)) with pure particle properties (\( m \) and \( v \)).

Because \( h \) is extremely small (\( 10^{-34} \)), the de Broglie wavelength of macroscopic objects is unobservably tiny. However, for microscopic subatomic particles with very small mass, the wavelength falls into a measurable range.

The de Broglie Wavelength of an Electron

Consider an electron of mass \( m_e \) and charge \( e \) accelerated from rest through a potential difference \( V \). The kinetic energy \( K \) gained by the electron is \( K = eV \).

Since kinetic energy and momentum are related by \( K = \frac{p^2}{2m} \), we have \( p = \sqrt{2mK} = \sqrt{2m_eeV} \). Substituting this into de Broglie's equation:

\[ \lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2m_eeV}} \]

Putting in the standard values for \( h, m_e \), and \( e \), we get a simplified formula strictly for accelerated electrons:

\[ \lambda = \frac{1.227}{\sqrt{V}}\text{ nm} \]

Comparison of Wave and Particle Models

Matter waves differ fundamentally from classical waves:

  • They are not electromagnetic waves. They can travel through a vacuum without any electric or magnetic fields.
  • They are not mechanical waves (like sound), as they do not require a material medium.
  • They represent probability waves; the amplitude of the matter wave at a point relates to the probability of finding the particle at that exact location.

This concept birthed modern Quantum Mechanics, replacing definitive classical orbits with probability clouds governed by the wave equations of Schrödinger.

Experimental Arrangement for Electron Diffraction

In 1927, C.J. Davisson and L.H. Germer provided the first direct experimental proof of de Broglie's hypothesis. If electrons act as waves, they must exhibit defining wave behaviors, primarily diffraction and interference.

Their apparatus consisted of:

  • An electron gun containing a heated tungsten filament to emit electrons via thermionic emission.
  • An accelerating anode to form the electrons into a fine, energetic beam of known kinetic energy.
  • A single crystal of Nickel, which acted as a three-dimensional diffraction grating.
  • A movable Faraday cylinder detector placed on a circular scale to measure the intensity of electrons scattered at different angles.

Measurement of Scattered Beam Intensity

The electron beam was directed perpendicularly onto the surface of the Nickel crystal. The electrons scatter off the regular atomic planes of the crystal lattice.

Davisson and Germer moved the detector around the circular scale, measuring the intensity of the scattered electron beam at various scattering angles \( \theta \) for different accelerating voltages ranging from 44V to 68V.

Observations and de Broglie Verification

They observed a distinct pattern: the intensity of the scattered beam was not uniform. It showed sharp peaks and variations. The most prominent peak in intensity was observed at an accelerating voltage of \( 54\text{ V} \) and a scattering angle of \( \theta = 50^\circ \).

This sharp peak is identical to the diffraction pattern produced by X-rays interacting with a crystal. It occurs specifically due to the constructive interference of electron waves scattered from different atomic planes in the Nickel crystal.

Using Bragg's Law of X-ray diffraction applied to the Nickel lattice geometry at the peak angle, the experimental wavelength of the diffracting matter was calculated to be \( 0.165\text{ nm} \).

Concurrently, calculating the theoretical de Broglie wavelength for an electron accelerated through 54V gives:

\[ \lambda = \frac{1.227}{\sqrt{54}}\text{ nm} \approx 0.167\text{ nm} \]

Conclusion for de Broglie Hypothesis

The fantastic agreement between the theoretical de Broglie wavelength (\( 0.167\text{ nm} \)) and the experimentally measured diffraction wavelength (\( 0.165\text{ nm} \)) unequivocally proved the wave nature of electrons.

This experiment validated de Broglie's relation \( \lambda = h/p \) and cemented the concept of wave-particle duality as a fundamental truth of nature.
