Einstein's Photon Theory

The birth of photon and its energy-momentum dynamics.

Concept

In 1905, Albert Einstein proposed a radical theory to explain the photoelectric effect. This phenomenon is fundamentally about the interaction between light (photo) and electrons (electric).

The classical wave model describes light as carrying energy continuously through space. When this continuous wave meets matter, energy is transferred from the electromagnetic wave to the material based on its macroscopic properties: absorptivity, reflectivity, and transmissivity.

Naturally, physicists expected this microscopic interaction to behave similarly to the continuous, macroscopic energy exchanges observed in phenomena like thermal radiation.

To quantify the energy exchange between light and receiving matter, we measure the average intensity of the light received at the location of the receiver. This macroscopic measurement of intensity is used whether we are applying the wave model or the particle model.

However, according to classical wave theory, the average intensity of an electromagnetic wave depends on its amplitude, and not on its frequency. This is precisely where the wave model fails to explain the photoelectric effect.

The experimental observation of a threshold frequency—a minimum frequency required for an electron to overcome the surface barrier, completely independent of the light's intensity—is a clear indication that the energy transferred to an individual electron depends strictly on the frequency of the light.

Einstein recognized that this required a fundamentally different understanding of how light exchanges energy with matter. He built upon Max Planck's earlier hypothesis that electromagnetic energy is emitted or absorbed by matter in discrete amounts.

Einstein argued that while we cannot deny light propagates through space as a wave, experimental observations show that it exchanges energy and momentum with sub-atomic particles as if it consists of individual, localized particles. Energy is not transferred to an electron continuously and arbitrarily; instead, it is exchanged in discrete packets.

The Quantum Hypothesis

At the microscopic level, our understanding of physical phenomena often begins with hypotheses. Avogadro's hypothesis, Einstein's light-quantum hypothesis and de Broglie's matter-wave hypothesis are examples. As experimental evidence accumulates, these hypotheses contribute to the development of established scientific theories.

Einstein proposed that, although light exhibits wave-like behaviour during propagation, its interaction with individual electrons cannot be described as a continuous transfer of energy. Instead, light transfers energy in discrete, localized packets.

According to this hypothesis, a beam of light consists of discrete bundles or packets of energy. These packets were later named photons.

The energy \(E\) carried by a single photon is determined by the frequency \(\nu\) of light:

\[ \boxed{E=h\nu} \]

Here, \(h\) is Planck's constant:

\[ h=6.626\times10^{-34}\,\text{J}\cdot\text{s} \]

An important feature of this relationship is that photon energy depends on frequency. When light travels from one medium to another, its speed and wavelength change, but its frequency remains unchanged. Consequently, the energy of the photon also remains unchanged during refraction.

We know that the speed, frequency and wavelength of a wave are related by:

\[ v=\nu\lambda \]

For light travelling in vacuum, its speed is \(c\), and its wavelength is denoted by \(\lambda_0\). Therefore, we can also express the energy of a photon as:

\[ \boxed{E=h\nu=\frac{hc}{\lambda_0}} \]

Here, \(\lambda_0\) is the wavelength of light in vacuum. Inside a medium, the wavelength changes, but the photon energy remains the same.

We will not discuss photon momentum here, as it is not required for the photoelectric-effect portion of the syllabus.

Einstein's Photoelectric Equation

Einstein viewed the photoelectric effect as a collision event between a single incoming photon and a single electron in the metal.

Unlike classical mechanical collisions, the energy exchange here does not depend on the elasticity or deformation of the colliding particles. This is a quantum collision. During this event, the photon is completely absorbed by the electron and ceases to exist.

Since a photon is a quantum of energy with zero rest mass, being completely absorbed means it transfers its entire energy \( h\nu \) to the electron instantaneously, without altering the electron's rest mass.

The Core Quantum Postulate:
An electron can only absorb an integer number of photons (typically just one), never a fraction of a photon.
Absorbing a photon means absorbing its entire energy payload all at once.

Once an electron below the metal surface acquires this energy, it becomes excited. This newly acquired energy can be utilized in the following ways:

  1. The excited electron may collide with other electrons and the lattice, dissipating its acquired energy as heat within the bulk metal body.
  2. The electron may attempt to overcome the surface barrier of the metal, which requires a minimum amount of energy equal to the work function \(\Phi\).
  3. If the electron escapes the metal, any remaining energy manifests as the kinetic energy (\(K\)) of the ejected photoelectron.

Einstein's Photoelectric Equation

Einstein proposed that such one-to-one photon-electron interactions occur in massive numbers across the illuminated surface.

To build a mathematical equation, we focus on the "luckiest" electron—one that loses no energy to internal collisions and only needs to cross the minimum surface barrier equal to the work function \(\Phi\) of the metal.

By the law of conservation of energy, this specific electron will emerge with the maximum kinetic energy (\(K_{\text{max}}\)) possible outside the metal:

\[ h\nu = \Phi + K_{\text{max}} \]

We can express the work function in terms of the threshold frequency (\(\nu_0\)) or threshold wavelength (\(\lambda_0\)) of the metal: \( \Phi = h\nu_0 = \frac{hc}{\lambda_0} \). Substituting this, we get:

\[ K_{\text{max}} = \frac{hc}{\lambda} - \frac{hc}{\lambda_0} = h(\nu - \nu_0) \]

Alternatively, since the maximum kinetic energy is experimentally measured using the stopping potential \(V_0\) (where \( K_{\text{max}} = eV_0 \)), the core equation is frequently written as:

\[ eV_0 = h\nu - \Phi \]

Explaining Experimental Observations

Einstein's photon theory successfully explained all the observations of the photoelectric experiment that the classical wave model could not.

  1. The Threshold Frequency: The equation shows \(K_{\text{max}} = h(\nu - \nu_0)\). Since kinetic energy cannot be negative, no emission can occur if \(\nu \lt \nu_0\), perfectly explaining the threshold.
  2. Maximum Kinetic Energy depends on Frequency: The equation explicitly shows \(K_{\text{max}}\) is a linear function of frequency \(\nu\), independent of intensity.
  3. Intensity means More Photons: Higher intensity simply means more photons per second. Since each photon causes one emission, photocurrent increases with intensity, but the energy of individual photons (and thus electrons) remains unchanged.
  4. Instantaneous Emission: A photon is a localized particle. The collision and energy transfer in the metal occur on a particle-to-particle basis, taking almost zero time.

However, the proposal itself was extraordinarily radical. The idea that light could also have a particulate nature challenged the established understanding of light as a continuous electromagnetic wave.

At the time, even the atomic theory of matter was still being debated. Although several physicists supported the idea that matter consisted of discrete atoms, others questioned their physical reality.

Against this background, Einstein proposed something even more remarkable: perhaps light, too, has a particulate nature!

Millikan's Verification and h/e Measurement

Robert Millikan was a meticulous experimental physicist, in contrast to Einstein's role as a theoretical physicist. In fact, Millikan spent over a decade trying to "disprove" Einstein's equation, as the particle theory of light seemed too radical to accept.

He carried out painstaking experiments with high-precision measurements of stopping potentials across various frequencies of light.

From Einstein's equation \( V_0 = \left(\frac{h}{e}\right)\nu - \left(\frac{\Phi}{e}\right) \), Millikan plotted \( V_0 \) against \( \nu \). To his surprise, he found exactly the straight line predicted.

By calculating the slope of the experimental graph, which theoretically must equal \( \frac{h}{e} \), and knowing the elementary charge \( e \), he calculated a value for Planck's constant \( h \).

His experimental value matched Planck's theoretical value precisely, inadvertently providing the most conclusive proof for Einstein's theory. Both Einstein and Millikan received Nobel Prizes for this work.

A question that often remains is: why is \( KE_{\text{max}} \) calculated assuming the electron absorbs only 1 photon?
If an electron absorbs 2 photons before coming out of the surface, then \( KE_{\text{max}} = 2h\nu - \Phi \).
Multi-photon photoelectric emission is indeed observed experimentally, but only under extreme intensity conditions (like modern lasers) which are beyond the scope of the exam.
For exam purposes, it is always assumed that light intensity is normal, meaning one-to-one photon-electron interactions dominate.

Intensity

The photon model describes energy interactions in terms of individual photons. However, the geometrical distribution of incident radiation remains the same as in the wave model. The difference is that we can now relate the incident power to the number of photons arriving at a receiving location.

The intensity of incident light is still defined as the power received per unit area. Its geometrical dependence is the same in both the wave and photon descriptions.

Geometry

For a point source of total power \(P_s\), assuming uniform radiation in all directions, the intensity at a point at a distance \(r\) is:

\[ I=\frac{P_s}{4\pi r^2} \]

For a uniform line source of total power \(P_s\) and length \(l\), the intensity at a point at a perpendicular distance \(r\) is:

\[ I=\frac{P_s}{2\pi rl} \]

For an ideal parallel beam, intensity does not depend on distance.

Photon Emission and Reception

This is where the photon description becomes useful. For monochromatic light, each photon carries an energy:

\[ E_{\text{photon}}=h\nu=\frac{hc}{\lambda} \]

If a source emits light with power \(P_s\), the number of photons emitted per second is:

\[ P_s=\dot N_s\frac{hc}{\lambda} \] \[ \boxed{\dot N_s=\frac{P_s\lambda}{hc}} \]

Here, \(\dot N_s\) represents the photon emission rate at the source. It depends on the source power and the energy of each photon, but not on the geometry or distance of the receiver.

As light propagates, its geometrical spreading determines how many photons reach a particular receiving area. If \(P_r\) is the power incident on a receiver, the number of photons arriving per second is:

\[ P_r=\dot N_r\frac{hc}{\lambda} \] \[ \boxed{\dot N_r=\frac{P_r\lambda}{hc}} \]

Therefore, the ratio of photon emission rate at the source to photon arrival rate at the receiver is equal to the ratio of their respective powers:

\[ \boxed{\frac{\dot N_s}{\dot N_r}=\frac{P_s}{P_r}} \]

The power received at a location depends on the intensity and the receiving area. Thus, the number of photons arriving per second also depends on the geometry of propagation.

Photon Flux

Photon flux \(\sigma_r\) is defined as the number of photons crossing unit area perpendicularly per second at the receiving location.

\[ \sigma_r=\frac{d^2N}{dA\,dt} \]

For a receiver of area \(A\) placed perpendicular to the incident beam:

\[ \sigma_r=\frac{\dot N_r}{A} \]

Since the intensity is the incident power per unit area:

\[ I=\frac{P_r}{A} \]

We obtain:

\[ \boxed{\sigma_r=\frac{I\lambda}{hc}=\frac{I}{h\nu}} \]

The geometrical dependence of photon flux follows the same pattern as intensity:

  • For a point source, \(\sigma_r\propto 1/r^2\).
  • For a line source, \(\sigma_r\propto 1/r\).
  • For an ideal parallel beam, photon flux does not depend on distance.

Photon Density

Photon density \(\rho_r\) is defined as the number of photons present per unit volume:

\[ \rho_r=\frac{dN}{dV} \]

For a beam of light propagating in vacuum at speed \(c\), consider the volume swept by the beam through an area \(dA\) in a time interval \(dt\):

\[ dV=c\,dt\,dA \]

The number of photons crossing this area during the same interval is:

\[ dN=\sigma_r\,dA\,dt \]

Therefore:

\[ \rho_r=\frac{dN}{dV}=\frac{\sigma_r\,dA\,dt}{c\,dt\,dA} \] \[ \boxed{\rho_r=\frac{\sigma_r}{c}} \]

Intensity and Photon Flux in Photoelectric Experiments

There is an important terminology issue in the standard discussion of photoelectric experiments. Some textbook treatments use the term intensity to refer to the number of photons incident per unit area per second. Strictly speaking, this quantity is photon flux, not the conventional intensity defined as power received per unit area.

Therefore, when such a treatment says that frequency is changed while keeping intensity constant, it means that the photon flux is maintained constant, rather than the incident power per unit area.

The distinction becomes clear from the relationship:

\[ \boxed{I=\sigma_r h\nu} \]

If photon flux is maintained constant while frequency is changed, the actual incident intensity changes with frequency:

\[ \boxed{I\propto\nu\qquad (\sigma_r=\text{constant})} \]

On the other hand, if the conventional physical intensity is maintained constant, photon flux changes inversely with frequency:

\[ \boxed{\sigma_r\propto\frac{1}{\nu}\qquad (I=\text{constant})} \]

This distinction is important when interpreting experimental observations involving changes in the frequency and intensity of incident light.
