Einstein's Photoelectric Theory

The birth of the photon concept and the algebraic description of light-matter interaction.

The Quantum Hypothesis

In 1905, Albert Einstein proposed a radical theory to explain the photoelectric effect, fundamentally breaking from classical wave theory. He built upon Max Planck's earlier idea that electromagnetic energy is emitted or absorbed in discrete amounts.

Einstein postulated that light itself is not a continuous wave spreading out through space. Instead, a beam of light consists of discrete, localized bundles or packets of energy. These packets were later named "photons". The energy \( E \) of a single photon is strictly determined by its frequency \( \nu \), given by the equation:

\[ E = h\nu \]

where \( h \) is Planck's constant (\( 6.6 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s} \)).

Einstein's Photoelectric Equation

Einstein viewed the photoelectric effect as an elastic collision between a single incoming photon and a single electron in the metal. The photon is completely absorbed, transferring its entire energy \( h\nu \) to the electron instantaneously.

This is the main postulate on which the theory is based.
An electron can only absorb integer multiples of photon and hence energy, and not a fraction of it.
Absorbing photon means absorbing its entire energy.

This energy is used by the electron in two parts:

  1. To overcome the surface barrier of the metal, which requires an amount of energy equal to the work function \( \Phi \).
  2. Any remaining energy becomes the maximum kinetic energy \( K_{\text{max}} \) of the escaping electron.

Einstein's Photoelectric Equation

According to the law of conservation of energy:

\[ h\nu = \Phi + K_{\text{max}} \]

Alternatively, since \( K_{\text{max}} = eV_0 \) (in electron Volt) and the work function \( \Phi = hc/\lambda_0 \), this can be written as:

\[ K_{\text{max}} = hc/\lambda - hc/\lambda_0 = h(\nu - \nu_0) \]

\[ eV_0 = h\nu - \Phi \]

Explaining Experimental Observations

Einstein's brilliantly simple equation resolves every classical failure:

1. The Threshold Frequency: The equation shows \( K_{\text{max}} = h(\nu - \nu_0) \). Since kinetic energy cannot be negative, no emission can occur if \( \nu \lt \nu_0 \), perfectly explaining the threshold.

2. Maximum Kinetic Energy depends on Frequency: The equation explicitly shows \( K_{\text{max}} \) is a linear function of frequency \( \nu \), independent of intensity.

3. Intensity means More Photons: Higher intensity simply means more photons per second. Since each photon causes one emission, photocurrent increases with intensity, but the energy of individual photons (and thus electrons) remains unchanged.

4. Instantaneous Emission: A photon is a localized particle. The collision and energy transfer in the metal occur on a particle-to-particle basis, taking almost zero time.

Millikan's Verification and h/e Measurement

Robert Millikan, an American experimentalist, spent over a decade trying to *disprove* Einstein's equation, as the particle theory of light seemed too radical. He carried out painstaking experiments with high-precision measurements of stopping potentials.

From Einstein's equation \( V_0 = \left(\frac{h}{e}\right)\nu - \left(\frac{\Phi}{e}\right) \), Millikan plotted \( V_0 \) against \( \nu \). He found exactly the straight line predicted. By calculating the slope of the experimental graph, which theoretically must equal \( \frac{h}{e} \), and knowing the elementary charge \( e \), he calculated a value for Planck's constant \( h \). His experimental value matched Planck's theoretical value precisely, inadvertently providing the most conclusive proof for Einstein's theory. Both Einstein and Millikan received Nobel Prizes for this work.

Last question that still remains to be answered is why \( KE_{\text{max}} \) is such that the electron absorbs only 1 photon?
If an electron absorbs 2 photons before coming out of the surface, then \( KE_{\text{max}} = 2h\nu - \Phi \)
Multi-Photon photo-electric emission is observed experimentally under extreme intensity laser conditions and beyond scope of exam.

Intensity

For a point source of power P, intensity at a point at a distance r is given by:

\[ I = \frac{P}{4 \pi r^2} \]

For a line source of power P and length l, intensity at a point at a distance r is given by:

\[ I = \frac{P}{2 \pi r l} \]

For a parallel beam, intensity does not depend on distance.

Photon

For a point source of power P, no. of photon emitted per second:

\[ P = Nhc/\lambda \Rightarrow N = P \lambda / hc \]

Photon flux \(\Phi_p\) is defined as no. of photons crossing unit area perpendicularly per second:

\[ \Phi_p = dN/dA = N / 4 \pi r^2 = \frac{I \lambda}{hc} \]

For a point source, \(\Phi_p \propto 1/r^2\) , For a line source, \(\Phi_p \propto 1/r\)

Photon density \(\rho_p\) is defined as no. of photons crossing unit volume per second:

\[ \rho_p = dN/dV = dN/c \cdot dA = \frac{\Phi_p}{c} \]
