Dual Nature of Radiation and Matter

Both light and matter exhibit wave-particle duality.

Conceptual Overview

Science, and particularly Physics, developed rapidly during and after the Newtonian era. The development of experimental methods and mathematical descriptions opened new avenues for understanding the physical world.

Discovery of the electron opened the gateway to particle physics. Since then, our understanding of the fundamental constituents of matter has advanced enormously. The Standard Model of elementary particles was developed, and research in this field continues even today.

Our understanding of the constituents of matter has reached a point where we can think of something that would have seemed almost unimaginable in earlier times: converting one element into another by understanding how matter is constructed from elementary particles. Gold and Iron are made from the same elementary particles, but differ in their configuration. If we could understand how to deconstruct matter and reconstruct it into a different elemental configuration, we could convert Iron into Gold. We would be playing God!

With so much advancement in the understanding of matter, physicists also began investigating the fundamental nature of light.

During the early development of Physics, light was considered to be composed of particles. Newton advocated this corpuscular model of light. However, around 1665, observations began to reveal phenomena that could not be explained satisfactorily using this model alone.

In 1873 Maxwell predicted electromagnetic waves and in 1887, Hertz conclusively demonstrated that light is an electromagnetic wave.

Wave-Particle Difference

To understand the difficulty in describing light, we first need to understand what distinguishes a wave from a particle.

A useful way to explore this distinction is to consider what happens when two entities meet. By a meeting, we mean that they occupy the same location in space at the same instant in time.

When two waves meet, they occupy the same region simultaneously to produce interference. They do not terminate at the meeting and continue to propagate in their respective directions after interfering.

When two particles meet, they undergo a collision. A collision involves a discrete exchange of energy and momentum.

This distinct behaviour during a meeting interaction is fundamentally what separates their nature.

When Wave Meets a Particle

Light (an EM wave) meeting matter is a common phenomenon. Thermal radiation is one such common experience. In fact, our sense of seeing is connected to this very event. We see an object because light interacts with it and then reaches our eyes.

But can we see an individual electron simply by improving the resolution of a microscope? Is the difficulty merely that an electron is extremely small, or is there something more fundamental about how light and an electron meet?

Wave–Particle Duality

When scientists began experimenting with the interaction of light and electrons, the results revealed an important feature that could not be adequately explained by the wave model alone. The interaction of light with an electron is fundamentally different from how light, as an EM wave, interacts with macroscopic matter in phenomena like thermal radiation and vision.

Light exchanges energy and momentum with particles such as electrons, and these exchanges occur in discrete interactions, just like in a collision. In this respect, the interaction resembles a particle–particle encounter: energy and momentum are transferred through discrete events.

At the same time, light also exhibits wave-like behaviour. Phenomena such as interference and diffraction demonstrate that the continuous wave description remains essential.

We therefore cannot describe all the observed behaviour of light using only the classical wave picture or only the classical particle picture. Light exhibits both wave-like and particle-like behaviour. This is referred to as wave–particle duality.

It is not that the wave model and particle model are mutually exclusive, exhaustive categories and light must strictly conform to only one.

Imagine there is a dog who is red on one side and green on the other.
Initially, you only see the red side and assume that the dog is entirely red. Then one day, you manage to reach the other side and observe that the dog is green!
It does not mean that the dog cannot be red and green—it is both.
It does not mean that all dogs must be exclusively red or exclusively green.

This dual nature is simply based on what is understood so far. Tomorrow, if a phenomenon is found that can be explained by neither model, then we will say that light displays a triple nature.

The historic development of formulating a model of light is reflected in standard textbooks by the ordering of chapters:

  • Reflection, Refraction and Dispersion: These macroscopic optical phenomena are explained using the ray model. The ray model describes the macroscopic propagation of light and so it is independent of the underlying wave-particle discussion.
  • Interference, Diffraction and Polarisation: These phenomena can be explained using the wave model of light.
  • Photoelectric Effect and Compton Effect: These phenomena can only be explained using the particle description of light.

Photoelectric Effect Experiment

The photoelectric effect is not observed in ordinary day-to-day life. However, photoelectric processes are widely used in light-sensing equipment.

The Photoelectric Effect Experiment studied in this chapter explores the phenomenon in which electrons are emitted from a material surface when light falls on it. Observing and measuring this process requires a specially designed experimental setup.

This is something what an actual scientist does. Scientific investigation often begins with observing a phenomenon and predicts an underlying model that could explain it. The scientist then deduces the implications and designs experiments to test those predictions.

Such experiments require carefully controlled conditions and suitable measuring instruments. The objective is not merely to observe a phenomenon, but to investigate its underlying physical mechanism through precise measurements.

Before studying the photoelectric effect experiment, it is useful to understand the broader phenomenon of Electron Emission from a metal surface.

To remove an electron from a metal surface, we must provide a minimum energy known as the Work Function (\(\Phi\)).

Electron emission can be achieved through different mechanisms:

  • Thermionic Emission: Energy is supplied by heating the metal.
  • Field Emission: A sufficiently strong electric field enables the electrons to escape by tunnelling through the surface potential barrier.
  • Photoelectric Emission: Energy is supplied through the absorption of incident light.

We then investigate photoelectric emission specifically: how does the emission of electrons depend on the frequency and intensity of incident light?

Experiment

The photoelectric effect experiment involves directing light onto a photosensitive metal surface and measuring the resulting electron emission and electric current.

By varying the frequency and intensity of incident light and applying suitable electric potentials, we can study the characteristics of the emitted electrons.

The experiment revealed several observations that cannot be explained using classical wave theory alone. The classical wave model suggests continuous energy transfer and therefore predicts different behaviour, particularly regarding the threshold frequency, time delay and kinetic energy of emitted electrons.

Einstein's Photon Theory resolved these contradictions by proposing that light exchanges energy with matter through discrete quanta called photons.

The photon theory was further supported by the Compton Effect (Compton scattering, beyond syllabus), which demonstrated the importance of photon momentum in explaining the scattering of light by electrons.

Photon Theory

Einstein's photon theory describes the particulate aspect of light in its energy and momentum interactions with matter. By particulate, we mean that light exchanges energy and momentum through discrete quantum units.

These discrete units are called photons. Each photon carries a definite amount of energy and momentum:

\[ E = h\nu \quad \text{and} \quad p = \frac{h}{\lambda} = \frac{E}{c} \]
  • The Quantum Shift: Light-matter interaction is not simply a continuous "soaking" of energy. Energy is exchanged through discrete photon quanta. An individual photon is absorbed or emitted as a whole, rather than transferring an arbitrary fraction of its energy.
  • Massless Nature: A photon has zero rest mass. It cannot exist at rest and always travels at the speed of light (\(c\)) in vacuum.
  • Energy and Momentum: A photon's energy is not mechanical energy in the classical \(\frac{1}{2}mv^2\) sense. It carries electromagnetic energy and linear momentum and can exert a physical force through momentum transfer.

The implication of this theory is that energy and momentum interactions between light and matter can be modelled using perfectly elastic collision-like interactions.

These interactions are governed by the conservation of energy and momentum. However, photon-matter interactions are not similar to classical collisions in all aspects. Collision involves quantum processes such as absorption, emission and scattering.

When light interacts with matter, photons may be absorbed, emitted or scattered. Photons are quantum entities and are absorbed or emitted in integer numbers. Energy and momentum are exchanged in these processes.

Photon as a Quantum Particle
Light-matter interactions can be analysed using the conservation principles used in classical collision problems. However, there are important differences.
In a classical collision between two billiard balls, the number of balls remains conserved.
In photon-matter interactions:
  1. Total energy and momentum of the interacting system are conserved.
  2. The number of photons is NOT necessarily conserved. A photon can be absorbed, or new photons can be created through emission.

The mechanical effects of photon momentum, such as radiation pressure, are observable experimentally. Even though photon energy is not classical mechanical energy, its momentum can be transferred to matter and produce a mechanical effect.

When photons strike a surface, they transfer momentum to it. A large number of such interactions produces radiation pressure.
This pressure pushes dust particles away from the Sun and contributes to the formation of a comet's dust tail.

Wave-Particle Duality

If radiation, traditionally considered a wave, could exhibit particle-like behaviour, a natural symmetry question arises: could matter, traditionally considered as particles, exhibit wave-like behaviour?

In 1924, Louis de Broglie proposed exactly this, suggesting that material particles such as electrons also possess a wave nature. This idea extended the description of light to matter and brought both under a common framework.

The underlying reasoning was based on symmetry: if light (wave) can behave like a particle, then matter (particle) should also behave like a wave.

This leads to the de Broglie relation:

\[ \lambda_{d} = \frac{h}{p} \]

This relation was experimentally observed in Davisson and Germer experiment.

The "dual nature" does not mean that radiation or matter behaves exactly like a classical wave and a classical particle at the same time.
Rather, it means that classical models ("wave" or "particle") are limited descriptions, each valid only in certain situations.
The complete behaviour is beyond either model alone.
This is similar to the analogy of blind men describing an elephant—each description is partially correct, but none captures the full reality.
