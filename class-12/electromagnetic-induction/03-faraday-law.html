Faraday's Law

The laws that determine when an EMF is induced and the direction in which it acts.

Faraday's Law of Electromagnetic Induction

In 1831, Michael Faraday discovered that an electromotive force (EMF) is induced in a conducting loop whenever the magnetic flux through the loop changes with time. This discovery revealed a deep connection between electricity and magnetism: a changing magnetic field can produce an electric field.

The magnitude of the induced EMF depends only on how rapidly the magnetic flux through the loop changes. Mathematically,

\[ |\mathcal{E}| = \frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \]

If the circuit consists of a coil with \(N\) turns, each turn links the same magnetic flux \( \Phi_B \). The total flux linkage therefore becomes \(N\Phi_B\). Taking this into account, Faraday's law can be written as

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{E} = -N\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}} \]

The negative sign in the equation expresses Lenz's Law, which determines the direction of the induced EMF (and therefore the direction of the induced current).

Faraday's Law is concerned only with the rate of change of magnetic flux. It does not matter how the flux changes — whether the magnetic field changes, the area of the loop changes, or the orientation of the loop changes. The induced EMF depends only on how fast the flux through the circuit changes with time.

Lenz's Law

Lenz's Law specifies the direction of the induced current. It states:

The induced current flows in such a direction that the magnetic field produced by it opposes the change in magnetic flux that produced it.

In other words, the induced current always acts in a way that resists the change that created it. If the magnetic flux through the loop increases, the induced current produces a magnetic field that attempts to decrease it. If the flux decreases, the induced current produces a magnetic field that attempts to increase it.

A deeper understanding of Lenz's Law requires analysing the physical cause of induction and the interaction between magnetic fields and currents. That discussion is taken up later. For now, it is sufficient to note that the negative sign in Faraday's law represents Lenz's Law:

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{E} = -N\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}} \]

Thus Faraday's law determines the magnitude of the induced EMF, while Lenz's law determines its direction.

Three Ways to Change Flux

Magnetic flux through a loop is given by

\[ \Phi_B = BA\cos\theta \]

where \(B\) is the magnetic field strength, \(A\) is the area of the loop, and \(\theta\) is the angle between the magnetic field and the area vector of the loop.

Since flux depends on these three quantities, there are exactly three possible ways in which magnetic flux can change. Any of these changes will produce an induced EMF according to Faraday's law.

1. Changing \( B \) (Transformer EMF)

If the magnetic field \(B\) passing through the loop changes with time while the loop itself remains stationary and its orientation is fixed, the magnetic flux changes accordingly. In this case,

\[ \mathcal{E} = -NA\cos\theta \cdot \frac{dB}{dt} \]

This mechanism occurs when the magnetic field itself varies with time. A typical example is a loop placed inside a solenoid whose current is changing with time. The changing current produces a changing magnetic field, which induces an EMF in the loop. This principle forms the basis of transformers.

2. Changing \( A \) (Motional EMF)

If a conductor moves in a magnetic field in such a way that the area enclosed by the circuit changes while the magnetic field remains steady and the orientation of the loop does not change, the magnetic flux changes because the area changes.

\[ \mathcal{E} = -NB\cos\theta \cdot \frac{dA}{dt} \]

A common example is a conducting rod sliding along rails in a uniform magnetic field. As the rod moves, the area of the circuit increases or decreases, producing an induced EMF. This type of induction is called motional EMF.

3. Changing \( \theta \) (Generator EMF)

Flux can also change if the orientation of the loop relative to the magnetic field changes. If the loop rotates in a uniform magnetic field, the angle between the magnetic field and the loop's area vector varies with time.

\[ \theta=\omega t \] \[ \Phi_B=BA\cos(\omega t) \] \[ \mathcal{E}=-N\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}=NBA\omega\sin(\omega t)=\mathcal{E}_0\sin(\omega t) \]

This is the operating principle of an AC generator. The maximum value of the induced EMF is

\[ \mathcal{E}_0 = NBA\omega \]
All three cases — transformer EMF, motional EMF and generator EMF — are governed by the same fundamental law \( \mathcal{E} = -N\,d\Phi_B/dt \). The physical mechanism producing the change in flux may differ, but the mathematical law describing electromagnetic induction remains universal.

Core Topics

The classification above provides a useful framework for analysing electromagnetic induction problems. In practice, most applications of Faraday's Law can be understood in terms of these three mechanisms.

Later we will see that motional EMF can also be explained using the Lorentz force acting on charges moving through a magnetic field. This provides an alternative physical explanation of the same phenomenon.

  • Transformer EMF — Occurs when the magnetic field through a stationary loop changes with time.
  • Motional EMF — Occurs when a conductor or loop moves through a magnetic field while the field itself remains static.
  • Generator EMF — Occurs when the loop rotates in a magnetic field, causing the angle between the field and the loop's area vector to change.
