Faraday's Law

The law that determine when and how EMF is induced.

Concept

Faraday's Law does much more than relate induced EMF to the rate of change of magnetic flux. It establishes the complete framework for understanding electromagnetic induction:

  • Flux — Introduces the physical quantity used to describe the magnetic field linked with a circuit.
  • Induction — Establishes the process through which a changing magnetic flux produces an EMF.
  • EMF — Quantifies the magnitude of the induced EMF.

Electromagnetic induction is also the physical principle behind wireless transfer of electrical power, where energy is transferred between circuits without direct electrical contact.

We have already studied how to calculate magnetic flux through an arbitrary imaginary surface in a region where a magnetic field exists. We have also understood magnetic field as flux density, which represents the local magnetic field contribution per unit area.

However, while applying Faraday's Law, we are no longer considering an arbitrary surface. We consider a surface bounded by a closed conducting loop.

This distinction is important because EMF is evaluated around the closed loop, whereas magnetic flux is evaluated through the surface bounded by that loop. Thus, we need to establish a geometrical connection between the surface integral and the circulation around its boundary.

For this, the orientation of the area vector must be chosen consistently with the positive direction of circulation around the loop. We use the right-hand rule: curl the fingers in the chosen direction of circulation, and the thumb gives the positive direction of the area vector.

Faraday's Law

In 1831, Michael Faraday discovered that an EMF is induced in a conducting loop whenever the magnetic flux through the surface bounded by the loop changes with time. This phenomenon is called electromagnetic induction.

This discovery revealed a fundamental connection between electricity and magnetism: a changing magnetic field can produce an electric field.

For a single-turn conducting loop, the magnitude of the induced EMF depends on how rapidly the magnetic flux through the loop changes:

\[ |\mathcal{E}| = \left|\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}\right| \]

If the circuit consists of a coil with \(N\) turns, each turn linking the same magnetic flux \(\Phi\), the total flux linkage with the coil is:

\[ \lambda=N\Phi \]

Taking the flux linkage into account, Faraday's Law for the coil becomes:

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{E}=-\frac{d\lambda}{dt}=-N\frac{d\Phi}{dt}} \]

The negative sign expresses Lenz's Law. It determines the polarity of the induced EMF and, consequently, the direction of the induced current. We will study its physical significance separately.

Faraday's Law relates the induced EMF to the rate of change of magnetic flux linkage.
It does not matter how the flux changes.
The physical mechanism producing the change may differ, but the same fundamental law governs the induction.
For a plane loop in a uniform magnetic field: \[ \Phi=BA\cos\theta \] We can therefore identify three ways of changing the magnetic flux:
  • The magnetic field strength \(B\) may change.
  • The area \(A\) enclosed by the loop may change.
  • The orientation of the loop relative to the magnetic field (\(\theta\)) may change.
These are three different ways of producing a change in magnetic flux, but they all lead to the same Faraday's Law.

Average vs Instantaneous

Just like velocity in kinematics, induced EMF can be studied over a finite time interval (average) or at a particular instant (instantaneous).

  • Average EMF: Evaluated over a finite time interval \(\Delta t\). \[ \mathcal{E}_{\text{avg}}=-N\frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}=-N\frac{\Phi_{\text{final}}-\Phi_{\text{initial}}}{t_{\text{final}}-t_{\text{initial}}} \]
  • Instantaneous EMF: Evaluated at a particular instant using the derivative. \[ \mathcal{E}_{\text{inst}}=-N\frac{d\Phi}{dt} \]

Important Deduction: Induced Charge

An induced EMF produces current in a closed conducting circuit. For a circuit of total resistance \(R\), Ohm's law gives:

\[ I=\frac{\mathcal{E}}{R} \]

Since electric current is the rate of flow of charge:

\[ I=\frac{dq}{dt} \]

Combining these relationships with Faraday's law:

\[ \frac{dq}{dt}=-\frac{N}{R}\frac{d\Phi}{dt} \]

Rearranging and integrating over the complete time interval:

\[ \int_{q_i}^{q_f}dq=-\frac{N}{R}\int_{\Phi_i}^{\Phi_f}d\Phi \]

Therefore:

\[ \Delta q=-\frac{N}{R}(\Phi_f-\Phi_i) \]

Taking the magnitude, the total charge transferred for a monotonic change in flux is:

\[ \boxed{|\Delta q|=\frac{N}{R}|\Delta\Phi|} \]

This gives us an interesting result: the total charge transferred depends only on the change in magnetic flux and the resistance of the circuit. It does not depend on the time taken for the change.

Does the time taken to move the magnet matter?
Suppose we pull a magnet out of a coil, producing the same initial and final magnetic flux in two different situations.
In the first case, we take 1 second, while in the second, we take 10 seconds.
The faster motion produces a larger induced EMF and hence a larger current.
The slower motion produces a smaller EMF and current.
However, the duration of current flow changes accordingly.
Consequently, the total charge transferred is the same in both cases.

Three Ways to Change Flux

Magnetic flux through a loop is given by

\[ \Phi = BA\cos\theta \]

where \(B\) is the magnetic field strength, \(A\) is the area of the loop, and \(\theta\) is the angle between the magnetic field and the area vector of the loop.

Since flux depends on these three quantities, there are exactly three possible ways in which magnetic flux can change. Any of these changes will produce an induced EMF according to Faraday's law.

1. Changing \( B \) (Transformer EMF)

If the magnetic field \(B\) passing through the loop changes with time while the loop itself remains stationary and its orientation is fixed, the magnetic flux changes accordingly. In this case,

\[ \mathcal{E} = -NA\cos\theta \cdot \frac{dB}{dt} \]

This mechanism occurs when the magnetic field itself varies with time. A typical example is a loop placed inside a solenoid whose current is changing with time. The changing current produces a changing magnetic field, which induces an EMF in the loop. This principle forms the basis of transformers.

2. Changing \( A \) (Motional EMF)

If a conductor moves in a magnetic field in such a way that the area enclosed by the circuit changes while the magnetic field remains steady and the orientation of the loop does not change, the magnetic flux changes because the area changes.

\[ \mathcal{E} = -NB\cos\theta \cdot \frac{dA}{dt} \]

A common example is a conducting rod sliding along rails in a uniform magnetic field. As the rod moves, the area of the circuit increases or decreases, producing an induced EMF. This type of induction is called motional EMF.

3. Changing \( \theta \) (Generator EMF)

Flux can also change if the orientation of the loop relative to the magnetic field changes. If the loop rotates in a uniform magnetic field, the angle between the magnetic field and the loop's area vector varies with time.

\[ \theta=\omega t \] \[ \Phi_B=BA\cos(\omega t) \] \[ \mathcal{E}=-N\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}=NBA\omega\sin(\omega t)=\mathcal{E}_0\sin(\omega t) \]

This is the operating principle of an AC generator. The maximum value of the induced EMF is

\[ \mathcal{E}_0 = NBA\omega \]
All three cases — transformer EMF, motional EMF and generator EMF — are governed by the same fundamental law \( \mathcal{E} = -N\,d\Phi_B/dt \). The physical mechanism producing the change in flux may differ, but the mathematical law describing electromagnetic induction remains universal.

Role of Induced Fields

Consider a permanent magnet moving towards a ring placed in its vicinity. As the magnet approaches, its magnetic field at the location of the ring changes with time. This changing magnetic field produces an electric field in the surrounding space.

Case 1: Insulating (Plastic) Ring

Suppose the ring is made of an ideal insulating and non-magnetic material such as plastic.

The changing magnetic field still produces an electric field. However, the material does not have free mobile charge carriers that can sustain current in the ring.

Consequently, no induced current is produced and there is no induced magnetic field due to the circulating current in the ring. The magnet therefore does not experience any electromagnetic braking and continues to move with same speed.

Case 2: Conducting Loop

Now replace the plastic ring with a conducting loop. The changing magnetic field again produces an electric field. This induced electric field acts on the mobile charges in the conductor and drives a current in the closed circuit.

The induced current produces its own magnetic field. This magnetic field repels the original magnet thus opposing its approach. Consequently, the magnet experiences a retarding magnetic force and loses kinetic energy.

Thus, the conducting loop develops its own magnetic response because of the induced current.

Roles of the Fields

It is important to distinguish the original magnetic field from the fields produced during induction.

  • Magnetic field of the permanent magnet: Its change with time produces an electric field in the surrounding space.
  • Induced electric field: It acts on mobile charges in the conducting loop and drives the induced current.
  • Magnetic field of the induced current: It interacts with the permanent magnet and produces the magnetic force opposing its motion.

The induced electric field does electrical work on the charges in the circuit. The magnetic interaction between the induced current and the permanent magnet produces negative mechanical work on the moving magnet.

The loss of mechanical energy of the magnet is transferred to the electrical circuit and ultimately dissipated as heat in its resistance.

Connection with Electrostatic Induction

This phenomenon has a broad conceptual similarity with electrostatic induction.

When a neutral conducting body is brought near a charged body, the external electric field redistributes the mobile charges in the conductor. This redistribution produces its own electric field, resulting in an electrostatic interaction between the two bodies.

Similarly, in electromagnetic induction, a changing magnetic field produces an electric field that drives current in a conducting loop. The induced current produces its own magnetic field, which interacts with the original magnet.

Thus, just as an external electric field can produce an induced charge distribution and its associated electric field, a changing magnetic field can produce an induced current and its associated magnetic field.
