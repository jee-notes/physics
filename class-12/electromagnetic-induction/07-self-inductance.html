Self Inductance

A circuit element's opposition to its own changing current — the inertia of electromagnetism.

Concept

It almost feels like a new chapter starts with this topic.

So far, we have mainly looked at electromagnetic induction from the mechanical side — how moving a magnet or a conductor can produce electrical energy, with the magnetic field doing zero work between the mechanical and electrical parts of the system.

Now, we will shift focus towards the electrical source of induction. We will study what happens when an electrical source establishes a current in a circuit and that current changes with time. The changing current produces a changing magnetic field, which changes the magnetic flux linked with the same circuit and induces an EMF in it.

We will study electromagnetic induction in which electrical energy is supplied to the circuit, and understand how the induced EMF affects the current and how energy is exchanged between the circuit and the magnetic field.

This does not mean that mechanical energy cannot be involved in these processes. It simply means that our focus here is no longer on the mechanical arrangement producing induction, but on the electrical behaviour and energy dynamics of the circuit.

A similar change in perspective occurs when we study capacitance. Initially, we may consider how mechanical work, as in a Van de Graaff generator, is used to move positive charge against the electric field and build up a potential difference.

But mechanical work is not the only way to charge a capacitor. A battery can also supply the energy needed to move charge and establish the potential difference.

When we study capacitors in DC circuits, we focus on their electrical behaviour and the energy stored in their electric field. We are no longer concerned with whether the energy used to charge the capacitor came from mechanical work or from a battery.

Similarly, we will now study inductors as circuit elements and understand their behaviour and energy dynamics in DC circuits — particularly how electrical energy supplied to the circuit gets stored in the magnetic field.

Self Inductance

So far, we studied about the process of induction in an external magnetic field. But now we'll study about induction in an internal magnetic field.

It is just talked about in such a way to unnecessarily make it sound confusing. We already know that internal or external depends on what we consider as a system.

We establish a current in a closed conducting loop, and so it produces a magnetic field around it.

The magnetic field lines due to this field also pass through the area enclosed by the loop. Therefore, a magnetic flux gets associated with the loop which is due to the current passing in the loop itself.

If the current in the loop changes with time, the magnetic field also changes, and hence the magnetic flux linked with the loop changes.

According to Faraday’s Law, a change in magnetic flux induces an emf in the loop.

This induced emf is produced in the same loop in which the current is changing. This phenomenon is called self induction.

Inductance

We studied Self Inductance as a process but Self Inductance is also a physical property of a conducting loop.

The magnetic field produced by a current-carrying conductor is proportional to the current in the loop:

\[ B(t) \propto i(t) \]

Magnetic flux is given by:

\[ \Phi(t) = \int \vec{B}(t) \cdot d\vec{A} \]

Since \( B(t) \propto i(t) \), the flux through the loop is also proportional to current:

\[ \Phi(t) \propto i(t) \]

To convert this proportionality into an equation, we introduce a constant:

\[ \Phi(t) = L i(t) \]

This constant \(L\) depends only on the geometry of the loop (shape, size, number of turns, etc.) and its material properties. It is similar to capacitance \(C = \frac{A\epsilon}{d}\) or resistance \(R = \rho\frac{l}{A}\)

This constant is called the self inductance of the loop.

Induced EMF

From Faraday’s Law:

\[ \mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt} \]

Substituting \( \Phi = LI \):

\[ \mathcal{E} = -\frac{d}{dt}(Li) \]

If \(L\) is constant:

\[ \mathcal{E} = -L\frac{di}{dt} \]

This is the expression for self-induced EMF.

Chain of Cause → Effect
Current → Magnetic Field → Magnetic Flux
Changing Flux → Induced EMF
Each step is proportional to the previous one, which is why: \[ \Phi \propto i \quad \text{and} \quad \mathcal{E} \propto \frac{di}{dt} \]
An inductor is the electromagnetic analogue of inertia.
• Mass resists change in velocity
• Inductance resists change in current
The larger the inductance, the harder it is to change the current.

Definition via Flux Linkage

Coil — A system of current-carrying loops connected in series having same current passing through them.

In case of a solenoid, the current-carrying loops are called as turns and all turns enclose an area individually through which flux gets linked.

Just like we have systems of particles, systems of charges and systems of conductors (capacitor), we can extend this idea to systems of conducting loops and systems of coils and systems of inductors.

A key feature of a system-level description is that it remains valid even for a single element. The same definition applies whether we consider one loop or many turns. This is why the system approach is useful and natural in electromagnetism.

Even a single conducting loop can have non-zero inductance. Multiple turns simply represent an extension of the same idea.

We use a system approach because the physically relevant quantity is the total magnetic flux linked with the entire configuration. This quantity is called flux linkage.

The simplest case is a coil of \(N\) turns such that all turns are connected in series and each turn encloses the same area. In this case, each turn experiences the same magnetic flux \(\Phi\).

Therefore, the total flux linkage of the system is:

\[ \Lambda = N\Phi \]

From earlier, flux through a loop is proportional to the current producing it. Hence, the total flux linkage is also proportional to current:

\[ \Lambda \propto I \]

Introducing the constant of proportionality:

\[ \Lambda = LI \] \[ L = \frac{\Lambda}{I} = \frac{N\Phi_B}{I} \]

Thus, inductance measures how effectively a current produces magnetic flux linkage.

SI unit: Henry (H)

\[ 1 \text{ H} = 1 \text{ Wb/A} = 1 \text{ V·s/A} \]

Self Inductance of a Solenoid

For a solenoid of \(N\) turns, length \(l\), and cross-sectional area \(A\):

Magnetic field inside:

\[ B = \mu_0 n I \quad \text{where } n = \frac{N}{l} \]

Flux through one turn:

\[ \Phi = BA = \mu_0 n I A \]

Total flux linkage:

\[ \Lambda = N\Phi = \mu_0 n^2 l A \cdot I \] \[ \boxed{L = \mu_0 \frac{N^2 A}{l}} \]

With a core of relative permeability \( \mu_r \):

\[ L = \mu_r \mu_0 \frac{N^2 A}{l} \]
  • \( L \propto N^2 \) — turns are extremely powerful
  • \( L \propto A \) — larger area → more flux
  • \( L \propto \frac{1}{l} \) — compact coils are stronger

Back EMF and Circuit Behavior

The induced EMF always opposes the change in current:

  • If current increases → EMF opposes rise
  • If current decreases → EMF tries to maintain it

This leads to important effects:

  • Current cannot change instantaneously
  • Inductors oppose sudden switching
  • In AC circuits, current lags voltage
An inductor resists change, not current itself. Once steady current is established, it behaves like a wire.

Energy Stored in an Inductor

Work must be done against the back EMF to build current in an inductor. This work is stored as energy in the magnetic field.

\[ U = \frac{1}{2} L I^2 \]

This energy is stored in the magnetic field surrounding the coil.

Where is the energy stored?
Not in the wire — but in the magnetic field in space around it.
Energy density of magnetic field: \[ u = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0} \]
