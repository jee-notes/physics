Eddy Currents and Applications

Induced currents in bulk conductors — a source of loss and a tool for engineering.

What are Eddy Currents?

When a solid conducting object is placed in a changing magnetic field — or moved through a non-uniform field — the induced EMF drives currents through the body of the conductor itself, not just along a defined circuit. These circulating currents, flowing in closed loops within the material, are called eddy currents (also Foucault currents).

Eddy currents are governed by the same Faraday's Law as any induced current. By Lenz's Law, they flow in directions that oppose the change in flux that caused them. This gives rise to a braking effect on moving conductors.

The name "eddy" comes from the whirlpool-like pattern of these currents within the conductor. They were discovered by Léon Foucault in 1851 — which is why they are also called Foucault currents.

Energy Loss Due to Eddy Currents

Like any current flowing through a resistive material, eddy currents dissipate energy as heat (\( P = I^2 R \)). This is called eddy current loss or iron loss — a major concern in transformers, electric motors, and generators.

The power loss due to eddy currents is approximately:

\[ P_{\text{eddy}} \propto B_{\text{max}}^2 f^2 t^2 \]

where \( B_{\text{max}} \) is the peak field, \( f \) is the frequency, and \( t \) is the thickness of the material. The strong dependence on thickness (\( \propto t^2 \)) is the key to reducing eddy current losses.

Minimising Eddy Currents: Lamination

The core of a transformer or motor is made of thin sheets of iron (laminations) separated by insulating varnish or oxide, instead of a solid block. This forces eddy currents to be confined within each thin lamination, drastically reducing the cross-sectional area available for current flow and—since resistance increases as area decreases—greatly raising resistance. The power loss \( P = \mathcal{E}^2/R \) is reduced.

In high-frequency applications, powdered iron cores or ferrite cores are used instead of laminations, as they confine eddy currents to microscopic grains.

Applications of Eddy Currents

1. Electromagnetic Braking

A conducting disc or plate moving through a magnetic field experiences eddy currents that create a braking force opposing its motion (Lenz's Law). This is used in:

  • Braking systems of electric trains and roller coasters (smooth, contactless braking).
  • Speedometers in vehicles — a spinning magnet induces eddy currents in an aluminium disc, pulling it proportionally to the speed.
  • Energy meters (electromechanical type) — a disc rotates due to eddy currents and is braked by a permanent magnet, giving a speed proportional to power.

2. Induction Heating

The heat generated by eddy currents \( (P = I^2R) \) can be used for heating. By driving a high-frequency alternating current through a coil around the conductor, intense eddy currents — and hence intense heat — are generated within the material:

  • Induction furnace: Melts metals without contact or combustion. Used in metallurgy for very pure melting conditions.
  • Induction cooktops: A coil under the ceramic surface induces currents in the iron base of the cookware — the pot heats directly, not the cooktop surface.
  • Induction hardening: Surface layers of steel tools are rapidly heated and quenched for selective hardening.

3. Metal Detection

Metal detectors emit an alternating magnetic field. When a metallic object is nearby, eddy currents are induced in it, which create their own magnetic field. This modified field is detected by the device, revealing the presence and approximate location of the metal.

4. Eddy Current Testing (Non-Destructive Testing)

The pattern of eddy currents induced in a metal component depends on the material's conductivity, permeability, and the presence of cracks or voids. By analysing the signals from eddy current probes, flaws can be detected in aircraft structures, pipelines, and rail tracks without any physical damage to the component.
