Motional EMF

Conductor cuts magnetic field lines like a sword cutting grass.

Concept

Faraday's law that we studied is more of a combined-effect law. It tells us the net EMF produced around a complete circuit.

An analogy is Archimedes' principle of buoyancy. Pressure acts on individual surface elements of a body in contact with a fluid. Archimedes' principle gives us the combined effect of all those pressure forces — the resultant buoyant force.

Similarly, Faraday's law gives us the net EMF around a circuit, just as Archimedes' principle gives us the resultant buoyant force on a body.

But if we want to understand where exactly in the circuit the EMF-producing mechanism operates, we need to examine the mechanisms responsible for induction.

There are two mechanisms that contribute to induced EMF, and Faraday's law unifies their combined effect:

  • Motional EMF (due to motion of a conductor through a magnetic field)
  • Induced Electric Field (produced by a changing magnetic field)

In the motional EMF mechanism, we have a moving conductor arrangement (e.g. a sliding rod), and the motional EMF is generated across the moving rod.

In induction due to a changing magnetic field, an induced electric field circulates in space. When a conducting circuit is present, this field drives charges around the circuit and produces EMF.

Both mechanisms can also contribute in the same arrangement. Faraday's law accounts for their combined result. But now we study them separately to understand how each one produces EMF.

Motional EMF

When a conductor moves through a magnetic field, free charges inside it experience the Lorentz magnetic force:

\[ \vec{F}_m = q(\vec{v} \times \vec{B}) \]

This force pushes the free electrons towards a particular side across the length of the conductor.

As a result, an electric field develops due to charge separation. This electrostatic field opposes further separation of charges.

\[ \vec F_e=q\vec E \]

For an open circuit, charges accumulate until equilibrium is reached. At equilibrium, the electric and magnetic forces balance each other: \(\vec F_e+\vec F_m=0\) .

\[ q\vec E+q(\vec v\times\vec B)=0 \] \[ \boxed{\vec E=-\vec v\times\vec B} \]

Consider a straight conductor of length \(\ell\) along the Y axis (\(\vec\ell=\ell\hat j\)), moving translationally with velocity \(v\) along the X axis (\(\vec v=v\hat i\)), in a region where the magnetic field is directed along the negative Z axis (\(\vec B=-B\hat k\)). The magnetic field is uniform across the length of the conductor.

We first determine the direction of the electric field developed inside the conductor due to charge separation.

\[ \vec E=-\vec v\times\vec B=-vB\hat j \]

The electric field is directed along \(-\hat j\). Thus, the lower end A becomes negative and the upper end B becomes positive. We need to find the potential difference between the two ends.

\[ \mathcal E_{A\rightarrow B}=V_B-V_A \]
There is confusion between potential and velocity as both are represented by letter "V".
We use capital \(V\) for voltage and small \(v\) for velocity.

By definition of potential difference:

\[ V_B-V_A=-\int_A^B\vec E\cdot d\vec\ell =-\int_A^B(-vB\hat j)\cdot(d\ell\hat j) =vB\int_A^B d\ell \]
\[ \boxed{|\mathcal E|=B\ell v} \]

We will study the differential form and then understand that this formula is obtained by summing the magnetic force acting on charges along the length of the rod.

Direction and Polarity of Motional EMF

When a conductor moves through a magnetic field, the magnetic force separates charges across the conductor. One end becomes positively charged and the other negatively charged.

Therefore, determining the direction of motional EMF essentially means determining the polarity of the conductor — which end is at a higher electric potential.

We know that the magnetic force on a positive charge moving along with the conductor is:

\[ \vec F_m=q(\vec v\times\vec B) \]

Thus, the direction of \(\vec v\times\vec B\) tells us the direction in which positive charges are pushed. The end towards which positive charges accumulate becomes the higher-potential end. Electrons, being negatively charged, move in the opposite direction.

1. Right-Hand Rule for Cross Product

We can directly use the right-hand rule for the cross product \(\vec v\times\vec B\):

  • Point the index finger of your right hand along \(\vec v\).
  • Point the middle finger along \(\vec B\), keeping it perpendicular to the index finger.
  • Your thumb then points along \(\vec v\times\vec B\).

The thumb therefore indicates the direction of magnetic force on positive charges. For a straight conductor aligned along this direction, the end towards which the thumb points becomes the higher-potential end.

2. Right-Hand-Palm Rule

Another way to determine the same direction is the right-hand-palm rule.

  • Straighten your right hand.
  • Keep your thumb along the direction of velocity \(\vec v\).
  • Keep your fingers along the direction of magnetic field \(\vec B\).
  • The direction in which your palm faces or pushes gives the direction of magnetic force on positive charges.

Therefore, the end of the conductor towards which the palm pushes becomes the higher-potential end.

3. Fleming's Right-Hand Rule

We can also use Fleming's right-hand rule. Stretch the thumb, forefinger and middle finger of your right hand such that they are mutually perpendicular.

  • Thumb represents the direction of motion of the conductor.
  • Forefinger (index finger) represents the direction of magnetic field.
  • Middle finger represents the direction of induced conventional current.

In a closed circuit, the middle finger indicates the direction of conventional induced current through the moving conductor. For an open conductor, no sustained current flows, but the same direction tells us the direction in which positive charges would be driven and hence identifies the higher-potential end.

All three rules give us the same direction: the direction of \(\vec v\times\vec B\), which is the direction of magnetic force on positive charges.
The higher-potential end is the end towards which positive charges are driven.
Electrons move in the opposite direction.

Differential Form

The expression \(B\ell v\) was obtained for a straight conductor moving in a uniform magnetic field, with the conductor length, velocity and magnetic field mutually perpendicular.

To generalise the expression for arbitrary directions, we consider an infinitesimal conductor element \(d\vec{\ell}\). The element may be oriented in any direction and may experience a different magnetic field or velocity from other parts of the conductor.

The motional EMF contribution across this infinitesimal length is given by the component of the magnetic force per unit charge along the conductor:

\[ d\mathcal{E}=(\vec{v}\times\vec{B})\cdot d\vec{\ell} \]

To find the total motional EMF across a conductor extending from A to B, we add the contributions of all its infinitesimal elements by integrating along its length:

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{E}_{\text{mot}}=\int_A^B(\vec{v}\times\vec{B})\cdot d\vec{\ell}} \]

Here, the velocity and magnetic field are evaluated locally at each conductor element. The integral therefore also accounts for a non-uniform magnetic field, a curved conductor or non-uniform motion.

Geometrical Meaning: Area Swept by the Conductor

There is an interesting geometrical interpretation hidden in the scalar triple product.

Consider an infinitesimal conductor element \(d\vec{\ell}\) moving with velocity \(\vec{v}\). During a small time interval \(dt\), the element moves through a displacement \(\vec{v}\,dt\) and sweeps out an infinitesimal area.

\[ d\vec{A}_{\text{swept}}=d\vec{\ell}\times(\vec{v}\,dt) \]

The magnetic flux through this swept area is:

\[ d\Phi_{\text{swept}}=\vec{B}\cdot d\vec{A}_{\text{swept}} \]

Dividing by \(dt\):

\[ \frac{d\Phi_{\text{swept}}}{dt}=\vec{B}\cdot(d\vec{\ell}\times\vec{v}) \]

Using the scalar triple product:

\[ \boxed{d\mathcal{E}=\frac{d\Phi_{\text{swept}}}{dt}} \]

The orientation of the swept area is chosen consistently with the direction of \(d\vec{\ell}\). This shows that the motional EMF contribution of a conductor element is directly related to the magnetic flux through the area swept by that element per unit time.

The "Sword Cutting Grass" Analogy
Think of the moving conductor element as a sword sweeping through space.
The area swept by the conductor is important because magnetic flux through this area accounts for the motional EMF contribution.
If \(\vec{v}\), \(\vec{B}\) and \(d\vec{\ell}\) are coplanar, then: \[ (\vec{v}\times\vec{B})\cdot d\vec{\ell}=0 \] Geometrically, \(d\vec{\ell}\) and \(\vec{v}\) generate a swept area whose plane contains the magnetic field direction.
Therefore, no magnetic flux passes through this swept area and the motional EMF contribution is zero.
The conductor is moving, but it is not cutting across the magnetic field lines in the relevant geometrical sense.

Shape of Conductor

Does the shape of a conductor affect the motional EMF induced across it?

Consider an open conducting wire having two endpoints A and B. We are considering a conductor, not a closed loop. Suppose the entire conductor moves with the same translational velocity \(\vec v\) through a spatially uniform magnetic field \(\vec B\).

Interestingly, the shape of the conductor does not affect themotional EMF between its endpoints. Whether the conductor is straight or curved, the motional EMF depends only on the relative positions of its two endpoints.

The motional EMF is given by:

\[ \mathcal{E}_{\text{mot}}=\int_A^B(\vec v\times\vec B)\cdot d\vec\ell \]

Since the conductor undergoes translational motion, its velocity is the same at every point. Also, the magnetic field is uniform across the conductor. Therefore, both \(\vec v\) and \(\vec B\) can be taken outside the integral:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{E}_{\text{mot}} &=(\vec v\times\vec B)\cdot \int_A^B d\vec\ell\\ &=(\vec v\times\vec B)\cdot\vec\ell_{\text{eff}} \end{aligned} \]

Here, we define the effective length vector as:

\[ \boxed{\vec\ell_{\text{eff}}=\int_A^B d\vec\ell=\overrightarrow{AB}} \]

Notice that the integration of all the infinitesimal length vectors along the conductor gives the net displacement vector from A to B.

Therefore, the effective length is simply the straight-line vector joining the endpoints of the original conductor, irrespective of how curved the actual conductor is.

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{E}_{\text{mot}}=(\vec v\times\vec B)\cdot\vec\ell_{\text{eff}}} \]
For the same endpoint positions, translational velocity and uniform magnetic field, a straight conductor and a curved conductor have the same motional EMF between their endpoints.
The actual length of the wire may be different, but its effective length vector remains the same.
