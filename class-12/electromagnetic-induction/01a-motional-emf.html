Motional EMF

EMF generated by a conductor moving through a magnetic field.

The Sliding Rod on Rails

Consider a conducting rod of length \( l \) sliding with velocity \( \vec{v} \) along two parallel conducting rails separated by the same length \( l \), all in a uniform magnetic field \( \vec{B} \) directed perpendicular to the plane of the rails (into the page).

As the rod moves, free electrons inside it also move with velocity \( \vec{v} \). A magnetic force acts on each electron:

\[ \vec{F} = q\vec{v} \times \vec{B} \]

This force drives electrons along the rod (say, from top to bottom), creating a charge separation — a potential difference across the rod. This potential difference is the motional EMF:

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{E} = Blv} \]

where \( \theta = 90° \) between \( \vec{v} \) and \( \vec{B} \). More generally:

\[ \mathcal{E} = Blv\sin\theta \]

Derivation via Faraday's Law

The same result follows from Faraday's Law. In time \( dt \), the rod sweeps out an area \( dA = l \cdot v\, dt \). The change in flux through the circuit is:

\[ d\Phi_B = B \cdot dA = Blv\, dt \] \[ \mathcal{E} = \frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} = Blv \]

The agreement confirms that the force on charge carriers and Faraday's Law are two descriptions of the same phenomenon.

Motional EMF arises from the magnetic force on moving charges — it is a non-electrostatic EMF. Yet Faraday's Law, which appears to be about flux change alone, gives the same result. This equivalence is deep: it reflects the relativistic relationship between electric and magnetic fields.

Current and Power

If the rails have total resistance \( R \), the induced current is:

\[ I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R} = \frac{Blv}{R} \]

The power delivered to the circuit (electrical power output) equals:

\[ P_{\text{elec}} = \mathcal{E} I = \frac{B^2 l^2 v^2}{R} \]

The current-carrying rod in the field also experiences a braking force (Lenz's Law):

\[ F_{\text{brake}} = BIl = \frac{B^2 l^2 v}{R} \]

The mechanical power needed to maintain constant velocity equals:

\[ P_{\text{mech}} = F_{\text{brake}} \cdot v = \frac{B^2 l^2 v^2}{R} = P_{\text{elec}} \]

Mechanical power input equals electrical power output — energy is conserved. ✓

Rotating Rod

A conducting rod of length \( l \) rotating about one end with angular velocity \( \omega \) in a uniform field \( B \) (perpendicular to the plane of rotation). Each element \( dr \) at distance \( r \) has velocity \( v = \omega r \), contributing:

\[ d\mathcal{E} = B(\omega r)\, dr \] \[ \mathcal{E} = \int_0^l B\omega r\, dr = \frac{1}{2}B\omega l^2 \]
