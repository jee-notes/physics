Area Vector

Area Vector Direction and Circulation.

Concept

The direction of the area vector of an isolated surface can be chosen arbitrarily. We can choose either of the two possible surface normals as its positive direction.

However, when resolving an area vector into components, those components cannot be assigned independent directions or signs. They must combine to reproduce the actual area vector.

Similarly, when multiple surface patches are joined along their boundaries to form a single connected surface, their area-vector orientations cannot be chosen independently. They must be oriented consistently with the circulation along their common boundaries.

We discuss both situations here.

Resolution of Area Vector

Solved Illustration

A magnetic field \(\vec{B} = (4\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} + 2\hat{k})\; \text{ T}\) passes through a rectangular surface of area \(5\,\text{m}^2\) placed in space as shown in the figure. The normal to the surface makes an angle of \(53^\circ\) with the \(x\)-axis in the \(xz\)-plane. Find the magnetic flux through the surface.

Solution

The magnetic field is uniform and the surface is flat planar. Hence, the magnetic flux through a surface is

\[ \Phi = \vec{B}\cdot\vec{A} \]

The magnitude of the area vector equals the area of the surface:

\[ |\vec{A}| = 5 \text{ m}^2 \]

It is difficult to use the geometric dot product \(\vec{B}\cdot\vec{A} = |\vec{B}|\,|\vec{A}|\,\cos\theta\) as we don't know the angle between vectors. Hence, we use algebraic dot product and focus on writing \(\vec{A} = A_x\,\hat{i}+A_z\,\hat{k}\)

The surface normal lies in the \(xz\)-plane and makes an angle \(53^\circ\) with the +x axis. From the diagram the normal points toward +x and −z.

Therefore the area vector must be written as

\[ \vec A = A(\cos53^\circ \hat i - \sin53^\circ \hat k) \]

Substituting \(A = 5\):

\[ \vec A = 5(\cos53^\circ \hat i - \sin53^\circ \hat k) \] \[ \cos53^\circ = \frac35, \qquad \sin53^\circ = \frac45 \] \[ \vec A = 5\left(\frac35 \hat i - \frac45 \hat k\right) \] \[ \vec A = 3\hat i - 4\hat k \]

Now compute the flux:

\[ \Phi = (4\hat i + 3\hat j + 2\hat k)\cdot(3\hat i - 4\hat k) \] \[ \Phi = 12 - 8 \] \[ \Phi = 4 \] \[ \boxed{\Phi = 4 \text{Wb}} \]
The main idea of this illustration is that the area vector components \(A_x\) and \(A_z\) are not independent.
We cannot write the area vector as: \[ \vec A = 3\hat i + 4\hat k \] This is because, the signs of the area vector components must be determined from the actual direction of the area vector.
In this figure the area vector points toward \(+x\) and \(-z\).
Therefore there is only 1 correct way to write the area vector: \[ \vec A = 3\hat i - 4\hat k \] All components of the area vector should add up to give the area.

Surfaces Joined at the Boundaries

When calculating magnetic flux through an open surface, the choice of the area vector's direction is arbitrary. You can choose either the outward or inward normal. However, if a surface is composed of multiple connected sections, their area vectors are no longer independent.

To understand why, we must look at the circulation around the boundary of the surface. By the Right-Hand Rule, the direction of the area vector is firmly linked to the direction we traverse the boundary (curl of the fingers).

Case 1: Disconnected Surfaces

Imagine two separate, non-overlapping square loops in the \(xy\)-plane.

  • Square 1: vertices at (0,0), (1,0), (1,1), (0,1)
  • Square 2: vertices at (2,0), (3,0), (3,1), (2,1)

Because they do not share a boundary, they are mathematically independent. We can choose the area vector of Square 1 to be either \(+\hat{k}\) or \(-\hat{k}\). We have the exact same two choices for Square 2.

This gives us 4 valid configurations for the area vectors: \((+\hat{k}, +\hat{k})\), \((+\hat{k}, -\hat{k})\), \((-\hat{k}, +\hat{k})\), and \((-\hat{k}, -\hat{k})\).

Case 2: Connected Surfaces

Now, move Square 2 so that it shares a boundary with Square 1.

  • Square 1: vertices at (0,0), (1,0), (1,1), (0,1)
  • Square 2: vertices at (1,0), (2,0), (2,1), (1,1)

They now share the edge from \(x=1, y=0\) to \(x=1, y=1\). To treat these two squares as a single continuous surface, the internal shared boundary must mathematically cancel out (since no actual physical outer boundary exists there in a unified surface).

If we choose the area vector of Square 1 as \(+\hat{k}\), its circulation is Counter-Clockwise (CCW). This means the path goes from \((1,1)\) down to \((1,0)\) along the shared boundary.

For the shared boundary to cancel, the circulation of Square 2 must traverse that same edge in the opposite direction: from \((1,0)\) up to \((1,1)\). This forces the circulation of Square 2 to also be CCW.

Because Square 2 is forced into CCW circulation, its area vector is firmly locked to \(+\hat{k}\).

The Orientability Lock
When surfaces share a boundary, their area vectors are locked together by the circulation on that boundary.
Therefore, for the connected squares, there are only 2 valid configurations: both must be \(+\hat{k}\), or both must be \(-\hat{k}\).
Mixed combinations like \((+\hat{k}, -\hat{k})\) are mathematically invalid because they would represent a surface where the boundary circulations crash into each other along the shared edge, failing to form a continuous surface.
