Mutual Inductance

Energy exchange between coupled systems — the foundation of the transformer.

What is Mutual Induction?

In Self Inductance, a circuit interacts with its own magnetic field. In Mutual Induction, we consider a system of two coils.

When a current \( I_1 \) flows through Coil 1 (the primary), it creates a magnetic field that spreads through space. If Coil 2 (the secondary) is placed nearby, some of those magnetic field lines will pass through it.

If \( I_1 \) changes with time, the magnetic flux linked with Coil 2 (\( \Phi_2 \)) also changes. According to Faraday’s Law, this induces an EMF in Coil 2.

The Coupling Chain
Current in Coil 1 \(\to\) Field in Space \(\to\) Flux through Coil 2
Change in \( I_1 \) \(\to\) Change in \( \Phi_2 \) \(\to\) Induced EMF in Coil 2.

The Coefficient of Mutual Inductance (\( M \))

The magnetic flux linked with Coil 2 is directly proportional to the current in Coil 1 that produced it:

\[ \Phi_2 \propto I_1 \]

Introducing the constant of proportionality, \( M \):

\[ \Phi_2 = M I_1 \]

The induced EMF in the second coil is:

\[ \mathcal{E}_2 = -\frac{d\Phi_2}{dt} = -M \frac{dI_1}{dt} \]

The constant \( M \) is the Mutual Inductance of the pair of coils. Like self-inductance, it is measured in Henrys (H).

The Reciprocity Theorem: Nature's Symmetry

One of the most profound results in electromagnetism is that the "coupling" between two coils is perfectly symmetrical.

If you pass current through Coil 1, the flux in Coil 2 is \( \Phi_2 = M_{21} I_1 \).
If you pass the same current through Coil 2, the flux in Coil 1 is \( \Phi_1 = M_{12} I_2 \).

Mathematically and physically:

\[ M_{12} = M_{21} = M \]
Symmetry of Interaction
The Mutual Inductance \( M \) is a property of the system as a whole. It doesn't belong to Coil 1 or Coil 2; it describes the geometric "handshake" between them.

Factors Affecting \( M \)

While self-inductance depends only on the geometry of one coil, mutual inductance depends on:

  • Individual Geometries: \( N \), \( A \), and \( l \) of both coils.
  • Relative Distance: Closer coils share more field lines.
  • Relative Orientation: Coils must be aligned to maximize flux linkage (perpendicular coils have \( M \approx 0 \)).
  • Medium: A high-permeability core (\( \mu_r \)) "channels" the flux from one coil to the other.

5. Example: Two Coaxial Solenoids

Consider a long solenoid of radius \( r_1 \) and turns per unit length \( n_1 \). A second solenoid of radius \( r_2 \) and turns \( n_2 \) is wound over it.

If we pass current \( I_1 \) through the inner solenoid, the field is \( B_1 = \mu_0 n_1 I_1 \). The flux through each turn of the outer solenoid is \( \Phi = B_1 A_{inner} \).

Total flux linkage in the outer solenoid:

\[ \Lambda_2 = N_2 ( \mu_0 n_1 I_1 \pi r_1^2 ) \]

Since \( \Lambda_2 = M I_1 \):

\[ \boxed{M = \mu_0 n_1 n_2 l \pi r_1^2} \]
Note that \( M \) depends on the area of the inner solenoid, as that is where the magnetic field is confined.

Coupling Coefficient (\( k \))

In an ideal world, all flux from Coil 1 would pass through Coil 2. In reality, there is "leakage." We define the coupling coefficient \( k \):

\[ M = k \sqrt{L_1 L_2} \]
  • \( k = 1 \): Perfect coupling (all flux shared).
  • \( k = 0 \): No coupling (coils are far apart or perpendicular).

Connection to Bond Graph Theory

In our Effort–Flow framework, Mutual Inductance represents a multi-port storage element.

Energy is stored in the combined magnetic field of both coils. The power transfer between the coils occurs because the Flow in one system creates an Effort in the other.

\[ \text{Energy Stored} = \frac{1}{2} L_1 I_1^2 + \frac{1}{2} L_2 I_2^2 \pm M I_1 I_2 \]
The \(\pm M I_1 I_2\) term is the interaction energy. It tells us that the total energy of the system depends on whether the currents are "helping" or "opposing" each other's fields.
