Mutual Inductance

How a changing current in one circuit induces EMF in another — the transformer principle.

Definition

When two coils are placed near each other, a changing current in coil 1 produces a changing magnetic field which links (partly or fully) with coil 2, inducing an EMF in it. This interaction is characterised by the mutual inductance \( M \).

The flux linkage in coil 2 due to current \( I_1 \) in coil 1 is proportional to \( I_1 \):

\[ N_2 \Phi_{21} = M I_1 \]

The induced EMF in coil 2:

\[ \mathcal{E}_2 = -M\frac{dI_1}{dt} \]

Similarly, current \( I_2 \) in coil 2 induces EMF in coil 1:

\[ \mathcal{E}_1 = -M\frac{dI_2}{dt} \]

The mutual inductance \( M \) is the same in both directions — this is the reciprocity theorem: \( M_{12} = M_{21} = M \). The SI unit is the Henry (H).

The reciprocity of mutual inductance (\( M_{12} = M_{21} \)) is a non-trivial result from energy considerations. It means that the same \( M \) describes the coupling regardless of which coil drives and which receives.

Mutual Inductance of Two Coaxial Solenoids

Consider two coaxial solenoids — solenoid 1 of \( N_1 \) turns, length \( l \), area \( A \), and solenoid 2 of \( N_2 \) turns wound over it. Current \( I_1 \) in solenoid 1 creates:

\[ B_1 = \mu_0 n_1 I_1 = \mu_0 \frac{N_1}{l} I_1 \]

Total flux linkage through solenoid 2 (all \( N_2 \) turns link the same flux \( B_1 A \)):

\[ N_2 \Phi_{21} = N_2 B_1 A = N_2 \mu_0 \frac{N_1}{l} A \cdot I_1 \] \[ \boxed{M = \frac{\mu_0 N_1 N_2 A}{l}} \]

Note: \( M = \sqrt{L_1 L_2} \) only when flux linkage is perfect (coupling coefficient \( k = 1 \)). In general:

\[ M = k\sqrt{L_1 L_2}, \quad 0 \leq k \leq 1 \]

The Transformer

A transformer uses mutual inductance to transfer AC power between two circuits at different voltage levels. An ideal transformer (100% coupling, no losses) satisfies:

\[ \frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p} \]

By energy conservation (power input = power output for an ideal transformer):

\[ V_p I_p = V_s I_s \implies \frac{I_s}{I_p} = \frac{N_p}{N_s} \]
  • Step-up transformer: \( N_s > N_p \) — voltage increases, current decreases.
  • Step-down transformer: \( N_s < N_p \) — voltage decreases, current increases.
Real transformers have losses due to eddy currents in the core (minimised by lamination), hysteresis in the core, and resistance of windings. Efficiencies of 95–99% are typical for power transformers.

Neumann Formula

The most general expression for mutual inductance between two arbitrary loops \( C_1 \) and \( C_2 \) is given by the Neumann formula:

\[ M = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \oint_{C_1} \oint_{C_2} \frac{d\vec{l}_1 \cdot d\vec{l}_2}{|\vec{r}_{12}|} \]

where \( \vec{r}_{12} \) is the vector from element \( d\vec{l}_1 \) to element \( d\vec{l}_2 \). This double line integral makes manifest the geometric nature of \( M \) and the symmetry \( M_{12} = M_{21} \).
