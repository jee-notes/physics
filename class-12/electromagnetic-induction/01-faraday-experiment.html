Faraday's Experiments

The experiments that revealed how and when an EMF is induced.

Faraday's Experiments

Michael Faraday discovered electromagnetic induction through a series of experiments performed in 1831. These experiments revealed a single fundamental fact: an EMF is induced in a circuit whenever the magnetic flux linked with the circuit changes.

Although the experimental setups looked different, they can all be understood today as different ways of changing the magnetic flux through a loop.

Experiment 1 — Changing Magnetic Field

Faraday wound two coils \(P\) and \(S\) on a common iron core. Coil \(P\) was connected to a battery and a key, while coil \(S\) was connected to a galvanometer.

  • When the key was pressed, the galvanometer showed a momentary deflection.
  • When the key was released, a deflection again appeared, but in the opposite direction.
  • When the key remained pressed, no deflection occurred.

Pressing the key causes the current in coil \(P\) to grow, producing a growing magnetic field in the core. This increases the magnetic flux linked with coil \(S\), inducing a current. When the key is released, the magnetic field decreases and the induced current reverses direction.

When the current becomes steady, the magnetic field and flux remain constant, so no EMF is produced.

This type of induction occurs because the magnetic field strength changes with time.

Experiment 2 — Relative Motion of Source and Coil

Faraday also observed that an EMF appears when the source of magnetic field moves relative to a nearby coil.

  • Moving an electromagnet or bar magnet toward a coil produces a galvanometer deflection.
  • Moving it away produces a deflection in the opposite direction.
  • If both remain stationary, no deflection occurs.

As the magnet approaches the coil, the magnetic flux through the coil increases. When it moves away, the flux decreases. The induced current appears only while this change in flux occurs.

Experiment 3 — Motion of the Coil in a Magnetic Field

Faraday also placed a coil connected to a galvanometer inside a region of uniform magnetic field.

  • If the coil moved entirely within the magnetic field, no deflection was observed.
  • If the coil entered or left the magnetic field region, the galvanometer showed a deflection.

When the coil moves so that the portion of the loop inside the magnetic field changes, the magnetic flux linked with the loop changes. This produces an induced current.

The EMF generated in this situation is called motional EMF. It can be explained either using the Lorentz force acting on electrons in the conductor or by applying Faraday's law of induction.

Although these experiments appear different, they all share a common feature: the magnetic flux through the circuit changes. In fact, magnetic flux is given by \[ \Phi_B = BA\cos\theta \] so the flux can change in three ways:
  • the magnetic field \(B\) may change,
  • the area of the loop \(A\) may change,
  • or the orientation of the loop (\(\theta\)) may change.
All practical methods of electromagnetic induction — transformers, motional EMF, and generators — arise from one of these three possibilities.

Field View

Faraday's experiments strongly supported the field view of electromagnetism.

Earlier theories often explained forces through the idea of action at a distance, where objects influenced one another directly across empty space. Faraday's results suggested a different picture: the region of space around electric charges and currents is filled with a field that carries physical influence.

In this view, the electromagnetic field is treated as a real physical entity that exists throughout space. Changes in this field propagate through space and affect nearby circuits, producing phenomena such as electromagnetic induction.

Magnetic flux is therefore defined in terms of the magnetic field.

To understand flux, we must first understand the idea of a field, because flux is defined for a field.

A field is a physical quantity that can be indexed by location (position) in space. For example, when we speak of instantaneous velocity, we mean the velocity associated with a particular instant of time. We can therefore define velocity at a specific time \( t=t_1 \) as \( \vec{v}(t_1) \).

In a similar way, a field assigns a value to every point in space. Thus the magnetic field at position \( \vec{r}=\vec{r}_1 \) can be written as \( \vec{B}(\vec{r}_1) \).

A useful way to view electromagnetic phenomena is through the behaviour of electric charges.
  • Charge at rest → produces only an electric field \( \vec{E} \).
  • Charge moving with constant velocity → produces both an electric field \( \vec{E} \) and a magnetic field \( \vec{B} \).
  • Accelerating charge → produces changing electric and magnetic fields that propagate through space as an electromagnetic wave.
Electrostatics studies charges at rest. Magnetostatics studies charges in steady motion (steady currents). Electromagnetic induction arises when fields change with time, and accelerating charges ultimately give rise to electromagnetic waves.
