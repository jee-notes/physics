Lenz's Law

Direction of induced current determined by conservation of energy.

Concept

The principle of conservation of energy is an inherent characteristic of the physical world. It governs how any event in nature takes place.

Conservation can be understood through the idea of accounting. Consider the water cycle involving rainfall and evaporation. We say that rainfall happens because water on the ground evaporates to form clouds.

Consider the water stored in clouds as an account. Evaporation, which is the cause, acts as a credit transaction because it increases the water stored in the clouds, whereas rainfall, which is the effect, acts as a debit transaction because it decreases the water stored in the clouds.

Both transactions affect the same account, but in opposite directions. Thus, conservation involves opposing transactions on the same account. The cause and effect have opposite transactional natures. It cannot rain more water than what got evaporated.

When this process of cause and effect is viewed as the one in which effect is birthed, the opposing nature of cause and effect, manifests as a resistance to the birth of the effect. When the cause produces the effect, the formation of that effect is accompanied by an opposing response. The effect does not emerge through an unrestricted, one-sided conversion of cause into effect. Else, all of the cause would have already converted to effect and only effect would have existed.

We have already encountered this idea in mechanics through inertia. An applied force attempts to change the state of motion of a body, while inertia manifests as resistance to that change. We also encountered this in capacitance. If we try to store more charge on the capacitor, its potential increases and it gets more and more difficult.

We encounter a similar philosophical requirement in electromagnetic induction. Faraday studied induced EMF and established its magnitude:

\[ |\mathcal{E}| = \left|\frac{d\Phi}{dt}\right| \]

However, determining the magnitude of an induced EMF does not determine its polarity. The polarity describes the nature and direction of the induced response. To understand it, we must consider the underlying principle of energy conservation and the opposing nature of the physical transactions involved.

Lenz's Law

Lenz's Law applies the energy conservation principle to determine the polarity of the emf induced in the coil.

When a magnet is moved toward or away from a conducting loop, the magnetic flux through the loop changes. The induced current produces a magnetic field that opposes this change.

  • The induced current produces a magnetic field that opposes the motion of the magnet.
  • An external force must be applied to keep the magnet moving.
  • The work done by the external agent is converted into electrical energy.

Flux Interpretation

Lenz's Law can also be understood purely in terms of magnetic flux.

  • If the external magnetic flux through the coil increases, the coil produces a magnetic field in the same direction.
  • If the external magnetic flux through the coil decreases, the coil produces a magnetic field in the opposite direction.

Case 1: External Flux Decreasing

If the external magnetic flux through the coil is decreasing, the induced current produces a magnetic field in the same direction as the original field.

This attempts to maintain the original magnetic flux.

Case 2: External Flux Increasing

If the external magnetic flux through the coil is increasing, the induced current produces a magnetic field in the opposite direction to the original field.

This attempts to reduce the increase in flux.

This is often a major source of confusion.
Students assume that the direction of induced magnetic field will always be opposite to that of the external field.
But, it depends on whether the flux due external field is decreasing or increasing.
If flux is increasing, then it will be in opposite direction, but if it is decreasing, then it will be in the same direction.

Example 1: Falling Magnet Through a Coil

A magnet falling through a conducting loop experiences a magnetic force due to the induced current.

  • Before crossing the coil: induced current produces an upward magnetic force.
  • After crossing the coil: induced current again produces an upward magnetic force.

Therefore the acceleration of the magnet becomes less than \(g\).

Example 2: Straight Wire Near a Coil

A current carrying wire produces a magnetic field through a nearby coil.

If the current in the wire increases, the magnetic flux through the coil increases. The induced current in the coil flows such that it opposes this increase.

Example 3: Two Coils Moving Apart

Two current carrying coils are placed near each other.

When the coils are moved away from each other, the magnetic flux linked with each coil decreases.

  • Induced currents appear in both coils.
  • The induced currents act in the same direction as the original currents.
  • This attempts to maintain the original magnetic flux.

Irodov 3.304: Induced Current in Closed Loops

Question

The figure below illustrates plane figures made of thin conductors which are located in a uniform magnetic field directed away from the reader (into the plane of the drawing). The magnetic induction starts diminishing. Find how the currents induced in these loops are directed.

Solution

Since the magnetic field \(\vec{B}\) (into the page) is diminishing, the induced current must create a magnetic field that is also into the page to oppose the change (Lenz's Law). This requires a net Clockwise (CW) circulation of current.

Analysis of individual cases:

Case (a): The system consists of two closed semicircular loops. In both, current is induced CW. Along the shared diameter, these currents oppose and cancel each other.
Result: Current flows Clockwise along the outer boundary.

Case (b): This is a single continuous loop. Following the wire path, if current flows CW in the outer arc, it must flow CCW in the inner arc. The net EMF is \(\mathcal{E}_{net} = \frac{dB}{dt}(A_{outer} - A_{inner})\). Since the outer area dominates, the net direction is dictated by the outer boundary.
Result: Current flows Clockwise in the outer section and Counter-Clockwise in the inner section.

Case (c): We have two independent concentric loops joined by a single radial bridge. While CW currents are induced in both loops, the bridge is a "dangling branch." Since it does not form a closed path between the two loops, no charge can flow through it (KCL).
Result: Current flows Clockwise in both rings; current in the bridge is Zero.

Case (d): This figure-eight is a single loop where the two sections are wound in opposite senses. The larger area \(A_1\) produces a larger EMF than the smaller area \(A_2\).
Result: The larger EMF dominates the circuit. The current flows Clockwise in the larger loop and Counter-Clockwise in the smaller loop.

The "Single Loop" Trick
In Case (b), common mistake is to treat the inner and outer arcs as separate circuits. Because they are part of the same wire, we must trace the path: a CW flow on the outside naturally becomes a CCW flow on the inside. The loop "encloses" the area between the two arcs, and Lenz's law acts on that net area.
KCL and "Dangling Branches"
In Case (c), the radial wire is a single point-to-point connection. For a steady current to exist, there must be a return path. Without one, any flow would lead to charge accumulation, violating the steady-state condition. This subtle application of Kirchhoff's Current Law is often asked in exam to check if students can distinguish between a "connected" system and a "complete" circuit.
