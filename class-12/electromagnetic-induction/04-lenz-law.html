Faraday's Law and Lenz's Law

The two laws that define how and in which direction EMF is induced.

Faraday's Law of Electromagnetic Induction

Michael Faraday discovered in 1831 that an EMF is induced in a conducting loop whenever the magnetic flux through the loop changes. The magnitude of the induced EMF is proportional to the rate of change of flux:

\[ |\mathcal{E}| = \frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \]

For a coil of \( N \) turns, each turn links the same flux \( \Phi_B \), so the total flux linkage is \( N\Phi_B \), and:

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{E} = -N\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}} \]

The negative sign is the mathematical statement of Lenz's Law — it tells us the direction of the induced EMF (and hence the induced current).

Faraday's Law is not about the cause of the flux change — it doesn't matter whether \( B \) changes, the area changes, or the orientation changes. Only the rate of change of flux \( d\Phi_B/dt \) determines the EMF.

Lenz's Law

Lenz's Law states: the induced current flows in a direction such that its magnetic effect opposes the change in flux that caused it.

To apply it:

  • Determine how the flux through the loop is changing (increasing or decreasing).
  • The induced current must create a field that opposes this change.
  • Use the right-hand rule to find the direction of current that produces that field.
Lenz's Law is a consequence of energy conservation. If the induced current reinforced the flux change instead of opposing it, it would create more flux, inducing more current, amplifying itself indefinitely — a perpetual motion machine. Nature forbids this.

Applications of Lenz's Law

Magnet Approaching a Loop

When the north pole of a magnet approaches a conducting loop, the flux through the loop increases (pointing toward the loop). The induced current flows to create a field opposing this increase — i.e., pointing away from the approaching magnet. By the right-hand rule, the induced current flows anticlockwise when viewed from the magnet's side.

This induced current also creates a north pole facing the approaching magnet — a repulsive force. Work must be done to push the magnet in; this work becomes the electrical energy in the induced current.

Magnet Receding from a Loop

As the north pole moves away, flux decreases. The induced current flows to oppose the decrease — it creates a field in the same direction as the original flux, i.e., a north pole on the far side, attracting the magnet. Again, external work must be done to pull the magnet away.

Quantitative Example

A rectangular coil of 200 turns and area \( 0.02 \text{ m}^2 \) is placed in a magnetic field that changes at \( 0.5 \text{ T/s} \). The induced EMF is:

\[ \mathcal{E} = N\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} = N \cdot A \cdot \frac{dB}{dt} = 200 \times 0.02 \times 0.5 = 2\text{ V} \]

Three Ways to Change Flux

Since \( \Phi_B = BA\cos\theta \), there are exactly three ways in which magnetic flux can change — and any of these produces an induced EMF.

1. Changing \( B \) (Transformer EMF)

If the magnetic field \( B \) changes with time while the loop remains stationary and its orientation is fixed, the induced EMF is

\[ \mathcal{E} = -NA\cos\theta \cdot \frac{dB}{dt} \]

Example: a loop placed inside a solenoid whose current is changing with time. This mechanism forms the basis of transformers.

2. Changing \( A \) (Motional EMF)

If a conductor moves in a steady magnetic field in such a way that the area of the circuit changes, the flux changes accordingly:

\[ \mathcal{E} = -NB\cos\theta \cdot \frac{dA}{dt} \]

Example: a sliding conducting rod moving along rails in a uniform magnetic field. This is known as motional EMF.

3. Changing \( \theta \) (Generator EMF)

If the loop rotates in a steady uniform magnetic field, the angle between the field and the loop normal changes with time.

\[ \theta=\omega t \quad \Rightarrow \quad \Phi_B=BA\cos(\omega t) \] \[ \mathcal{E}=-N\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}=NBA\omega\sin(\omega t)=\mathcal{E}_0\sin(\omega t) \]

This is the working principle of an AC generator. The peak value of the induced EMF is \( \mathcal{E}_0 = NBA\omega \).

All three cases — transformer EMF, motional EMF, and generator EMF — are governed by the same fundamental law \( \mathcal{E} = -N\,d\Phi_B/dt \). The physical mechanism producing the change in flux may differ, but the mathematical law describing the induction remains universal.

The Induced Electric Field

When the magnetic field in a region changes with time, it produces an electric field even in empty space — without the presence of any conductor.

The integral form of this relationship is

\[ \oint \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \]

This is the integral form of Maxwell's equation

\[ \nabla \times \vec{E} = -\frac{\partial \vec{B}}{\partial t} \]

For example, consider a circular loop of radius \( r \) inside a solenoid where the magnetic field changes uniformly with time. The induced electric field satisfies

\[ E(2\pi r) = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} = -\pi r^2 \frac{dB}{dt} \] \[ E = -\frac{r}{2}\frac{dB}{dt} \]
