Self Inductance

A circuit element's opposition to its own changing current — the inertia of electromagnetism.

What is Self Inductance?

When a current is established in a closed conducting loop, it produces a magnetic field around it.

This magnetic field passes through the area enclosed by the loop. Therefore, there is a magnetic flux associated with the loop.

If the current in the loop changes with time, the magnetic field also changes, and hence the magnetic flux linked with the loop changes.

According to Faraday’s Law, a change in magnetic flux induces an emf in the loop.

This induced emf is produced in the same loop in which the current is changing. This phenomenon is called self induction.

Why is Flux Proportional to Current?

The magnetic field produced by a current-carrying conductor is proportional to the current in the loop:

\[ B \propto i(t) \]

Magnetic flux is given by:

\[ \Phi_B = \int \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{A} \]

Since \( B \propto i(t) \), the flux through the loop is also proportional to current:

\[ \Phi_B \propto i(t) \]

To convert this proportionality into an equation, we introduce a constant:

\[ \Phi_B = L i(t) \]

This constant \( L \) depends only on the geometry of the loop (shape, size, number of turns, etc.).

This constant is called the self inductance of the loop.

Induced EMF

From Faraday’s Law:

\[ \mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \]

Substituting \( \Phi_B = LI \):

\[ \mathcal{E} = -\frac{d}{dt}(Li) \]

If \( L \) is constant:

\[ \mathcal{E} = -L\frac{di}{dt} \]

This is the expression for self-induced EMF.

Chain of Cause → Effect
Current → Magnetic Field → Magnetic Flux
Changing Flux → Induced EMF
Each step is proportional to the previous one, which is why: \[ \Phi \propto i \quad \text{and} \quad \mathcal{E} \propto \frac{di}{dt} \]
An inductor is the electromagnetic analogue of inertia.
• Mass resists change in velocity
• Inductance resists change in current
The larger the inductance, the harder it is to change the current.

Definition via Flux Linkage

Coil — A current-carrying loop that encloses an area is a coil. Just like we have systems of particles, systems of charges and systems of conductors (capacitor), we can extend this idea to systems of coils and systems of inductors.

A key feature of a system-level description is that it remains valid even for a single element. The same definition applies whether we consider one loop or many turns. This is why the system approach is useful and natural in electromagnetism.

Even a single current-carrying conductor (for example, a finite length \( \Delta l \)) can have non-zero inductance. Multiple turns simply represent an extension of the same idea.

We use a system approach because the physically relevant quantity is the total magnetic flux linked with the entire configuration. This quantity is called flux linkage.

The simplest case is a coil of \( N \) turns such that all turns are connected in series and each turn encloses the same area. In this case, each turn experiences the same magnetic flux \( \Phi_B \).

Therefore, the total flux linkage of the system is:

\[ \Lambda = N\Phi_B \]

From earlier, flux through a loop is proportional to the current producing it. Hence, the total flux linkage is also proportional to current:

\[ \Lambda \propto I \]

Introducing the constant of proportionality:

\[ \Lambda = LI \] \[ L = \frac{\Lambda}{I} = \frac{N\Phi_B}{I} \]

Thus, inductance measures how effectively a current produces magnetic flux linkage.

SI unit: Henry (H)

\[ 1 \text{ H} = 1 \text{ Wb/A} = 1 \text{ V·s/A} \]
System Thinking

For multi-turn coils, flux linkage naturally becomes a system-level quantity.
• Capacitor → inherently a system of conductors
• Inductor → can exist for a single loop, but becomes richer for multiple turns
Flux linkage plays a role analogous to charge in capacitors.

Self Inductance of a Solenoid

For a solenoid of \( N \) turns, length \( l \), and cross-sectional area \( A \):

Magnetic field inside:

\[ B = \mu_0 n I \quad \text{where } n = \frac{N}{l} \]

Flux through one turn:

\[ \Phi_B = BA = \mu_0 n I A \]

Total flux linkage:

\[ \Lambda = N\Phi_B = \mu_0 n^2 l A \cdot I \] \[ \boxed{L = \mu_0 \frac{N^2 A}{l}} \]

With a core of relative permeability \( \mu_r \):

\[ L = \mu_r \mu_0 \frac{N^2 A}{l} \]
  • \( L \propto N^2 \) — turns are extremely powerful
  • \( L \propto A \) — larger area → more flux
  • \( L \propto \frac{1}{l} \) — compact coils are stronger

Back EMF and Circuit Behavior

The induced EMF always opposes the change in current:

  • If current increases → EMF opposes rise
  • If current decreases → EMF tries to maintain it

This leads to important effects:

  • Current cannot change instantaneously
  • Inductors oppose sudden switching
  • In AC circuits, current lags voltage
An inductor resists change, not current itself. Once steady current is established, it behaves like a wire.

Energy Stored in an Inductor

Work must be done against the back EMF to build current in an inductor. This work is stored as energy in the magnetic field.

\[ U = \frac{1}{2} L I^2 \]

This energy is stored in the magnetic field surrounding the coil.

Where is the energy stored?

Not in the wire — but in the magnetic field in space around it.

Energy density of magnetic field: \[ u = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0} \]
