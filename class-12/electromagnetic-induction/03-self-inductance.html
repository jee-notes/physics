Self Inductance

A circuit's opposition to its own changing current — the inertia of electromagnetism.

What is Self Inductance?

When current flows through a coil, it creates a magnetic field and hence magnetic flux through the coil itself. If the current changes, the flux changes, inducing an EMF in the coil itself — opposing the change. This property is called self inductance.

The self-induced EMF is proportional to the rate of change of current:

\[ \mathcal{E}_L = -L\frac{dI}{dt} \]

where \( L \) is the self inductance (or simply inductance) of the coil. The negative sign reflects Lenz's Law — it opposes the change.

An inductor is the electromagnetic analogue of inertia. Just as mass resists changes in velocity, inductance resists changes in current. A large inductor maintains its current even after the driving EMF is removed.

Definition via Flux Linkage

The flux linkage \( \Lambda \) through a coil is proportional to the current producing it:

\[ \Lambda = N\Phi_B = LI \] \[ L = \frac{N\Phi_B}{I} = \frac{\Lambda}{I} \]

The SI unit of inductance is the Henry (H): \( 1\text{ H} = 1\text{ Wb/A} = 1\text{ V·s/A} \).

Self Inductance of a Solenoid

For a solenoid of \( N \) turns, length \( l \), and cross-sectional area \( A \), with \( n = N/l \) turns per unit length, carrying current \( I \):

The field inside is \( B = \mu_0 nI \). The flux through one turn is \( \Phi_B = BA = \mu_0 nIA \). Total flux linkage:

\[ \Lambda = N\Phi_B = (nl)(\mu_0 nIA) = \mu_0 n^2 l A \cdot I \] \[ \boxed{L = \mu_0 n^2 lA = \mu_0 \frac{N^2}{l} A = \frac{\mu_0 N^2 A}{l}} \]

Key observations:

  • \( L \propto N^2 \) — doubling turns quadruples inductance.
  • \( L \propto A \) — larger cross-section means more flux per turn.
  • \( L \propto 1/l \) — shorter solenoid has more turns per unit length for same \( N \).
  • With a core of relative permeability \( \mu_r \): \( L = \mu_r \mu_0 N^2 A / l \).

Back EMF and Its Role in Circuits

When the current through an inductor increases, the self-induced EMF \( \mathcal{E}_L = -L\, dI/dt \) opposes the increase — it acts like a "back EMF" that resists the rise of current. When current decreases, \( \mathcal{E}_L \) opposes the decrease — it tries to maintain the current.

This is why:

  • Inductors cause current to lag behind voltage in AC circuits.
  • When an inductive circuit is suddenly broken, the large \( dI/dt \) produces a very large back EMF — this causes the spark seen when switching off a high-current inductor.
A 1 H inductor carrying 1 A stores 0.5 J of energy. When the circuit is broken, this energy must go somewhere — if the switch creates an air gap, it can ionize the air and produce a visible arc. This is why inductors can be dangerous to switch abruptly.
