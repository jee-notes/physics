Motional EMF

EMF generated by time-varying Area Swept.

The Sliding Rod on Rails

Consider a conducting rod of length \( l' \) sliding with velocity \( \vec{v} \) along two parallel conducting rails separated by distance \( l \). The entire system lies in a uniform magnetic field \( \vec{B} \) directed into the page.

As the rod slides to the right, the rectangular loop formed by the rails and the rod increases in area.

If the rod moves a small distance \( dx \) in time \( dt \), the additional area swept by the rod is

\[ dA = l\,dx \]

To calculate the magnetic flux through the loop, we know \( \vec{B} \) is uniform and no change of orientation in area. So:

\[ \Phi_B = BA \]

Since the area of the loop is changing with time, the magnetic flux through the loop also changes.

The rate of change of flux is therefore

\[ \frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} = B \frac{dA}{dt} \]

But

\[ \frac{dA}{dt} = l \frac{dx}{dt} = lv \]

Hence

\[ \frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} = Blv \]

Applying Faraday's Law,

\[ \mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \]

we obtain the magnitude of the induced EMF:

\[ |\mathcal{E}| = Blv \]
Motional EMF is not a separate law. It is simply a consequence of Faraday's Law. When a conductor moves, the area of the circuit changes, which changes the magnetic flux through the loop. The induced EMF therefore arises because the loop sweeps through magnetic field lines.

Later we will show that the same result \( \mathcal{E} = Blv \) can also be derived from the Lorentz force acting on charges in the moving conductor.

Example: Rotating Conducting Rod

Question

A conducting rod of length \(l\) rotates with constant angular velocity \(\omega\) about one end in a uniform magnetic field \( \vec B \) directed into the page. Find the EMF induced between the ends of the rod.

Solution

In a small time interval \(dt\), the rod rotates through a small angle

\[ d\theta = \omega dt \]

The rod sweeps out a sector of a circle. The small area swept is

\[ dA = \frac{1}{2} l^2 d\theta \]

Substituting \( d\theta = \omega dt \):

\[ dA = \frac{1}{2} l^2 \omega dt \]

The change in magnetic flux is

\[ d\Phi = B\,dA \] \[ d\Phi = B\left(\frac{1}{2}l^2\omega dt\right) \]

Applying Faraday's Law,

\[ \mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt} \]

The magnitude of the induced EMF is

\[ |\mathcal{E}| = \frac{1}{2}B\omega l^2 \]
In motional EMF problems, the key quantity is the area swept by the conductor. The induced EMF follows directly from Faraday's Law: \[ \mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt} = -B\frac{dA}{dt} \] Therefore, instead of analysing complicated loop shapes or circuit connections, it is often easier to calculate the rate at which area is being swept by the moving conductor.
