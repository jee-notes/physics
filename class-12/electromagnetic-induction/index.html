Electromagnetic Induction

A changing magnetic field creates an electric field.

Conceptual Overview

Oersted demonstrated that an electric current produces a magnetic field. This was a pathbreaking discovery because, until then, magnetic fields were primarily associated with magnets. It established an unexpected connection between electricity and magnetism.

The motion of electric charge is associated with a magnetic field. This fact made Faraday wonder whether the reverse was also possible: could a moving magnet produce an electric effect? Faraday spent years searching for the answer.

In 1831, Faraday discovered electromagnetic induction. He found that a changing magnetic flux through a circuit induces an EMF. The key idea is change. A magnetic field, however strong, does not induce an EMF in a circuit if the magnetic flux through it remains constant.

A stationary charge produces an electrostatic field, and a stationary magnet produces a magnetostatic field. However, a stationary source is not a necessity for field production. A moving charge is associated with a magnetic field, and a moving magnet can produce an electric field. The field phenomena we associate with stationary sources can also arise when the sources are in motion.

Faraday went beyond describing the phenomenon merely as the effect of a moving magnet. He expressed it in terms of changing magnetic flux. This was a more general description: an electric field can be induced by a changing magnetic field without requiring a magnetic pole to move physically near the circuit.

This discovery unlocked the modern world of electrical power. Generators, transformers, and induction motors ultimately rely on the principle of electromagnetic induction.

The emphasis on the word "changing" also explains why the earlier chapters were called electrostatics and magnetostatics. Those chapters dealt with fields that do not vary with time.

Electromagnetic induction introduces a new idea: fields that change with time. Such phenomena belong to electrodynamics.

Faraday's Law — The Central Idea

A magnetic field may change with time in space. However, knowing that a magnetic field is changing somewhere in space is not sufficient to determine the induced EMF in a particular circuit. We need a way to establish a geometrical connection between the changing magnetic field and the circuit under consideration.

For this, we introduce the idea of magnetic flux. We consider a surface bounded by the circuit and evaluate the magnetic field passing through that surface. This establishes the geometrical association between the magnetic field and the circuit.

Think of a projector projecting an image onto a screen. The screen provides a definite surface on which the projected image can be captured. Similarly, the surface bounded by a circuit provides a geometrical reference through which we capture the magnetic field associated with that circuit. Magnetic flux quantifies this association.

Faraday's Law states that the induced EMF around a closed loop is equal to the negative rate of change of the magnetic flux through the surface bounded by that loop:

\[ \mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt} \]

The negative sign represents Lenz's Law. It determines the direction of the induced EMF: the magnetic field produced by the induced current opposes the change in magnetic flux responsible for the induction. This opposition is a direct consequence of the principle of energy conservation.

The magnetic flux \(\Phi\) through a surface bounded by a loop is defined as:

\[ \Phi = \int \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{A} \]

For a uniform magnetic field passing through a plane surface, this reduces to:

\[ \Phi = BA\cos\theta \]

Here, \(\theta\) is the angle between the magnetic field and the area vector (normal to the surface).

Magnetic flux can change in three ways: the magnetic field strength \(B\) may change, the area \(A\) of the surface may change, or the orientation \(\theta\) of the surface relative to the magnetic field may change. A change in any of these quantities can produce an induced EMF, provided it results in a net change in magnetic flux.

Self and Mutual Inductance

Any circuit carrying a changing current produces a changing magnetic field around it. This changing field alters the magnetic flux through the circuit itself and therefore induces an EMF in the same circuit.

This self-opposition to changes in current is characterised by the self-inductance \( L \):

\[ \mathcal{E}_{\text{self}} = -L\frac{dI}{dt} \]

When two circuits are placed near each other, a changing current in one circuit produces a changing magnetic flux through the other. As a result, an EMF is induced in the neighbouring circuit. This effect is characterised by the mutual inductance \( M \):

\[ \mathcal{E}_2 = -M\frac{dI_1}{dt} \]

Transformers operate entirely on the principle of mutual inductance, allowing electrical power to be transferred efficiently between circuits operating at different voltage levels.

Energy in the Magnetic Field

An inductor carrying current \( I \) stores energy in the magnetic field surrounding it. The total energy stored in the inductor is

\[ U = \frac{1}{2}LI^2 \]

Just as a capacitor stores energy in the electric field with energy density \( \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E^2 \), an inductor stores energy in the magnetic field. The energy density of the magnetic field is

\[ u_B = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0} \]
