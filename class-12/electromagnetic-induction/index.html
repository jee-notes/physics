Electromagnetic Induction

A changing magnetic field creates an electric field — the principle behind every generator and transformer.

Conceptual Overview

Oersted showed that electricity produces magnetism. Faraday then spent years searching for the reverse — could magnetism produce electricity?

In 1831 he found the answer: a changing magnetic field produces an electric field. The key word is changing. A static magnetic field, no matter how strong, produces no current. Only magnetic flux in motion — or changing — can do so.

This discovery unlocked the entire modern world of electrical power. Every generator, every transformer, and every induction motor ultimately operates on a single principle: Faraday's Law of Electromagnetic Induction.

The emphasis on the word "changing" also explains why the earlier chapters were called electrostatics and magnetostatics. Those chapters dealt with fields that do not vary with time. Electromagnetic induction introduces a new idea — fields that change with time. Such phenomena belong to electrodynamics.

Faraday's Law — The Central Idea

The induced EMF in any closed loop is equal to the negative rate of change of the magnetic flux through the loop:

\[ \mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \]

The negative sign — expressed through Lenz's Law — determines the direction of the induced current. The induced current always opposes the change in magnetic flux that produced it. This opposition is a direct consequence of the principle of energy conservation.

The magnetic flux \( \Phi_B \) through a surface bounded by a loop is defined as:

\[ \Phi_B = \int \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{A} = BA\cos\theta \]

Magnetic flux can change in three ways: the magnetic field \( B \) may change, the area \( A \) of the loop may change, or the angle \( \theta \) between the field and the surface may change. Any of these changes produces an induced EMF.

Self and Mutual Inductance

Any circuit carrying a changing current produces a changing magnetic field around it. This changing field alters the magnetic flux through the circuit itself and therefore induces an EMF in the same circuit.

This self-opposition to changes in current is characterised by the self-inductance \( L \):

\[ \mathcal{E}_{\text{self}} = -L\frac{dI}{dt} \]

When two circuits are placed near each other, a changing current in one circuit produces a changing magnetic flux through the other. As a result, an EMF is induced in the neighbouring circuit. This effect is characterised by the mutual inductance \( M \):

\[ \mathcal{E}_2 = -M\frac{dI_1}{dt} \]

Transformers operate entirely on the principle of mutual inductance, allowing electrical power to be transferred efficiently between circuits operating at different voltage levels.

Energy in the Magnetic Field

An inductor carrying current \( I \) stores energy in the magnetic field surrounding it. The total energy stored in the inductor is

\[ U = \frac{1}{2}LI^2 \]

Just as a capacitor stores energy in the electric field with energy density \( \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E^2 \), an inductor stores energy in the magnetic field. The energy density of the magnetic field is

\[ u_B = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0} \]
