Magnetic Flux and Induced EMF

What exactly is flux — and the three ways it can change to produce an EMF.

Magnetic Flux

The magnetic flux \( \Phi_B \) through a surface \( S \) is a measure of how many magnetic field lines pass through that surface. It is defined as:

\[ \Phi_B = \int_S \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{A} \]

For a uniform field \( \vec{B} \) making angle \( \theta \) with the normal \( \hat{n} \) to a flat surface of area \( A \):

\[ \Phi_B = BA\cos\theta \]

The SI unit of magnetic flux is the Weber (Wb): \( 1\text{ Wb} = 1\text{ T·m}^2 \).

  • \( \theta = 0° \): \( \Phi_B = BA \) — maximum, field perpendicular to surface.
  • \( \theta = 90° \): \( \Phi_B = 0 \) — field parallel to surface, no lines pass through.
Flux is a scalar. A positive flux means the predominant direction of \( \vec{B} \) is along the chosen normal to the surface; negative flux means it is opposite. The sign depends on the chosen orientation of the surface normal.

Flux Linkage

For a coil of \( N \) turns, the total flux linkage \( \Lambda \) is the sum of flux through all turns:

\[ \Lambda = N\Phi_B \]

Faraday's Law in terms of flux linkage:

\[ \mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Lambda}{dt} = -N\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \]

Three Ways to Change Flux

Since \( \Phi_B = BA\cos\theta \), there are exactly three ways to change it — and each produces an EMF:

1. Changing \( B \) (Transformer EMF)

If the field \( B \) changes with time while the loop is stationary and its orientation is fixed:

\[ \mathcal{E} = -NA\cos\theta \cdot \frac{dB}{dt} \]

Example: A loop inside a solenoid whose current is changing. Used in transformers.

2. Changing \( A \) (Motional EMF)

If a conductor moves in a steady field such that the area of the circuit changes:

\[ \mathcal{E} = -NB\cos\theta \cdot \frac{dA}{dt} \]

Example: A sliding rod on rails in a uniform field. This is motional EMF.

3. Changing \( \theta \) (Generator EMF)

If the loop rotates in a steady uniform field:

\[ \theta = \omega t \implies \Phi_B = BA\cos(\omega t) \] \[ \mathcal{E} = -N\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} = NBA\omega\sin(\omega t) = \mathcal{E}_0\sin(\omega t) \]

This is the principle of the AC generator. The peak EMF is \( \mathcal{E}_0 = NBA\omega \).

All three cases — transformer, motional, and generator EMF — are governed by the single equation \( \mathcal{E} = -N\, d\Phi_B/dt \). The physical mechanism may differ, but the mathematical law is universal.

The Induced Electric Field

When \( B \) changes with time in a region, it creates an electric field even in free space — with no conductor present. The integral form of this relationship is:

\[ \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \]

This is the differential Maxwell equation \( \nabla \times \vec{E} = -\partial\vec{B}/\partial t \) in integral form. For a circular loop of radius \( r \) inside a solenoid where \( B \) is changing uniformly:

\[ E \cdot 2\pi r = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} = -\pi r^2 \frac{dB}{dt} \] \[ E = -\frac{r}{2}\frac{dB}{dt} \]
