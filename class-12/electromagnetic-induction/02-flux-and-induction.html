Magnetic Flux

Calculating Flux is accumulating contributions of field over a surface for a given instant.

Concept

Flux is field linkage with the circuit (bounded area). The definition of infinitesimal flux at a particular location and instant is given by:

\[ d\phi = \vec{B}\cdot d\vec{A} \]

To determine the total flux through a finite surface \( S \) at an instant, we sum the contributions from all such infinitesimal elements over the surface. This leads to the surface integral. This is how we convert changing field to moving flux.

\[ \Phi(t) = \int_S \vec{B}(\vec{r},t)\cdot d\vec{A} \]

Surface integrals are compound integrals involving 2 variables (\(dA = dx\,dy\)) and not in syllabus.

We use geometric tools to solve surface integrals .

Unit and Dimensions of magnetic flux

The SI unit of magnetic flux is the Weber (Wb):

\[ 1\,\text{Wb} = 1\,\text{T·m}^2 \]

Flux is a scalar quantity.

A positive flux means the predominant direction of \(\vec B\) is along the chosen surface normal; a negative flux means it is opposite to it.

The sign therefore depends on the chosen orientation of the surface normal.

Area Vector

Magnetic flux measures how much magnetic field passes through a surface. Since magnetic field is a vector quantity, the amount of field passing through a surface depends not only on the size of the surface but also on its orientation.

To account for orientation, we represent the surface by an area vector.

The magnitude of the area vector equals the area of the surface, and its direction is perpendicular (normal) to the surface.

Using this convention, magnetic flux through a surface is defined as

\[ d\Phi = B\,dA\,\cos \theta \]

The dot product ensures that only the component of the magnetic field perpendicular to the surface contributes to the flux.

We have 3 cases based on orientation, minimum, maximum and zero.

  • If the magnetic field is parallel to the surface, no field lines pass through it and the flux is zero. \(\theta = 90^\circ\)
  • If the field is perpendicular to the surface, the flux is maximum or minimum depending on orientation of surface. \(\theta = 0^\circ\) or \(\theta = 180^\circ\)

Solved Example

Question

A uniform magnetic field exists in a region and is given by

\[ \vec{B} = B_0 \hat{i} \]

A bent rectangular coil has two surfaces:

  • One surface of area \(A_1\) lies in the YZ plane.
  • Another surface of area \(A_2\) lies in the XY plane.

Find the total magnetic flux through the coil.

Solution

Flux through a surface is given by

\[ \Phi = \vec{B}\cdot\vec{A} \]

First determine the area vectors.

  • For a surface in the YZ plane, the normal is along \( \hat{i} \).
  • For a surface in the XY plane, the normal is along \( \hat{k} \).

Thus the total area vector can be written as

\[ \vec{A} = A_1 \hat{i} + A_2 \hat{k} \]

The magnetic field is

\[ \vec{B} = B_0 \hat{i} \]

The flux becomes

\[ \Phi = \vec{B}\cdot\vec{A} \] \[ \Phi = (B_0 \hat{i}) \cdot (A_1\hat{i} + A_2\hat{k}) \] \[ \Phi = B_0A_1 \]

Since \( \hat{i}\cdot\hat{k} = 0 \), the surface in the XY plane contributes no flux.

Only the surface whose normal is parallel to the magnetic field contributes to the magnetic flux.

Magnetic Field Description

A magnetic field exists throughout a region of space. This means that at every point in space and at every instant of time we can assign a value to the magnetic field.

Mathematically, this idea is expressed by writing the magnetic field as a function of position and time:

\[ \vec{B} = \vec{B}(\vec{r},t) \]

Here \( \vec{r} = (x,y,z) \) represents the position in space and \(t\) represents time. This notation means that the value of the magnetic field may change when we move to a different point in space, and it may also change as time passes.

Because the magnetic field can depend on both position and time, we can describe its behaviour using two independent questions:

  • Does the magnetic field change from one place to another?
  • Does the magnetic field change with time?

These two questions lead to four possible types of magnetic fields.

1. Uniform and Static Field

A magnetic field is called uniform if it has the same value everywhere in space. It is called static if it does not change with time.

An example of such a field is

\[ \vec{B} = (2\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} + 4\hat{k}) \]

In this expression the magnetic field does not depend on position \(x,y,z\) and it also does not depend on time \(t\).

Therefore:

  • The field is the same at every point in space.
  • The field remains the same as time passes.

A good physical example is the magnetic field inside a long solenoid carrying steady current.

2. Uniform but Time-Varying Field

Sometimes the magnetic field may remain the same at every point in space but may change with time.

For example:

\[ \vec{B} = (2t^2)\hat{i} \]

In this case the magnetic field depends only on time \(t\).

This means:

  • At any given instant, the field is the same everywhere in space.
  • However, the magnitude of the field changes as time passes.

Such fields are very important in electromagnetic induction, because a magnetic field that changes with time can produce an electric field.

3. Non-Uniform but Static Field

In some situations the magnetic field changes from one place to another but remains constant with time.

For example:

\[ \vec{B} = (2x^2)\hat{i} \]

Here the magnetic field depends on the position coordinate \(x\).

This means:

  • The magnetic field has different values at different points in space.
  • However, once the field is established, it does not change with time.

A familiar example of this type is the magnetic field around a straight current-carrying wire.

4. Non-Uniform and Time-Varying Field

The most general case occurs when the magnetic field changes both with position and with time.

For example:

\[ \vec{B} = (2x^3 t^2)\hat{i} \]

In this situation:

  • The magnetic field has different values at different points in space.
  • The value of the field at a given point also changes with time.

Such fields arise in many electromagnetic phenomena, especially when charges are accelerating or when electromagnetic waves are produced.

Relation with Derivatives

Mathematically, the change of the magnetic field with position or time is described using partial derivatives.

If the field changes with position, then

\[ \frac{\partial \vec{B}}{\partial \vec{r}} \neq 0 \]

and if the field changes with time, then

\[ \frac{\partial \vec{B}}{\partial t} \neq 0 \]

Electromagnetic induction occurs whenever the magnetic field changes with time, that is, when

\[ \frac{\partial \vec{B}}{\partial t} \neq 0 \]

This is called as transformer EMF in time-varying magnetic field (TVMF).

Electromagnetic induction also occurs whenever there is relative motion in a static field.

\[ \frac{\partial \vec{B}}{\partial t} = 0 \qquad \text{relative motion} \neq 0 \]

This is called as motional EMF

Different Variables in the Calculus

Electromagnetic induction often involves two different types of calculus operations: integration over space and differentiation with respect to time. If these variables are not carefully distinguished, it is easy to make mistakes.

Faraday's law relates the induced EMF to the time rate of change of magnetic flux:

\[ \mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt} \]

However, the magnetic flux itself is defined as a surface integral of the magnetic field:

\[ \Phi = \int_S \vec{B}\cdot d\vec{A} \]

The magnetic field may depend on both position and time, and is generally written as

\[ \vec{B} = \vec{B}(\vec{r},t) \]

When calculating the flux, the integration is performed over spatial coordinates. Therefore time \(t\) is treated as a constant parameter during the integration. Only after computing the flux do we differentiate the result with respect to time when applying Faraday's law.

The following example illustrates how this works.

Question

A magnetic field passes through a rectangular surface of sides \(a\) and \(b\) placed in the \(xy\)-plane. Find the magnetic flux through the rectangle for the magnetic field

\[ \vec B = (2t^3 x)\hat{k} \]

Assume the rectangle extends from \(x=0\) to \(x=a\) and from \(y=0\) to \(y=b\).

Solution

Magnetic flux through a surface is defined as

\[ \Phi = \int_S \vec B \cdot d\vec A \]

Since the surface lies in the \(xy\)-plane, the area vector is along \( \hat{k} \).

\[ d\vec A = dA\,\hat{k} \]

The magnetic field is also along \( \hat{k} \):

\[ \vec B = (2t^3 x)\hat{k} \]

Therefore

\[ \vec B \cdot d\vec A = (2t^3 x)\, dA \]

The rectangle has length \(a\) along the \(x\)-direction and height \(b\) along the \(y\)-direction. We divide the area into vertical strips of width \(dx\).

\[ dA = b\,dx \]

Substituting into the flux integral:

\[ \Phi = \int_0^a (2t^3 x)(b\,dx) \]

The integration variable is \(x\), so \(t\) behaves like a constant and can be taken outside the integral.

\[ \Phi = 2bt^3 \int_0^a x\,dx \]

Evaluating the integral:

\[ \int_0^a x\,dx = \frac{a^2}{2} \]

Therefore the magnetic flux is

\[ \Phi = a^2 b t^3 \]
During the surface integration we integrate over spatial coordinates.
The time \(t\) is treated as a constant parameter.
Only after computing the flux do we differentiate with respect to time when applying Faraday's law.
