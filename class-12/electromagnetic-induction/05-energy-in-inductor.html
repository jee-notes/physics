Energy Stored in an Inductor

The work done against back-EMF is stored as magnetic field energy.

Derivation of Stored Energy

When current through an inductor rises from 0 to \( I \), the back-EMF at any instant is \( \mathcal{E}_L = -L\, di/dt \). To maintain the current against this back-EMF, the source must do work. The power delivered when the current is \( i \) and is increasing at \( di/dt \) is:

\[ dW = \mathcal{E}_L \cdot i\, dt = L\frac{di}{dt} \cdot i\, dt = Li\, di \]

Total work done (= energy stored) when current reaches \( I \):

\[ U = \int_0^I Li\, di = \frac{1}{2}LI^2 \] \[ \boxed{U = \frac{1}{2}LI^2} \]
This is the exact magnetic analogue of the energy stored in a capacitor: \( U_C = \frac{1}{2}CV^2 \). The inductance \( L \) plays the role of capacitance, and current \( I \) plays the role of voltage. The stored energy resides not in the coil wire, but in the magnetic field throughout its volume.

Energy Density of the Magnetic Field

For a solenoid of inductance \( L = \mu_0 n^2 lA \) carrying current \( I \), the stored energy is:

\[ U = \frac{1}{2}LI^2 = \frac{1}{2}\mu_0 n^2 lA \cdot I^2 \]

Since \( B = \mu_0 nI \) inside the solenoid, we have \( B^2 = \mu_0^2 n^2 I^2 \), so \( \mu_0 n^2 I^2 = B^2/\mu_0 \). Substituting:

\[ U = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0} \cdot (lA) \]

Since \( lA \) is the volume of the solenoid, the energy stored per unit volume — the magnetic energy density — is:

\[ \boxed{u_B = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}} \]

This result is universal — it holds for any magnetic field configuration, not just solenoids. Compare with the electric energy density \( u_E = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E^2 \).

Growth and Decay of Current in an LR Circuit

When an inductor \( L \) is connected in series with a resistor \( R \) to an EMF source \( \mathcal{E}_0 \), the current builds up exponentially:

\[ I(t) = \frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{R}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right), \quad \tau = \frac{L}{R} \]

When the source is disconnected (short-circuited), the current decays:

\[ I(t) = I_0 e^{-t/\tau} \]

The time constant \( \tau = L/R \) governs how quickly the inductor responds. Larger \( L \) or smaller \( R \) means a slower response — the inductor "remembers" its current for longer.
