Universal structure of energy transfer based on conservation of energy.

Power as Effort × Flow

Physics is often taught in separate sub-branches — Mechanics, Electromagnetism, and Fluid Dynamics — each with its own variables and terminology. However, in the 1950s, Professor Henry Paynter of MIT recognized a deep structural similarity across these domains and developed Bond Graph Theory.

The central idea is that energy transfer between any two systems can be represented as a bond. Each bond carries energy in the form of power, which is always expressed as the product of two complementary variables: effort and flow.

\[ P(t) = e(t)\,f(t) \]
By focusing on energy bonds, we shift attention from the specific physical system to the process of energy transfer. This reveals that different systems can behave identically at a functional level — for example, a capacitor behaves like a spring, and an inductor behaves like a mass.

Across all branches of physics, the rate at which energy is transferred (power) from one system to another can be written in the universal form:

\[ \text{Power} = (\text{Effort}) \times (\text{Flow}) \]

This is not an arbitrary definition, but a recurring structure that emerges whenever energy transfer is analysed in a consistent way.

Here:

  • Effort is the driving variable that "pushes" the transfer. It represents the cause that initiates motion or transport.
  • Flow is the responding variable that represents motion, transport, or the rate at which something changes.

Power exists only when both are present:

  • If effort is present but flow is zero → no energy transfer (no motion).
  • If flow exists without effort → no energy transfer (no driving cause).
  • Only when both coexist → energy is transferred.

Examples Across Physics

  • Mechanics: \( P = F \cdot v \)
    Force (effort) acting on a moving body (flow) transfers mechanical energy.
  • Electric Circuits: \( P = V I \)
    Voltage (effort) drives current (flow), transferring electrical energy.
  • Fluid Flow: \( P = P \cdot Q \)
    Pressure (effort) drives volume flow rate (flow), transferring fluid energy.
  • Thermal Systems: \( P = T \cdot \dot{Q} \)
    Temperature difference (effort) drives heat flow.
The form "effort × flow" is not a coincidence — it reflects a universal structure underlying all energy transfer processes. Different branches of physics use different variables, but the underlying mechanism remains the same.

Relation to Energy

Power is defined as the rate at which energy changes:

\[ P = \frac{dE}{dt} \]

Substituting the effort–flow structure:

\[ \frac{dE}{dt} = (\text{Effort}) \times (\text{Flow}) \]

Therefore,

\[ dE = (\text{Effort}) \times (\text{Flow}) \; dt \]

This shows that whenever a flow occurs under the action of an effort, energy is being transferred or transformed.

In other words:

  • No flow ⇒ no energy transfer
  • No effort ⇒ no driving force for transfer
  • Both together ⇒ energy transfer
Energy transfer is not a separate phenomenon — it is the result of flow occurring under an effort.

Storage vs Dissipation

This framework allows us to classify physical systems based on how they handle energy.

1. Storage Systems

In storage systems, effort is proportional to the rate of change of flow:

\[ \text{Effort} \propto \frac{d(\text{Flow})}{dt} \]

Examples:

  • \( F = m\,\frac{dv}{dt} \) (mass)
  • \( V = L\,\frac{dI}{dt} \) (inductor)

Here, energy is stored in the system:

  • Kinetic energy in mass
  • Magnetic field energy in inductors

Because energy must be stored, the system resists change in flow. This gives rise to inertia-like behavior.

2. Dissipative Systems

In dissipative systems, effort is proportional directly to flow:

\[ \text{Effort} \propto \text{Flow} \]

Examples:

  • \( V = IR \) (resistor)
  • \( F = bv \) (viscous drag)

Here, energy is not stored but continuously converted into heat or other irreversible forms.

As a result:

  • No energy accumulation
  • No resistance to change in flow
  • No memory of past states
In storage systems, energy is temporarily held within the system — like a self-transfer process — leading to resistance to change. In dissipative systems, energy immediately leaves the system, so no such resistance appears.

Connection to Inductance

For an inductor:

\[ P = V I = \left(L \frac{dI}{dt}\right) I \] \[ P = L I \frac{dI}{dt} \]

Integrating with respect to time:

\[ U = \int P\,dt = \int L I \frac{dI}{dt} dt \] \[ U = \int L I \, dI = \frac{1}{2} L I^2 \]

Thus, the effort–flow framework directly leads to the expression for energy stored in an inductor.

The "inertia" of an inductor arises because changing current requires power, which is stored in the magnetic field. The system resists change because energy must be built up or released.

Important Note

The relation "Power = Effort × Flow" is not a fundamental law like conservation of energy.

Conservation of energy states that energy cannot be created or destroyed.

The effort–flow relation, on the other hand, describes how energy transfer takes place.

Thus:

  • Conservation of energy → tells us energy is conserved
  • Effort × Flow → tells us how energy moves
The effort–flow framework is a universal pattern that unifies different branches of physics by revealing a common structure behind energy transfer.
