Induced Electric Field

Non-Conservative Electric Fields.

Necessity of Electric Field

In motional EMF, when a conductor moves through a magnetic field, the induced EMF can be understood using the Lorentz force. Charges inside the conductor experience a magnetic force \( q\vec{v}\times\vec{B} \), which drives them along the conductor and produces current.

However, this explanation fails when the conductor is stationary and the magnetic field changes with time. In such a situation, an EMF is still induced in the circuit.

This immediately raises an important question:

What force drives the charges if the conductor is not moving?

A magnetic field cannot provide the answer, because magnetic force acts only on moving charges. Charges initially at rest inside a stationary conductor cannot be set into motion by a magnetic field alone.

Therefore, there must exist an electric field inside the conductor that pushes the charges around the circuit.

This leads to a fundamental conclusion:

A time-varying magnetic field produces an electric field.

This electric field is called the induced electric field.

Magnetic force explains motional EMF (moving conductor). Induced electric field explains EMF in stationary circuits. Both are different physical mechanisms, but both ultimately appear through Faraday's Law.

Non-Conservative Nature of Induced Electric Field

Consider a charge \( q \) moving once around a closed conducting loop. The total work done on it by the induced electric field must equal the energy supplied per unit charge, i.e. the EMF.

Thus,

\[ \text{Work per unit charge} = \mathcal{E} \]

But work done per unit charge by an electric field is given by the line integral:

\[ \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} \]

Therefore,

\[ \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = \mathcal{E} \]

From Faraday's Law,

\[ \mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{\oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}} \]

This result has a very important implication.

The line integral of the induced electric field around a closed loop is not zero. Therefore, the induced electric field is non-conservative.

Important Note on Applicability

Faraday's Law in the form

\[ \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \]

is valid when the integration path (loop) is stationary.

The general form

\[ \mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \]

is always valid, even when the loop itself is moving.

Example: Circular Loop

Consider a stationary circular loop of radius \( r \). The magnetic field is time-varying and has cylindrical symmetry about the axis of the loop (for example, a uniform magnetic field changing with time and directed perpendicular to the plane).

Because the source (magnetic field) is symmetric under rotation about the axis, the induced electric field must also respect this symmetry.

Therefore, \( \vec{E} \) is the same at all points of the loop of given radius \( r \).

At a general point P, we break \( \vec{E} \) into tangential and radial components and try to decipher their magnitudes.

Step 1: Tangential Component from Faraday's Law

Faraday's Law states:

\[ \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \]

Since the magnetic flux is changing with time, the right-hand side is non-zero. Therefore,

\[ \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} \neq 0 \]

This means that the electric field must have a non-zero component along the path, i.e., a tangential component.

Faraday's Law mandates that the induced electric field must have a non-zero tangential component, so that the circulation of \( \vec{E} \) around the loop is non-zero.

Step 2: Radial Component from Gauss's Law

Gauss's Law for electric fields states:

\[ \nabla \cdot \vec{E} = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} \]

In this situation, there is no charge present in space (\( \rho = 0 \)). Therefore,

\[ \nabla \cdot \vec{E} = 0 \]

This implies that the electric field has no sources or sinks.

A radial component would correspond to field lines either emerging outward or converging inward, which would imply the presence of a source or sink of field lines (i.e., charge).

Since no such charges exist, the radial component must be zero.

Gauss's Law mandates that the induced electric field has no radial component, because there are no charges to act as sources or sinks.

Step 3: Final Form of the Field

Combining both results:

  • Faraday's Law ⇒ tangential component ≠ 0
  • Gauss's Law ⇒ radial component = 0

Therefore, the induced electric field must be purely tangential and form closed circular loops.

\[ \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = E \cdot (2\pi r) \]

Using Faraday's Law,

\[ E \cdot 2\pi r = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \]

Hence,

\[ E = -\frac{1}{2\pi r}\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \]
The induced electric field forms closed loops in space. It is fundamentally different from electrostatic fields, which originate from charges and are conservative.

Faraday to KVL

In circuits with steady currents, we use Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law (KVL), which assumes that the electric field is conservative:

\[ \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = 0 \]

However, when the magnetic field varies with time, an induced electric field is created which is non-conservative.

\[ \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \]

Thus KVL is replaced by:

\[ \sum IR = \frac{d\Phi}{dt} \]

We usually add dummy battery and solve circuit using KVL. But fundamentally - we are using Faraday only.

Two Coupled Loops

Question

A wire frame consists of two adjacent loops placed in a region where a uniform magnetic field is directed into the page. The magnetic field increases at a constant rate:

\[ \frac{dB}{dt} = 1 \, \text{T/s} \]
  • Resistance of wire = 1 Ω per meter
  • Left loop: square of side 1 m
  • Right loop: rectangle of dimensions 1 m × 0.5 m

Find the current in each branch.

Solution

Step 1: Induced EMF

\[ A_1 = 1, \quad \mathcal{E}_1 = 1 \] \[ A_2 = 0.5, \quad \mathcal{E}_2 = 0.5 \]

Step 2: Direction

Magnetic field increases into page → currents are anticlockwise.

Let \( I_1 \) = left loop, \( I_2 \) = right loop.

Step 3: Apply Faraday (ΣIR = emf)

Left loop:

\[ 4I_1 - I_2 = 1 \]

Right loop:

\[ - I_1 + 3I_2 = 0.5 \]

Step 4: Solve

\[ I_2 = \frac{3}{11}, \quad I_1 = \frac{7}{22} \]

Middle branch current:

\[ I = I_1 - I_2 = \frac{1}{22} \]
Key Insight

This problem demonstrates:

• Electric field is non-conservative • KVL does not apply in usual form • Must use Faraday’s Law directly

Battery substitution is only a mathematical trick — not the physical origin.
