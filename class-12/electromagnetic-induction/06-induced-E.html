Induced Electric Field

Transformer Induction Mechanism.

Concept

Electromagnetic induction is an observed phenomenon. Scientists have studied it to understand whether it is a fundamental process or a manifestation of underlying electromagnetic processes.

In Motional EMF, we understood the mechanism of induction through the magnetic part of the Lorentz force. The magnetic force on moving charges explains how a moving conductor develops an EMF in a magnetic field.

However, this explanation based on magnetic force alone is insufficient when the conductor is stationary and only the magnetic field changes with time, yet an EMF is still induced.

Consider a region containing a spatially uniform magnetic field directed along \(\hat{k}\), whose magnitude changes with time. Now consider a stationary test charge placed at a point in this region where the induced electric field is non-zero.

Since the charge is stationary, its velocity is zero. Therefore, the magnetic part of the Lorentz force is zero. Yet the charge experiences a force.

This raises an important question: What field exerts a force on a stationary charge when the magnetic field changes with time?

It cannot be the magnetic force, because the charge is stationary. There must therefore be an electric field produced by the time-varying magnetic field.

This thus became a new fundamental process in electromagnetism — a time-varying magnetic field produces an electric field, and such a field is called an induced electric field.

Later we will study that a time-varying electric field produces a magnetic field.
Thus, we do not need a static charge or a static magnet to produce an electric and a magnetic field.
These fields can produce each other, and so light, which is an electromagnetic wave, does not require a medium for propagation.

Nature of Induced Electric Field

To determine the exact mathematical nature of the induced electric field, we start from the fundamental definition of Electromotive Force (EMF).

By definition, the EMF \(\mathcal{E}\) around any closed circuit is the closed-loop line integral of the total force per unit charge (\(\vec{f} = \vec{F}/q\)) acting along the path at a given instant:

\[ \mathcal{E} \equiv \oint \vec{f} \cdot d\vec{l} \]

Contrast with a Chemical Battery

In a standard DC circuit driven by a battery, two distinct forces act on the charges:

  • Inside the battery (from negative terminal \(A\) to positive terminal \(B\)): A localized non-electrostatic chemical force per unit charge, \(\vec{f}_{\text{chem}}\), pumps positive charges "uphill" from \(A\) to \(B\).
  • Everywhere in space (both inside the battery and along the external wires): Charges accumulated at the terminals and wire bends create an ordinary electrostatic field, \(\vec{E}_{\text{es}}\).

Applying the definition of EMF around the complete closed loop:

\[ \mathcal{E} = \oint \left(\vec{f}_{\text{chem}} + \vec{E}_{\text{es}}\right) \cdot d\vec{l} = \oint \vec{f}_{\text{chem}} \cdot d\vec{l} + \oint \vec{E}_{\text{es}} \cdot d\vec{l} \]

Because \(\vec{E}_{\text{es}}\) is conservative, its closed-loop circulation vanishes (\(\oint \vec{E}_{\text{es}} \cdot d\vec{l} = 0\)), and because \(\vec{f}_{\text{chem}}\) exists only inside the battery, the entire loop integral collapses to the interior of the battery:

\[ \mathcal{E} = \int_A^B \vec{f}_{\text{chem}} \cdot d\vec{l} \]

In the external circuit outside the battery, the current is driven purely by the conservative electrostatic field \(\vec{E}_{\text{es}}\).

Circulation of the Induced Electric Field

Now consider a stationary closed loop in a time-varying magnetic field, with no battery present (\(\vec{f}_{\text{chem}} = 0\)) and no conductor motion (\(\vec{v} = 0 \implies \vec{v} \times \vec{B} = 0\)).

The only force per unit charge acting along the loop is the total electric field \(\vec{E}\) (which can include both any electrostatic field \(\vec{E}_{\text{es}}\) from charge buildup and the induced electric field \(\vec{E}_{\text{ind}}\) from the changing magnetic field):

\[ \mathcal{E} = \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} \]

Combining this with Faraday's Law (\(\mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt}\)):

\[ \boxed{\oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt}} \]

Since any electrostatic component satisfies \(\oint \vec{E}_{\text{es}} \cdot d\vec{l} = 0\), the non-zero closed-loop integral comes entirely from the induced electric field:

\[ \oint \vec{E}_{\text{ind}} \cdot d\vec{l} = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt} \neq 0 \]
Conservative vs. Non-Conservative Fields
In a battery circuit, the non-conservative push (\(\vec{f}_{\text{chem}}\)) is trapped inside the battery box.
Everywhere in the external wires, the field is purely electrostatic (\(\oint \vec{E}_{\text{es}} \cdot d\vec{l} = 0\)), so potential \(V\) is uniquely defined.
In electromagnetic induction, the induced electric field \(\vec{E}_{\text{ind}}\) has a non-zero closed-loop circulation (\(\oint \vec{E}_{\text{ind}} \cdot d\vec{l} \neq 0\)).
Therefore, \(\vec{E}_{\text{ind}}\) is non-conservative and is distributed continuously throughout space rather than localized inside a battery.

Exhaustive Faraday's Law

The circulation equation

\[ \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt} \]

holds strictly when the closed integration path is stationary in the observer's frame.

When the loop moves or deforms in a time-varying magnetic field, the general flux rule \(\mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt}\) remains valid because it automatically combines both mechanisms—the motional \(\vec{v} \times \vec{B}\) force and the induced electric field \(\vec{E}_{\text{ind}}\):

\[ \mathcal{E} = \oint \left(\vec{v} \times \vec{B}\right) \cdot d\vec{l} - \iint_S \frac{\partial \vec{B}}{\partial t} \cdot d\vec{A} \]

For a planar loop of instantaneous area vector \(\vec{A}\) in a spatially uniform magnetic field \(\vec{B}(t)\), this simplifies to:

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{E} = \oint \left(\vec{v} \times \vec{B}\right) \cdot d\vec{l} - \vec{A} \cdot \frac{d\vec{B}}{dt}} \]
Magnetic Lorentz force explains motional EMF when a conductor moves through a magnetic field.
An induced electric field explains induction when a magnetic field changes with time.
These are two distinct physical mechanisms, and Faraday's Law unifies their combined effect.

Cylindrically Symmetric Magnetic Field

As part of the syllabus, we deal with one primary geometric configuration for induced electric field problems: a time-varying magnetic field that has cylindrical symmetry about an axis (such as inside a long solenoid).

To determine the induced electric field produced by such a magnetic field, we use the circulation relation established earlier: \(\oint \vec{E}_{\text{ind}} \cdot d\vec{l} = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt}\).

Consider an imaginary stationary circular path of radius \(r\) coaxial with a cylindrically symmetric magnetic field confined to a cylindrical region of radius \(R\). The circular path is intentionally chosen to exploit this symmetry.

Because the source (\(\frac{d\vec{B}}{dt}\)) is cylindrically symmetric, the magnitude of the induced electric field \(\vec{E}\) (as well as its radial and tangential components) must be identical at all points along a circle of radius \(r\). By translational and reflection symmetry along the axis, there is no axial component (\(E_z = 0\)).

At a general point \(P\) on this circle, we resolve \(\vec{E}\) into a tangential component \(E_t\) and a radial component \(E_r\).

Step 1: Tangential Component from Faraday's Law

Applying Faraday's Law around the stationary circular path of radius \(r\):

\[ \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt} \]

Since the magnetic flux through the circle is changing with time, the right-hand side is non-zero. Thus, the electric field must have a non-zero tangential component \(E_t\) along the circular path.

Step 2: Radial Component from Gauss's Law

Consider a closed cylindrical Gaussian surface of radius \(r\) and height \(dH\) coaxial with the magnetic field, with our circular path lying on its curved surface. By Gauss's Law for electricity:

\[ \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0} \]

Because there is no net electric charge enclosed in space (\(Q_{\text{enc}} = 0\)), the total electric flux out of the closed cylinder is zero. With \(E_z = 0\), zero flux crosses the top and bottom flat caps, leaving only the curved surface flux \(E_r (2\pi r \, dH) = 0\). Thus, no field lines diverge outward or converge inward radially—the radial component is zero (\(E_r = 0\)).

Step 3: Final Form of the Field

Combining both laws:

  • Faraday's Law \(\Rightarrow\) tangential component \(E_t \neq 0\)
  • Gauss's Law \(\Rightarrow\) radial component \(E_r = 0\)

Therefore, the induced electric field is purely tangential and forms closed concentric circular loops around the axis:

\[ \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = E_t (2\pi r) = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt} \] \[ \boxed{E_t = -\frac{1}{2\pi r}\frac{d\Phi}{dt}} \]

Here, the minus sign indicates that the direction of \(\vec{E}_{\text{ind}}\) opposes the change in flux (given directly by Lenz's Law).

Inside vs. Outside a Cylindrical Field of Radius \(R\)

If a spatially uniform magnetic field \(B(t)\) is confined to a cylinder of radius \(R\), the magnitude \(E\) of the induced electric field varies with radial distance \(r\) as:

  • Inside (\(r \lt R\)): \(\Phi = B(\pi r^2) \implies E = \frac{r}{2}\left|\frac{dB}{dt}\right|\) (increases linearly, \(E \propto r\)).
  • Outside (\(r \gt R\)): \(\Phi = B(\pi R^2) \implies E = \frac{R^2}{2r}\left|\frac{dB}{dt}\right|\) (falls off as \(E \propto \frac{1}{r}\), even though \(B = 0\) outside!).

Faraday to KVL

In circuits with steady currents, we use Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law (KVL), which relies on the electrostatic field being conservative:

\[ \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = 0 \]

However, when a time-varying magnetic field threads a circuit, the induced electric field is non-conservative:

\[ \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt} \]

Thus, standard KVL is replaced by Faraday's loop law:

\[ \sum IR = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt} \]

In circuit problems, we replace each loop's changing flux with a "dummy battery" of EMF \(\left|\frac{d\Phi}{dt}\right|\) (oriented according to Lenz's Law) and apply standard KVL.

While this mathematical trick always yields the correct branch currents, remember that no localized battery exists—the EMF is distributed continuously along the wire.

The Voltmeter Trap:
Because \(\vec{E}_{\text{ind}}\) is non-conservative, potential difference \(V\) between two points of the circuit is not uniquely defined.
Two identical voltmeters connected across the exact same two nodes of a ring in a changing magnetic field will show different readings if their connecting leads enclose different amounts of magnetic flux!

Solved Example: Two Coupled Loops

Question

A wire frame consists of two adjacent loops placed in a region where a uniform magnetic field is directed into the page. The magnetic field increases at a constant rate:

\[ \frac{dB}{dt} = 1 \, \text{T/s} \]
  • Resistance of wire = \(1\,\Omega\text{/m}\)
  • Left loop (\(AEFD\)): square of side \(1\,\text{m}\)
  • Right loop (\(EBCF\)): rectangle of dimensions \(1\,\text{m} \times 0.5\,\text{m}\)

Find the current in each branch.

Solution

Step 1: Induced EMF Magnitude in Each Loop

\[ A_1 = 1\,\text{m}^2 \implies \mathcal{E}_1 = A_1 \frac{dB}{dt} = 1\,\text{V} \] \[ A_2 = 0.5\,\text{m}^2 \implies \mathcal{E}_2 = A_2 \frac{dB}{dt} = 0.5\,\text{V} \]

Step 2: Direction of Loop Currents

Since the magnetic field into the page is increasing, Lenz's Law requires the induced currents to produce a field out of the page. Thus, both loop currents circulate counter-clockwise (CCW).

Let \( I_1 \) be the CCW mesh current in the left loop (\(AEFD\)) and \( I_2 \) be the CCW mesh current in the right loop (\(EBCF\)). Along the shared branch \(EF\), \( I_1 \) flows downward (\(E \to F\)) while \( I_2 \) flows upward (\(F \to E\)).

Step 3: Apply Faraday's Loop Law (\(\sum IR = \mathcal{E}\))

Left loop (\(AEFD\), perimeter = \(4\,\text{m} \implies R_1 = 4\,\Omega\)):

\[ 3I_1 + 1(I_1 - I_2) = 1 \implies 4I_1 - I_2 = 1 \]

Right loop (\(EBCF\), perimeter = \(3\,\text{m} \implies R_2 = 3\,\Omega\)):

\[ 2I_2 + 1(I_2 - I_1) = 0.5 \implies -I_1 + 3I_2 = 0.5 \]

Step 4: Solve the Linear System

Multiplying the first equation by \(3\) and adding it to the second gives \(11I_1 = 3.5\), so:

\[ I_1 = \frac{7}{22}\,\text{A} \quad (\text{in branch } FDAE), \qquad I_2 = \frac{3}{11}\,\text{A} \quad (\text{in branch } EBCF) \]

Current in the middle branch \(EF\):

\[ I_{EF} = I_1 - I_2 = \frac{7}{22} - \frac{6}{22} = \frac{1}{22}\,\text{A} \quad (\text{downward, from } E \text{ to } F) \]
Key Insight

This problem demonstrates why you cannot assign a single scalar potential \(V_E\) and \(V_F\) to the junctions:

- The induced electric field is non-conservative.
- Standard KVL (\(\sum \Delta V = 0\)) does not apply.
- Applying Faraday’s Law directly to each closed mesh (\(\sum IR = \mathcal{E}\)) resolves the network cleanly.

Battery substitution is only a computational shortcut for finding currents — not the physical reality.
