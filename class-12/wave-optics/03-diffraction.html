Diffraction of Light

The bending of light around the corners of obstacles and apertures.

Introduction to Diffraction

When light encounters an obstacle or a slit whose size is comparable to its wavelength (\( \lambda \)), it bends around the edges and encroaches into the region of geometrical shadow. This phenomenon is called diffraction. It is a direct consequence of the wave nature of light, stemming from Huygens' principle where secondary wavelets from the exposed part of the wavefront superpose.

Sound waves diffract easily around buildings because their wavelengths are very large (meters). Light waves have very small wavelengths (\( \sim 10^{-7} \text{ m} \)), so diffraction of light is only noticeable when passing through very narrow slits or around tiny obstacles.

Fresnel vs Fraunhofer Diffraction

Diffraction phenomena are broadly classified into two categories based on the distances of the source and screen from the diffracting obstacle.

  • Fresnel Diffraction: The source of light or the screen (or both) are at a finite distance from the obstacle. The incident and diffracted wavefronts are spherical or cylindrical. Lenses are not required. It is mathematically complex.
  • Fraunhofer Diffraction: The source of light and the screen are at infinite distances from the obstacle. The incident wavefront is plane. This is achieved practically by placing the source at the focus of a converging lens and the screen at the focus of another. It is mathematically simpler and mostly studied in the JEE syllabus.
