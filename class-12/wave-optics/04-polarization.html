Polarization of Light

Restricting the vibrations of light to a single plane.

Unpolarized and Polarized Light

Light is a transverse electromagnetic wave, consisting of oscillating electric and magnetic fields perpendicular to the direction of propagation.

Unpolarized Light: The electric field vectors vibrate randomly in all possible directions perpendicular to the direction of wave propagation (like light from the sun or a bulb).

Polarized Light: The electric field vectors are restricted to vibrate in a single specific plane. This phenomenon is called polarization, and it proves that light is a transverse wave (longitudinal waves like sound cannot be polarized).

A polarizer (like a Polaroid sheet) passes only those electric field vibrations that are parallel to its transmission axis, blocking all others. When unpolarized light of intensity \( I_0 \) passes through a polarizer, the transmitted polarized light has intensity \( I = \frac{I_0}{2} \).

Methods of Polarization

Besides using Polaroid sheets (dichroism), unpolarized light can become partially or fully polarized through natural processes:

  • By Scattering: When unpolarized light is scattered by molecules (like in the sky), the light scattered at 90° to the incident direction is completely plane-polarized.
  • By Reflection: When unpolarized light reflects off a non-metallic surface (like water or glass), the reflected light is partially polarized. At a specific angle, it becomes completely polarized.

Malus's Law and Brewster's Law

Malus's Law

When completely plane-polarized light of intensity \( I_0 \) is incident on an analyzer, the intensity \( I \) of the transmitted light depends on the angle \( \theta \) between the transmission axes of the polarizer and the analyzer.

\[ I = I_0 \cos^2 \theta \] This is Malus's Law. If the axes are parallel (\( \theta = 0^\circ \)), \( I = I_0 \). If they are crossed (\( \theta = 90^\circ \)), \( I = 0 \).

Brewster's Law

When unpolarized light is incident on a transparent boundary, at a certain angle of incidence called the Brewster angle (or polarizing angle, \( i_p \)), the reflected light is completely plane-polarized (with electric vectors perpendicular to the plane of incidence).

At this specific angle, the reflected and refracted rays are exactly perpendicular to each other (\( i_p + r = 90^\circ \)). Using Snell's Law (\( n = \frac{\sin i_p}{\sin r} \)) and substituting \( r = 90^\circ - i_p \):

\[ n = \frac{\sin i_p}{\cos i_p} = \tan i_p \] This is Brewster's Law. It relates the refractive index of the medium to the polarizing angle.
