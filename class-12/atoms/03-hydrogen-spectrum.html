Line Spectrum Experiment

Decoding the unique electromagnetic fingerprints of elements.

Gas Discharge Tubes: Probing the Subatomic Realm

A gas discharge tube is the fundamental experimental apparatus that birthed modern atomic physics.

It consists of a sealed glass envelope containing a gas at controlled low pressure, fitted with two metallic electrodes (a negative Cathode and a positive Anode) connected to a high-voltage source.

Under normal atmospheric conditions, gases are excellent electrical insulators. This is because they do not have free charged particles in large numbers (like metals) which may respond to an applied electric field.

However, by evacuating the tube and applying an immense potential difference (often exceeding 10 kV), electrical breakdown occurs, allowing current to flow through the gas. This mechanism of charge flow is much different from the common conduction in a metallic resistor that we are used to. It is called as gas discharge.

Control Parameters

There are two main control parameters to customize the experiment:

  • Gas Pressure (\(P\)): This is the main control variable which determines the Mean Free Path (\(\lambda\)) of the electrons.
  • Distance between Electrodes (\(d\)): This is the main control variable which determines the magnitude of Applied Voltage (\(V\)).

Mean Free Path (\(\lambda\))

The behavior of the electrical discharge inside the tube is fundamentally governed by the gas pressure.

Manipulating the internal pressure directly alters the number density of gas molecules, which in turn dictates the mean free path of an accelerated electron—the average distance an electron travels before colliding with a gas obstacle.

By adjusting the ratio of the mean free path to the electrode distance (d), physicists can shift the apparatus between two entirely distinct physical regimes: Atomic Luminescence and Pure Particle Beams.

The Two Critical Operating Regimes

  1. The Luminescence Regime: Line Spectra (\( \approx 10^{-1}\) to \(10^{-2}\) Torr)

    • Condition: The mean free path is extremely short compared to the tube length (\( \lambda \lt d\)).
    • Physical Mechanism: Electrons emitted from the cathode accelerate briefly before undergoing massive numbers of inelastic collisions with neutral gas molecules. If the gas is molecular (like \( H_2\)), the initial impacts transfer kinetic energy to break the strong covalent bonds (Dissociation: \( H_2 \rightarrow 2H\)). Subsequent electron collisions knock the bound electrons of these free atoms into higher stationary states.
    • Observable Output: As the excited atoms spontaneously drop back to lower energy levels, the entire volume of gas glows brightly with a characteristic elemental color (the Positive Column). Passing this emitted light through a prism yields the sharp, discrete wavelengths of an emission line spectrum.

  2. The Cathode Ray Regime: Subatomic Particles (\( \approx 10^{-4}\) to \(10^{-6}\) Torr)

    • Condition: The tube is evacuated to a high vacuum (Crookes vacuum) where the mean free path exceeds the physical length of the apparatus (\( \lambda \approx d\)).
    • Physical Mechanism: Because practically all gas molecules have been pumped out, an electron leaving the cathode accelerates continuously toward the anode without encountering a single gas obstacle. Because there are no collisions to trigger atomic excitation, the ambient gas glow completely extinguishes (known as the expansion of the Crookes Dark Space until it fills the tube).
    • Observable Output: High-speed electrons shoot straight through the vacuum as an uninterrupted, ballistic subatomic stream. This invisible projectile beam—the cathode ray—travels in a straight line until it strikes the far glass wall or a phosphorescent screen, producing a localized localized glowing spot. J.J. Thomson utilized precisely this regime to isolate and discover the electron.

What is Line Spectrum?

Heat a solid metal tungsten filament in the bulb, by heating it through passage of current. It starts glowing and the color depends on temperature. This is called as incandescence.

Use a prism (or a diffraction grating) to separate the various constituent wavelengths from the incandescent light into a spectrum and process it on a photographic plate.

It is observed that the spectrum is continuous; light of all wavelengths is present.

But if the source is a gas carrying an electric discharge (like Hydrogen), then only a few colors appear, in the form of isolated sharp parallel lines.

A spectrum of this sort is called a line spectrum. Each line corresponds to a definite wavelength and frequency.

It was discovered early in the 19th century that each element in its gaseous state has a unique set of wavelengths in its line spectrum.
This mechanism of luminescence is not incandesescence, but called as gas discharge.
The spectrum of hydrogen always contains a certain set of wavelengths; neon produces a different set, and so on.
Scientists find the use of spectra to identify elements and compounds to be an invaluable tool. For instance, astronomers have detected the spectra from more than 100 different molecules in interstellar space, including some that are not found naturally on earth.

The characteristic spectrum of an atom was presumably related to its internal structure, but attempts to understand this relationship solely on the basis of classical mechanics and electrodynamics - classical physics summarized in Newton's three laws and Maxwell's four equations - were not successful.

Emission and Absorption Spectra

Line Spectrum is one of the greatest discoveries made using gas discharge tubes because it demonstrated that atoms do not emit arbitrary wavelengths of light. Instead, every element emits and absorbs only certain specific wavelengths, producing a unique spectral fingerprint.

This observation became one of the strongest evidences for the existence of discrete internal energy states inside atoms.

Emission Spectrum

When a low-pressure gas is excited using electrical discharge, electrons inside atoms gain energy and jump to higher energy states.

These excited states are unstable. The electrons eventually return to lower energy states by emitting photons.

Because atomic energy levels are discrete, only certain specific energy differences are possible.

Therefore, only certain specific photon frequencies (or wavelengths) are emitted.

When this emitted light is passed through a prism or diffraction grating, it separates into discrete bright lines called an emission line spectrum.

Each bright spectral line corresponds to a specific electronic transition between two allowed energy states.
Different elements possess different internal energy structures, and therefore produce different spectral fingerprints.
This made spectroscopy one of the most powerful tools for identifying atomic structure and chemical composition.

Absorption Spectrum

Suppose continuous white light is passed through a comparatively cooler low-pressure gas before entering a prism.

Atoms in the gas absorb only those photons whose energies exactly match the allowed energy differences between atomic states.

The absorbed photons are therefore missing from the transmitted light.

When the transmitted light is analyzed using a prism, a continuous spectrum containing dark missing lines is observed. This is called an absorption spectrum.

The dark absorption lines occur at exactly the same wavelengths as the bright emission lines of that element.
Emission and absorption are therefore complementary processes involving the same allowed atomic transitions.
Absorption spectrum is also a line spectrum and it is a photographic negative of the emission spectrum.

Correction in Chem NCERT

The NCERT diagram for hydrogen absorption spectrum in atomic structure chapter of physical chemistry, is conceptually misleading if interpreted literally.

The figure shows multiple dark absorption lines throughout the visible region, suggesting that ordinary hydrogen gas absorbs visible wavelengths extensively.

However, under normal conditions, this is not correct for hydrogen.

At room temperature and low pressure, almost all hydrogen atoms are in the ground state (\(n=1\)).

Therefore, absorption can primarily occur only from:

\[ n=1 \rightarrow n=2,3,4,... \]

These transitions belong to the Lyman series, which lies in the ultraviolet region.

For ordinary hydrogen gas, absorption mainly occurs in the ultraviolet region, not throughout the visible spectrum.
The commonly shown visible dark-line absorption pattern would require a significant population of hydrogen atoms already present in excited states such as \(n=2\).
But under ordinary conditions, the population of excited hydrogen atoms is extremely small because excited states rapidly decay back to the ground state.

Thus, the visible Balmer lines are prominently observed in emission spectra of excited hydrogen gas, but not typically as strong absorption lines in ordinary cool hydrogen gas.
