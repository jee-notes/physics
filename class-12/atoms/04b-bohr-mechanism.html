Mechanism of Energy Transactions between Atom and Light.

Bohr Mechanism

The postulates of Bohr did more than just assigning allowed states to an atom.

They provided a mechanism for understanding how matter and radiation interact through energy.

Any atom (need not be only hydrogen like) cannot absorb or release an arbitrary amount of energy. Because the electron is restricted to certain allowed stationary states, the atom can have only certain allowed energies.

Therefore, when an atom interacts with radiation, the energy exchange is governed by its internal quantized structure.

If the atom absorbs energy, the electron moves from one allowed energy state to another higher allowed state. If the atom releases energy, the electron moves from a higher allowed state to a lower allowed state.

The energy exchanged in such a transition is not arbitrary. It is exactly equal to the difference between the two allowed energy states and is carried by a photon.

\[ h\nu = |E_f-E_i| \]

Thus, Bohr's model provides a physical mechanism for the experimentally observed fact that atoms absorb and emit radiation only at certain specific energies and therefore at certain specific wavelengths.

Bohr's model was able to provide quantitative description of this mechanism only for simple cases of Hydrogen like atoms that are single electron species.

Quantitative description of Mechanism

We discuss the mechanism quantitatively in this section.

We are considering a single atom of hydrogen gas \(Z=1\) . Its not practically possible to extract just 1 atom for study. But, it is pure gas at extremely low pressure in a glass tube. So, we can consider a single atom.

We have already calculated that the total energy of the hydrogen atom is given by:

\[ TE_n=-13.6\frac{Z^2}{n^2}\text{ eV} = \frac{-13.6}{n^2} \qquad \text{as } Z =1 \]

We can put \(n=1,2,3,\dots\) . The minimum energy is at \(n=1\) , TE = \(-13.6\text{ eV}\) .

If the atom absorbs energy, the electron moves from one allowed energy state to another higher allowed state. If the atom releases energy, the electron moves from a higher allowed state to a lower allowed state.

This minimum energy level of the atom when \(n=1\) is known as the ground state. It is the lowest allowed energy state of any hydrogen like atom and it exists because an atom exists as a stable system.

As n increases, the total energy of the atom also increases.

\[ n \uparrow \qquad \leftrightarrow \qquad TE \uparrow \]

We can now examine how the different Bohr parameters change as \(n\) increases. We already know that the allowed values of the main parameters are functions of \(n\).

For a hydrogen-like atom, the radius of the \(n\)-th orbit is

\[ r_n=0.529\frac{n^2}{Z}\,\mathring{\text{A}} \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{r_n\propto n^2} \]

As \(n\) increases, the electron moves to an orbit of larger radius. The distance between the nucleus and the electron therefore increases.

The velocity of the electron is

\[ v_n=\frac{c}{137}\frac{Z}{n} \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{v_n\propto\frac{1}{n}} \]

Therefore, as \(n\) increases, the electron revolves more slowly.

Since the kinetic energy is

\[ KE_n=\frac12mv_n^2 \]

we get

\[ \boxed{KE_n\propto\frac{1}{n^2}} \]

Thus, the kinetic energy decreases as \(n\) increases.

The potential energy is

\[ PE_n=-2KE_n \]

and therefore

\[ \boxed{PE_n\propto-\frac{1}{n^2}} \]

Hence, the potential energy becomes less negative (increases) as \(n\) increases.

The total energy is

\[ TE_n=-KE_n \]

so

\[ \boxed{TE_n\propto-\frac{1}{n^2}} \]

Therefore, the total energy also increases with \(n\), but remains negative for every bound state.

As \(n\) increases:

\[ r_n\uparrow \qquad v_n\downarrow \qquad KE_n\downarrow \qquad PE_n\uparrow \qquad TE_n\uparrow \]
The electron moves farther from the nucleus, moves more slowly, has less kinetic energy, while its potential energy and total energy become progressively less negative.

This gives a useful physical picture of excitation. Moving the electron to a higher \(n\) means moving it to a larger orbit with lower speed and higher total energy.

The energy levels therefore become progressively closer to the ionization limit \(TE=0\) as \(n\) increases.

Incandescence vs Emission Spectrum

Both incandescence and emission spectrum are natural phenomena that involve matter emitting electromagnetic radiation.

The fundamental mechanism behind both phenomenon is same - matter can emit electromagnetic radiation only as quantized energy packets.

But the nature of their emission spectrum is different. Incandescence shows a continuous spectrum whereas an emission spectrum of a particular atom is unique.

This difference is not due to the difference in mechanism, but due to difference between the physical condition of the two types of matter and, consequently, in the nature of the spectrum produced.

When a dilute gas is excited, its atoms are relatively far apart and can be treated approximately as independent atoms. Each atom has a discrete set of allowed energy states, and therefore it can emit photons only for specific transitions between those states. The result is a line emission spectrum containing discrete wavelengths characteristic of the atoms present.

If a mixture of different atomic species is excited (like a mixture of \(H\) and \(He^+\)), each species would contribute its own characteristic spectral lines. The observed spectrum would therefore be a combination of the emission spectra of the different constituents of the mixture.

Incandescence is different because it occurs in dense matter (or bulk matter).

This condensed matter has an enormous number of atoms and molecules that are electromagnetically interacting strongly with one another.

Their individual atomic energy states are combined, broadened and split into very closely spaced states. A huge number of possible energy transitions therefore contribute to the radiation.

These spectral features overlap so densely that the emitted radiation appears as an effectively continuous spectrum rather than a collection of isolated lines.

In a dilute gas, atoms behave approximately as independent systems.
Their discrete energy levels therefore produce distinct spectral lines.
In dense matter, the enormous number of interacting particles produces a very large number of closely spaced and broadened allowed states. The resulting radiation appears effectively continuous.
Thus, a continuous spectrum does not mean that microscopic matter has lost its discrete structure.
The continuous macroscopic spectrum can emerge from the collective behaviour of a huge number of microscopic constituents.

This is why a hot solid such as an incandescent filament produces a broad continuous spectrum, whereas an excited dilute gas produces characteristic emission lines.
