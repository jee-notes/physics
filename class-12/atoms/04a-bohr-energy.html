Bohr Energy

Energy Levels in Bohr Model.

Third Postulate

The third postulate is the main operational postulate of Bohr's model. The first two postulates establish the allowed structure of the atom; the third describes how this quantized atom actually interacts with its surroundings through energy exchange.

We can think of a single atom as a system with which we can interact by supplying or extracting energy. In the context of atomic physics, this interaction is primarily observed through photons.

If an atom absorbs an incident photon, the atom gains energy. If an atom emits a photon, the atom loses energy.

The crucial point is that, because the atom has only discrete allowed energy states, its energy cannot change by an arbitrary amount. Energy exchange occurs through transitions between these allowed states.

When a photon is absorbed, the electron moves from a lower-energy state \(E_i\) to a higher-energy state \(E_f\). The energy of the absorbed photon is

\[ h\nu=\frac{hc}{\lambda}=E_f-E_i \]

When a photon is emitted, the electron moves from a higher-energy state \(E_i\) to a lower-energy state \(E_f\). The energy of the emitted photon is

\[ h\nu=\frac{hc}{\lambda}=E_i-E_f \]

Thus, the mechanism of atomic energy interaction is a transition between allowed energy states.

In the Bohr picture, these transitions correspond to jumps between the corresponding stationary orbits.

The quantized nature of the atom's interaction with its surroundings is therefore a direct consequence of its quantized internal structure: the electron is restricted to certain stationary orbits, and the atom can exchange only the energy differences between those allowed states.

Our direct experimental interaction with an atom is essentially through the energy it absorbs or emits.
We cannot directly observe an electron sitting in a particular stationary orbit.
Instead, we probe the atom through its interaction with energy, especially through absorption and emission of photons.
The third postulate therefore connects the internal quantized structure of the atom to its observable experimental behaviour.

The transition itself takes place over a very short time, typically on the order of \(10^{-9}\,\text{s}\) for many allowed atomic transitions.

Energy Levels in Hydrogen like Atom

The total energy of the atom as a complete system, is determined by the total mechanical energy of the entire atom system:

  • Kinetic Energy: The electron revolves around a fixed nucleus. So, only electron is assumed to have Kinetic Energy of the atom.
  • Potential Energy: This represents the electric potential energy of the electron-nucleus system.

The potential energy of the electron-nucleus system is negative which indicates that the electrostatic force between them is attractive in nature.

As the electrostatic force is attractive, if the distance between them increases, then the attractive force does negative work and the electric potential energy of the electron-nucleus system increases.

If the potential energy of the electron-nucleus system is represented by \(U(r)\) , where \(r\) is the distance between electron and nucleus, then \(U \uparrow\) when \(r \uparrow\) .

Thus, \(dU/dr \gt 0\) and \(F = -dU/dr \lt 0\) . Negative force indicates attraction.

We now derive energy relations. Mathematically, we can start with Kinetic Energy \(KE_n\) first. This is because we know the centripetal force relation

\[ \frac{mv^2}{r}=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{Ze^2}{r^2} \qquad mv^2 = \frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0r} \]

We know that \(KE=\frac{1}{2}mv^2\) and so:

\[ KE = \frac12mv^2 = \frac12\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0r} \]

Before substituting for \(r_n\) , we first determine potential energy (\(PE\)) as well. This is because there is an interesting relation between KE and PE.

For the electron-nucleus system, the electrostatic potential energy at a separation \(r\) is

\[ U(r)=U=PE=-\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{Ze^2}{r} \]

Thus, by observing the expression for both - PE and KE, we can say that:

\[ PE=-2(KE) \]

The total mechanical energy of the atom (\(TE\)) is by definition given as \(E=K+U\):

\[ TE = PE+KE = -2(KE) + KE = -(KE) \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{TE=-KE} \] \[ \boxed{PE=-2KE=2TE} \]

We now substitute the expression for \(r_n\) into the kinetic energy relation:

\[ KE_n=\frac12\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0r_n} \]

We know that

\[ r_n=\frac{\epsilon_0h^2}{\pi me^2}\frac{n^2}{Z} \]

Therefore,

\[ KE_n=\frac12\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{1}{\frac{\epsilon_0h^2}{\pi me^2}\frac{n^2}{Z}} \]

On simplifying,

\[ KE_n=\frac{me^4}{8\epsilon_0^2h^2}\frac{Z^2}{n^2} \]
\[ \boxed{KE_n=\frac{me^4}{8\epsilon_0^2h^2}\frac{Z^2}{n^2}} \] \[ \boxed{KE_n=13.6\frac{Z^2}{n^2}\text{ eV}} \]

We have already established that \(TE=-KE\) .

Hence, the total energy of the electron in the \(n\)-th allowed orbit is

\[ \boxed{TE_n=-\frac{me^4}{8\epsilon_0^2h^2}\frac{Z^2}{n^2}} \] \[ \boxed{TE_n=-13.6\frac{Z^2}{n^2}\text{ eV}} \]

Similarly, since \(PE=-2KE=2TE\) , the potential energy of the electron in the \(n\)-th orbit is

\[ \boxed{PE_n=-\frac{me^4}{4\epsilon_0^2h^2}\frac{Z^2}{n^2}} \] \[ \boxed{PE_n=-27.2\frac{Z^2}{n^2}\text{ eV}} \]
