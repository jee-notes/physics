History of Atom

The journey from continuous to discrete.

What is Atom?

The first chapter of Physical Chemistry is about the "Mole Concept" — which is fundamentally another way of saying that matter is of particulate nature.

Matter may appear continuous at macroscopic scales, but at microscopic scales it is made up of discrete constituents called atoms and molecules.

Continuous vs Discrete:
Entire concept of atomic theory is essentially a fight between the continuous and the discrete.
Matter looks continuous to our senses, but is actually discrete in structure.
Feynman considered this one of the greatest discoveries produced by scientific intelligence.
Imagine going to a shopkeeper and asking for 1 L of water or 1 kg of water. This feels natural because water appears continuous.
But asking for "5 water" sounds ridiculous in everyday life, unlike asking for 5 apples.
The beauty of atomic theory is that, in principle, it is perfectly meaningful to ask for exactly 5 water molecules.
The issue is not conceptual validity, but practical measurability.

Dalton's Atomic Theory

In 1808, John Dalton proposed the atomic theory primarily to account for chemical changes and the laws of chemical combination.

Phenomena such as the law of multiple proportions strongly suggested that matter participates in reactions in fixed discrete ratios, and atomic theory provided a natural explanation for this behavior.

However, the chemists of that era were mainly concerned with masses and combining ratios rather than the actual physical dimensions of atoms.

Mass vs Size:
Early chemists like Dalton did not particularly care about the actual geometric size of atoms.
Their focus was mainly on relative masses and stoichiometric behavior.
For example, they could infer that an oxygen atom is roughly 16 times heavier than a hydrogen atom without knowing anything about the actual diameter of either atom.

The Physicist's Need for Physical Dimensions

Chemists were largely satisfied with relative masses, but physicists encountered a completely different problem.

The drive to determine the actual physical dimensions of atoms emerged from physics, particularly from thermodynamics and the kinetic theory of gases.

When Maxwell and Boltzmann developed the kinetic theory, gases were modeled as enormous collections of microscopic particles undergoing collisions.

To explain macroscopic phenomena like pressure, diffusion, thermal conductivity and viscosity, atoms could no longer remain merely philosophical entities or bookkeeping devices — they had to behave like actual physical projectiles with definite size.

We cannot calculate the mean free path of collisions without assigning a physical cross-sectional area to the particles themselves.

Loschmidt and the 10-10 m Scale

In 1865, Josef Loschmidt made one of the first mathematical estimations of molecular dimensions.

To estimate molecular size, physicists needed information about both the spacing between collisions and the actual volume occupied by molecules.

Loschmidt achieved this using a system of equations involving measurable macroscopic properties:

  • The Mean Free Path: Derived using transport phenomena such as viscosity and internal friction in gases.
  • The Condensation Volume Limit: The volume occupied when a gas condenses into a liquid, approximating the true physical volume occupied by tightly packed molecules.

By combining these ideas, Loschmidt estimated molecular diameters to be roughly on the order of \(10^{-10}\) meters.

This was an extraordinary achievement because it attempted to estimate the dimensions of completely invisible microscopic objects using only macroscopic laboratory measurements.

Scientific Developments against The Great Resistance

Even after estimates of atomic dimensions emerged, many prominent physicists still rejected the physical reality of atoms.

Scientists such as Ernst Mach argued that atoms were merely mathematical constructs rather than physically real objects because they could not be directly observed.

The idea that invisible \(10^{-10}\) m objects genuinely existed was considered philosophically unacceptable by many scientists of that era.

In 1897, J. J. Thomson discovered the electron and proposed the plum pudding model of the atom. This did not significantly alter the estimated size of atoms, but it established that atoms possessed internal structure and were not indivisible.

In 1905, Albert Einstein provided a quantitative theoretical explanation of Brownian motion. He showed that the observable random motion of pollen grains suspended in water could arise naturally from collisions with invisible water molecules.

This provided extremely strong evidence that atoms and molecules were physically real entities rather than merely mathematical abstractions.

Later experimental work by Jean Perrin further confirmed Einstein's predictions and helped establish broad scientific acceptance of atomic reality.

In 1911, Rutherford discovered the positively charged nucleus through the gold foil experiment, revealing that the nucleus had a size on the order of \(\sim 10^{-15}\) m.

This showed that most of the atom is actually empty space.

The previously estimated \(10^{-10}\) m atomic scale therefore did not represent a hard solid sphere, but rather the spatial extent of the electron cloud governed by electrostatic interactions.

In 1913, Max von Laue and the Bragg father-son duo pioneered X-ray crystallography.

By firing X-rays — whose wavelengths are themselves on the order of \(10^{-10}\) m — onto crystals, they used Bragg's Law to determine precise interatomic spacings within crystal lattices.

X-ray diffraction is considered as the final phenomenon to establish atomic scale distances.
However, X-ray diffraction is not the origin of the atomic scale, but rather the first technique capable of giving direct and precise measurements of distances between atomic centers.
Physicists knew about the existence of the \(10^{-10}\) m atomic scale and so they realized that X-rays would be suitable probes for atomic structure.
Successful diffraction requires the wavelength of the probing wave to be comparable to the size of the obstacle or spacing being investigated.
Till X-ray was not discovered, such distances cannot be established as experimentally verified.
This entire journey — from philosophical speculation to broad scientific acceptance — took nearly a century.

Transition from Classical to Modern Physics

By the beginning of the twentieth century, the existence of atoms had become firmly established through a series of independent experimental and theoretical developments. The question was no longer whether atoms existed, but how their microscopic behaviour could be described.

A major insight of modern physics was that we do not have to choose between a continuous macroscopic world and a discrete microscopic world. Both descriptions can be correct at their respective scales.

At the microscopic level, matter is composed of discrete atoms and molecules, and many physical quantities can take discrete, quantized values. At the macroscopic level, however, we deal with enormous numbers of microscopic particles, and their collective behaviour can appear smooth and continuous.

This macroscopic continuity is therefore not necessarily a fundamental property of the microscopic constituents. It can emerge statistically from the collective behaviour of a very large number of discrete microscopic constituents.

Microscopic vs Macroscopic:
Microscopic reality may be discrete.
Macroscopic behaviour may appear continuous.
The continuous behaviour of macroscopic systems can emerge from the statistical behaviour of enormous numbers of discrete microscopic particles.

This is one of the central ideas behind statistical physics. Quantities such as pressure, temperature and density describe the collective behaviour of huge numbers of particles rather than the behaviour of a single particle.

Classical physics therefore did not become completely meaningless when atoms were discovered. Instead, it became understood as an effective macroscopic description that emerges from deeper microscopic physics under appropriate conditions.

Modern physics then provided the microscopic framework needed to describe atoms and their constituents. Quantum mechanics introduced a fundamentally different description of microscopic systems, while statistical physics connected this microscopic description to the continuous macroscopic quantities of everyday experience.

The historical journey was therefore not simply a transition from continuous physics to discrete physics. It was the discovery that nature contains both levels of description: discrete microscopic constituents and continuous macroscopic behaviour emerging from them.

We did not reject the existence of atoms because classical physics could not explain them.
We changed our physics framework because the experimental evidence for atoms became impossible to ignore.
The atom remained; our description of nature changed.
The microscopic world required modern physics, while the familiar continuous macroscopic world could emerge statistically from that microscopic discreteness.
