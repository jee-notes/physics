Atomic Spectrum

The Bohr model successfully explained the atomic spectrum of hydrogen-like atoms.

Experimentally, hydrogen atoms do not absorb or emit radiation of arbitrary wavelengths.

The absorption spectrum contains only certain specific wavelengths, and the emission spectrum contains the same characteristic set of wavelengths.

Bohr explained this through the quantized internal structure of the atom.

Since the electron can exist only in certain stationary states, the atom can exchange only specific amounts of energy corresponding to transitions between these allowed states.

Therefore, only specific wavelengths of radiation can be absorbed or emitted.

Emission and Absorption Spectra

When an atomic gas is excited, for example by passing an electric discharge through it, its electrons absorb energy and move to higher allowed energy states.

These higher-energy states are unstable, and the electrons therefore return to lower allowed energy states.

During these downward transitions, the atoms emit electromagnetic radiation. Since the energy difference between the allowed states can take only certain discrete values, the emitted radiation consists of discrete, well-defined wavelengths.

This is known as the emission spectrum of the atom.

It appears as separated bright lines on a dark background.

Conversely, if continuous light containing a range of wavelengths is passed through a cool atomic gas, the atoms can absorb only those photons whose energies exactly match the differences between their allowed energy levels.

The transmitted light, when analyzed, therefore shows dark lines at the wavelengths that were absorbed. This is called the absorption spectrum of the atom.

The Fingerprint of Matter:

Because every element has its own characteristic set of quantized energy levels, it produces its own characteristic line spectrum.
Spectroscopy therefore allows scientists to identify the elemental composition of distant stars simply by analyzing the light they emit.

Rydberg Formula

The Rydberg formula was discovered experimentally before Bohr's postulates provided a theoretical explanation for the atomic spectrum.

For the emission spectrum of hydrogen, the experimentally observed wavelengths were found to satisfy:

\[ \frac{1}{\lambda}=R\left(\frac{1}{n_f^2}-\frac{1}{n_i^2}\right) \]

Here, \(n_i>n_f\), since the electron moves from a higher-energy state to a lower-energy state during emission.

For an absorption spectrum, the electron moves from a lower-energy state to a higher-energy state. Therefore, we simply reverse the order of the two quantum numbers:

\[ \frac{1}{\lambda}=R\left(\frac{1}{n_i^2}-\frac{1}{n_f^2}\right) \qquad n_f>n_i \]
  • \(R\): Rydberg constant. For hydrogen, \(R_H\approx1.097\times10^7\text{ m}^{-1}\).
  • \(n_i,n_f\): Principal quantum numbers of the initial and final states.

For a hydrogen-like single-electron species with atomic number \(Z\), the same formula becomes:

\[ \frac{1}{\lambda}=R_H Z^2\left(\frac{1}{n_f^2}-\frac{1}{n_i^2}\right) \]

Thus, the spectrum of hydrogen-like atoms follows the same pattern as hydrogen, with an additional factor of \(Z^2\).

Bohr's model later explained this experimentally discovered formula by showing that the energy of the allowed states varies as \(Z^2/n^2\), and the photon emitted or absorbed during a transition carries the corresponding energy difference.

Ground State

When an atomic gas is excited, for example by passing an electric discharge through it or by supplying radiation, its electrons absorb energy and move to higher energy states.

These higher-energy states are unstable, and the electrons therefore return to lower allowed energy states while emitting photons of corresponding wavelengths. This process is called de-excitation.

De-excitation occurs randomly and almost instantaneously. For example, if an electron is initially in \(n=3\), it may directly jump from \(n=3\) to \(n=1\), or it may first jump from \(n=3\) to \(n=2\) and then from \(n=2\) to \(n=1\).

The particular sequence of transitions is probabilistic, so different atoms can follow different de-excitation pathways.

However, once the electron reaches \(n=1\), it cannot de-excite any further because there is no lower allowed energy state available.

This state is known as the ground state. It is the lowest allowed energy state of the atom and therefore corresponds to the minimum energy of the atom.

Spectral Series of Hydrogen Atom

For the Hydrogen atom (\(Z=1\)), the spectral lines are empirically classified into different series based on the final orbit \( n_f \) to which the electron transitions.

  • Lyman Series (Ultraviolet Region): Transitions to the ground state.
    \( n_f = 1 \) and \( n_i = 2, 3, 4, \dots \)
  • Balmer Series (Visible Region): Transitions to the first excited state. This was the first series observed.
    \( n_f = 2 \) and \( n_i = 3, 4, 5, \dots \)
  • Paschen Series (Infrared Region):
    \( n_f = 3 \) and \( n_i = 4, 5, 6, \dots \)
  • Brackett Series (Infrared Region):
    \( n_f = 4 \) and \( n_i = 5, 6, 7, \dots \)
  • Pfund Series (Far Infrared Region):
    \( n_f = 5 \) and \( n_i = 6, 7, 8, \dots \)

Determining Longest and Shortest Wavelengths

In any given series characterized by \( n_f \):

  • The longest wavelength (minimum energy, minimum frequency) corresponds to the transition from the immediately adjacent level: \( n_i = n_f + 1 \). This is often called the first line of the series.
  • The shortest wavelength (series limit, maximum energy) corresponds to the transition from infinity: \( n_i = \infty \).

For example, in the Balmer series (\( n_f = 2 \)), the longest wavelength (H-\(\alpha\) line) is from \(n=3 \to 2\), and the series limit is from \(n=\infty \to 2\).

Energy Level Diagram

An energy level diagram represents the allowed energy states of an atom as horizontal lines drawn against an energy scale. For Hydrogen (\(Z=1\)):

  • Ground State (\( n=1 \)): \( E_1 = -13.6 \text{ eV} \)
  • First Excited State (\( n=2 \)): \( E_2 = -3.4 \text{ eV} \)
  • Second Excited State (\( n=3 \)): \( E_3 = -1.51 \text{ eV} \)
  • Third Excited State (\( n=4 \)): \( E_4 = -0.85 \text{ eV} \)
  • ...
  • Ionized State (\( n=\infty \)): \( E_\infty = 0 \text{ eV} \)

Important Note on Maximum Spectral Lines: If a sample of hydrogen gas is excited to the \(n\)-th state, the maximum possible number of distinct emission spectral lines observed when the electrons fall back to the ground state is given by the combination formula:

\[ \text{Number of lines} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2} \]

However, if only a single isolated atom is excited to state \(n\), the maximum number of photons it can emit as it cascades down to the ground state is \( (n-1) \).
