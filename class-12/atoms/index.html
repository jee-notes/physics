Atoms

The fundamental building blocks of matter and the birth of quantum physics.

Conceptual Overview

For centuries, the atom was considered a solid, indivisible sphere. However, as experimental techniques advanced in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, it became clear that the atom was a complex system with its own internal structure. This discovery marked the end of classical mechanics as the ultimate description of nature and heralded the birth of quantum mechanics.

The transition began with J.J. Thomson's discovery of the electron, which showed that the atom had constituent parts. It was furthered by Ernest Rutherford, whose scattering experiments revealed that the atom's mass and positive charge are concentrated in a tiny central nucleus. Yet, classical physics could not explain why electrons in such a system wouldn't spiral into the nucleus while radiating energy.

Niels Bohr solved this by introducing quantization — the idea that electrons can only exist in specific, stable orbits without radiating. While the Bohr model had its limits, it successfully explained the spectral lines of hydrogen and laid the groundwork for modern wave mechanics. In this chapter, we explore how these discoveries redefined our understanding of the microscopic world.

Alpha-Particle Scattering and the Nucleus

Rutherford's alpha-particle scattering experiment provided the first direct evidence for the atomic nucleus. By bombarding a thin gold foil with high-energy alpha particles, his team observed that while most particles passed through, some were deflected at large angles, and a few even bounced back.

This led to the nuclear model: the atom is mostly empty space with a dense, positively charged core. Key parameters for describing these interactions include:

  • Distance of Closest Approach: The minimum distance an alpha particle reaches before being repelled by the nucleus.
  • Impact Parameter: The perpendicular distance of the initial velocity vector of the alpha particle from the center of the nucleus.

Bohr's Postulates and Quantization

Bohr combined classical mechanics with early quantum concepts to stabilize the nuclear model. His model is built on three fundamental postulates:

1. Stable Orbits

Electrons revolve around the nucleus in certain stable, non-radiating orbits (stationary states).

2. Angular Momentum Quantization

The angular momentum of an electron in a stable orbit is an integral multiple of \(h/2\pi\):

\[ L = mvr = \frac{nh}{2\pi} \]

3. Energy Transitions

An electron can jump from a higher energy orbit to a lower one by emitting a photon of energy equal to the difference between the levels: \(h\nu = E_i - E_f\).

Atomic Energy Levels and Transitions

Using Bohr's postulates, we can derive the energy, radius, and velocity of an electron in a hydrogen-like atom (single electron system). The quantized energy levels are given by:

\[ E_n = -13.6 \frac{Z^2}{n^2} \text{ eV} \]

When electrons transition between these levels, they produce sharp spectral lines. This explains the various spectral series:

  • Lyman Series: Transitions to \(n=1\) (Ultraviolet).
  • Balmer Series: Transitions to \(n=2\) (Visible).
  • Paschen, Brackett, Pfund: Transitions to higher levels (Infrared).
