Atoms

The structure of Atoms.

Conceptual Overview

This chapter is a theory trap similar to the Dual Nature of Matter and Radiation chapter. Studying it for entrance exams is quite different from studying it for boards.

The historical development of atomic theory is relevant from the very beginning. In 1808, Dalton more or less formalised the idea that matter is composed of atoms and that atoms were indivisible. Different materials were believed to exist because they were made of different kinds of atoms.

Thus, the atom was initially considered to be a solid, indivisible sphere. The next major question was about the physical structure of this atom — if atoms were the basic constituents of matter, what were they actually like?

J.J. Thomson's discovery of the electron in 1897 showed that the atom was not indivisible and had constituent parts. This led to the plum-pudding model, in which electrons were embedded in a positively charged body.

Ernest Rutherford's scattering experiments in 1911 provided evidence for a tiny, dense nucleus at the centre of the atom. Almost all of the atom's mass and its positive charge were found to be concentrated in this small region, with most of the atom being empty space.

Scattering is a method already used in nature.
Bats use echolocation to "see" objects in the dark.
They emit sound waves, which are scattered by obstacles and detected back by the bat.

However, the nuclear model created a new problem. According to classical physics, an electron revolving around the nucleus should continuously radiate energy and gradually spiral into the nucleus. The atom should therefore be unstable.

In 1913, Niels Bohr addressed this problem by introducing quantization — the idea that electrons can exist only in certain allowed, stable states without radiating energy.

The Bohr model had important limitations, but it successfully explained the spectral lines of hydrogen and became an important step in the development of modern quantum mechanics.

Bohr Model

Students reading this chapter for the first time often get absorbed in the historical theory and miss the central idea of the chapter.

The main hero of this chapter is Bohr's model of the atom. At the time, two different experimental observations had to be explained.

Rutherford's scattering experiment had established the nuclear structure of the atom, but this nuclear model was unstable according to classical physics. Independently, experiments on hydrogen had shown that its emitted light consists of a discrete line spectrum, rather than a continuous spectrum.

These were two separate observations requiring explanation. Bohr's model was remarkable because it could account for both of them within a single model.

  • Atomic Stability: Bohr resolved the instability of Rutherford's nuclear model by proposing that electrons can exist in certain stable, non-radiating states.
  • Hydrogen Spectrum: Bohr explained the observed line spectrum of hydrogen by relating the emitted photons to transitions between allowed energy levels.

The fact that one model could successfully explain two independent experimental phenomena gave strong support to the Bohr model.

What makes the Bohr model particularly interesting is that it lies almost at the boundary between classical physics and quantum physics. The model continues to use many ideas from classical physics — such as the electron revolving around the nucleus, Newtonian mechanics, electrostatic force and the concept of orbital motion.

At the same time, it introduces a completely new idea from the emerging quantum theory: physical quantities associated with the electron can take only certain allowed, quantized values.

Thus, the Bohr model is neither purely classical nor fully quantum mechanical. It takes the familiar framework of classical physics and imposes a new quantum restriction on it.

This makes the model a natural bridge between the classical picture of the atom and the quantum picture that developed later.

Further developments in physics eventually showed that the Bohr model does not provide the true picture of the atom. The modern quantum-mechanical description differs from it in several fundamental ways.

Nevertheless, the Bohr model remains important because it demonstrates the transition from classical physics to quantum physics in a particularly simple and mathematically tractable form.

Nuclear Model

Rutherford's alpha-particle scattering experiment provided strong experimental evidence for the existence of a tiny atomic nucleus.

In the experiment, a beam of high-energy alpha particles was directed at a very thin gold foil.

Most of the alpha particles passed through the foil with little or no deflection. Some were deflected through relatively large angles, and a very small number were even scattered back towards the source.

These observations could not be explained if the positive charge and mass of the atom were spread uniformly throughout its volume, as proposed by Thomson's model.

Rutherford interpreted the scattering mathematically by studying how the deflection of an alpha particle depends on quantities such as its impact parameter and distance of closest approach.

  • Impact Parameter: The perpendicular distance between the initial line of motion of the alpha particle and the centre of the nucleus.
  • Distance of Closest Approach: The minimum distance reached by the alpha particle from the nucleus during its approach.

The mathematical analysis led to the conclusion that almost all of the atom's positive charge and mass must be concentrated in a very small, dense region at the centre. This region was called the nucleus.

The electron had already been established through Thomson's work. With the discovery of the nucleus, the nuclear model of the atom emerged naturally: a tiny, positively charged nucleus surrounded by electrons, with most of the atom being empty space.

However, this model created a new problem. According to classical physics, an electron revolving around the nucleus should continuously radiate energy and lose its orbital energy. It would therefore spiral into the nucleus, making the atom unstable.

But atoms are stable.

This conflict between the classical prediction and the observed stability of atoms was the conundrum that the Bohr model attempted to resolve.

Hydrogen Spectrum

The second major experimental observation relevant to the atom came from the study of the light emitted by different substances. This is usually introduced through the emission spectrum of hydrogen.

Hydrogen gas is sealed in a discharge tube at low pressure and an electric voltage is applied across it. The gas is excited and emits light. When this emitted light is passed through a prism or a diffraction grating, it does not produce all wavelengths continuously. Instead, distinct bright lines are observed at specific wavelengths.

This is called the line spectrum of hydrogen. Each line corresponds to a particular wavelength of emitted light. Thus, hydrogen does not emit light of arbitrary wavelengths; it emits only certain specific wavelengths.

This characteristic nature of emitted light is not unique to hydrogen. Different elements and compounds produce their own characteristic spectral patterns. A familiar example is the flame test, where certain elements produce characteristic flame colours. Spectroscopy provided a much more precise way of studying this phenomenon by measuring the wavelengths of the emitted light.

The important observation was therefore that the spectrum of an element contains information that is characteristic of that element. In the case of hydrogen, the wavelengths of the spectral lines follow definite patterns and can be measured experimentally.

This created another major problem for atomic theory. If electrons in an atom could have arbitrary energies, there would be no obvious reason for the atom to emit only certain specific wavelengths. Classical physics did not provide an explanation for the observed discrete line spectrum of hydrogen.

Thus, while Rutherford's experiment raised the problem of atomic stability, the study of hydrogen spectrum raised a separate problem: why does an atom emit only certain specific wavelengths of light?

The Bohr model provided an explanation for both problems within the same atomic model.

Bohr's Quantized Model

The Rutherford model established the nuclear structure of the atom, but classical physics could not explain the stability of such an atom. The hydrogen spectrum presented another independent problem: atoms emit only certain specific wavelengths of light.

Bohr proposed a new model that retained the basic classical picture of Rutherford — a small, positively charged nucleus with electrons moving around it — but introduced a fundamentally new idea from quantum physics: quantization.

Bohr did not propose a fundamentally different picture of the atom from Rutherford.
He introduced a quantitative structure to the Rutherford nuclear model by imposing quantum restrictions on the possible states of the electron.
In this sense, the Bohr model can be viewed as a quantized version of Rutherford's nuclear model.

According to Bohr, an electron cannot exist in an arbitrary state around the nucleus. Only certain allowed states are possible. These allowed states are called stationary states, and an electron in such a state does not continuously radiate energy.

Thus, the electron cannot exchange energy with the atom continuously. It can move from one allowed state to another, with the energy difference being emitted or absorbed as electromagnetic radiation.

In this way, Bohr introduced quantum restrictions into the classical nuclear model and obtained a framework that could address both the stability of the atom and the discrete hydrogen spectrum.

Atomic Energy Levels and Transitions

The central idea of the Bohr model is that the atom does not have arbitrary possible energies. Its electron can exist only in certain allowed energy states, and therefore the atom has discrete energy levels.

This is the main crux of the Bohr model.
Consider an atom as a system consisting of a positive nucleus and a negative electron having some energy.
The atom can exchange energy with its surroundings — it can absorb energy as well as release energy. But the energy of the atom cannot change by an arbitrary continuous amount. Only certain discrete energy changes are allowed.

When the atom changes from one allowed energy state to another, the corresponding energy difference is exchanged with the surroundings in the form of electromagnetic radiation.

This provides the physical explanation for the line spectrum of hydrogen. Since only certain energy changes are possible, only certain photon energies — and therefore only certain wavelengths — can be emitted or absorbed.

The different groups of spectral lines observed in hydrogen are organised into spectral series, such as the Lyman, Balmer, Paschen, Brackett and Pfund series.

The quantitative energy levels of the atom and the resulting spectral series can then be derived from the Bohr model.
