Early Atomic Models

Discovery of the dense nucleus.

Modern Physics Experiments

The experiments studied in modern physics are generally not related to ordinary day-to-day human-scale observations.

Unlike mechanics or thermodynamics, modern physics deals with the deeper structural nature of matter and radiation.

At the macroscopic level, we do not directly interact with individual atoms, electrons, molecules or photons. Matter appears continuous and smooth to our senses and our conventional measuring instruments.

Yet, its the microscopic constituents hidden beneath the macroscopic appearance that ultimately determine the behavior and properties of materials around us.

Modern chemistry is considered to be one of the greatest applications of modern physics.
Modern chemistry explains the different physical and chemical properties of different materials, through atomic structure.
For example, tungsten has the highest melting point among metals because of its extremely strong metallic bonding and electronic structure. Certain metals like platinum act as excellent catalysts because of their surface adsorption properties and electronic interactions with reacting molecules.
In organic chemistry and biochemistry, even the three-dimensional shape of molecules determines how they participate in enzymatic and biological reactions.
All these material properties ultimately arise from the atomic (or molecular) structure.
The principles responsible for these explanations are rooted in the concepts developed through modern physics experiments.

Nature of Experiments:

The primary objective of these modern physics experiments was to uncover the deep structural nature of matter and radiation.

  • Condensed matter such as solids and liquids do not reveal the microscopic structural behavior because of their high density interactions. Even dense gases are less useful in that sense.
  • Scientists thus performed experiments involving rarefied gases where particles could move more freely and individual effects became easier to observe.
  • Gas discharge tube experiments became common during this period. Different gases and metallic electrodes were studied under high voltage conditions, leading to the observation of various emissions and radiations.
  • Matter was bombarded using different forms of energy such as light energy, thermal energy, electrical energy and electromagnetic radiation to study how microscopic constituents responded.
  • Matter was also bombarded by other particles possessing kinetic energy and momentum. Scattering experiments later became one of the most powerful tools for probing internal structure.
  • The natural radioactivity of matter was also intensely investigated. In fact, Rutherford discovered the atomic nucleus while studying the scattering behavior of radioactive alpha particles.

Plum Pudding Model

J. J. Thomson discovered the electron and proposed the first major physical model of the atom.

The theory behind Thomson's experiment is covered in physical chemistry. In physics, we mainly cover the implications.

As per Thomson's model, the atom was a sphere of uniformly distributed positive electric charge having a radius of roughly \(10^{-10}\text{ m}\). Negatively charged electrons were embedded inside this positive sphere like "plums in a pudding" or seeds embedded inside a watermelon.

Thomson also determined the value of the charge-to-mass ratio \(\left(\frac{e}{m}\right)\) of the electron using cathode ray experiments.

Later, Millikan determined the elementary charge \(e\) through the oil drop experiment.

Combining both results gave complete information about the electron:

Charge: \( e = -1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C} \)

Mass: \( m = 9.1 \times 10^{-31}\text{ kg} \)

Thomson's model successfully explained why atoms are electrically neutral overall.
It also qualitatively supported phenomena such as thermionic emission and photoelectric emission because electrons were treated as the "mobile constituents" of atom (conduction electrons).
However, the model failed to explain Rutherford's gold foil experiment involving the scattering of alpha particles. If positive charge were truly spread uniformly throughout the atom, alpha particles should experience only weak distributed deflections rather than sudden large-angle scattering.
The model also failed to explain the discrete spectral lines observed in hydrogen and other atomic spectra.

Lenard's Dynamides (1903)

Before the gold foil experiment disproved Thomson's solid sphere of positive charge, experimental physicist Philipp Lenard identified a profound structural anomaly using cathode rays.

Lenard noted that fast-moving cathode rays (electrons) could pass through thin sheets of materials, such as aluminum foil, almost entirely undeviated and unabsorbed.

If atoms were solid, continuous spheres of matter and charge, the majority of the incident electrons would collide with them and fail to propagate forward. Because the rays passed straight through, Lenard suggested in 1903 that the atom must consist almost entirely of empty space.

To account for atomic mass and charge neutrality, Lenard proposed that matter was composed of highly localized, discrete neutral pairs called dynamides. Each dynamide consisted of an electron bound to an immensely heavy, tiny positively charged particle. But then, this failed to answer, why on heating a metal, these tiny positively charged particles were not ejected ?

The Conceptual Bridge to Rutherford:

Lenard's model correctly deduced the absolute necessity of empty space, but his distributed "dynamides" struggled to explain overall stability. Ernest Rutherford ultimately synthesized these realities.

He retained Lenard's empty space, but anchored 100% of the positive charge and mass into one singular, ultra-dense central point: the nucleus.

This central anchor explains both - why cathode rays pass straight through the surrounding void, and why thermal heating only boils off the lightweight outer conduction electrons.

Rutherford's Experiment

In 1911, H. Geiger and E. Marsden, under the direction of Ernest Rutherford, performed an experiment where they bombarded a very thin gold foil (thickness \( \approx 2.1 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m} \)) with a beam of high-energy \( \alpha \)-particles (\( \approx 5.5\text{ MeV} \)) emitted from a radioactive source (like Bismuth-214).

\(5.5\text{ MeV}\) is extremely high Kinetic Energy
An \( \alpha \)-particle has a charge of \(+2e\) .
The accelerating voltage is around \(2.75\text{ MV}\) or 2.75 million volts.

Scattering means when a particle travelling in a straight line gets deflected by an angle with the original trajectory. The angle of deflection is determined similar to angle of deviation in ray optics.

Key Observations:

  • Most \( \alpha \)-particles passed through the gold foil undeflected, indicating that most of the space inside the atom is empty.
  • Only about 0.14% of the incident \( \alpha \)-particles scattered by more than \( 1^\circ \). (0.14% is about 7 in 5000)
  • A very small fraction (about 1 in 8000) suffered a deflection of more than \( 90^\circ \), and some even bounced back (\( 180^\circ \)).
Rutherford's astonishment:
Rutherford described the \( 180^\circ \) scattering as being "almost as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you".
This massive deflection is impossible if the positive charge is spread out as in Thomson's model.

Rutherford's Nuclear Model

To explain these massive deflections, Rutherford proposed a new atomic model:

  1. The entire positive charge and almost all the mass of the atom are concentrated in a tiny central region called the nucleus. Its size is on the order of \( 10^{-15}\text{ m} \text{ to } 10^{-14}\text{ m} \), whereas the size of the whole atom is about \( 10^{-10}\text{ m} \).
  2. The negatively charged electrons revolve around the nucleus in circular orbits, much like planets revolving around the sun. The electrostatic force of attraction between the nucleus and the electron provides the necessary centripetal force.
  3. The atom as a whole is electrically neutral, leading to zero net charge.

Drawbacks of Rutherford's Model:

According to classical electromagnetic theory, an accelerating charged particle continuously radiates electromagnetic energy.

An electron revolving in a circular orbit revolves with continuously changing velocity (thus accelerating).

Therefore, it should emit radiation, lose energy, and spiral into the nucleus within \( \approx 10^{-8} \text{ s} \).

The Size of Nucleus

The scattering experiment also provides an estimate of the size of the nucleus. Consider an \( \alpha \)-particle of mass \(m\) and initial velocity \(v\) projected directly towards the nucleus. This is a head-on collision, for which the impact parameter is \(b=0\).

As the positively charged \( \alpha \)-particle approaches the positively charged nucleus, it experiences electrostatic repulsion and slows down. At a certain distance \(r_0\), its velocity becomes momentarily zero before it reverses its direction and moves back. This distance is called the distance of closest approach.

Notice that this situation occurs for the \( \alpha \)-particle that is scattered through \(180^\circ\). Only in this case does the particle reach a state of momentary rest and therefore have a clearly defined turning point.

The distance of closest approach also gives an upper limit for the size of the nucleus. If the nucleus were larger than \(r_0\), the \( \alpha \)-particle would have encountered the nuclear region before reaching the calculated turning point.

Since the particle can be described by Coulomb scattering down to \(r_0\), the nuclear radius must be smaller than this distance.

Distance of Closest Approach (\(r_0\)):

We use conservation of mechanical energy. The electrostatic force is conservative, so the total mechanical energy of the \( \alpha \)-particle–nucleus system remains constant.

In the usual approximation that the massive gold nucleus remains stationary, the initial kinetic energy of the \( \alpha \)-particle is completely converted into electrostatic potential energy at the point of closest approach.

\[ K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f \] \[ \frac{1}{2}mv^2 + 0=0 + \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{(2e)(Ze)}{r_0} \] \[ r_0=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{2Ze^2}{K} \]

Where:

  • \( Z \): Atomic number of the target nucleus (for gold, \(Z=79\)).
  • \( e \): Elementary charge (\(1.6\times10^{-19}\text{ C}\)).
  • \( K \): Initial kinetic energy of the \( \alpha \)-particle (\(\frac12mv^2\)).

Impact Parameter

The impact parameter \(b\) is the perpendicular distance between the initial line of motion of an \( \alpha \)-particle and the centre of the target nucleus, when the particle is sufficiently far from the nucleus that the interaction has not yet significantly changed its trajectory.

The scattering angle \( \theta \) of an \( \alpha \)-particle depends strongly on its impact parameter. A small impact parameter means that the particle passes close to the nucleus and therefore experiences a large electrostatic repulsion, producing a large scattering angle. A large impact parameter means that the particle passes farther from the nucleus and undergoes only a small deflection.

Since the repulsive interaction between the \( \alpha \)-particle and the nucleus is a central \(1/r^2\) Coulomb force, the resulting trajectory can be shown to be a hyperbola.

Impact Parameter (\(b\)) Formula:

\[ b=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{Ze^2\cot(\theta/2)}{K} \]

Where:

  • \( \theta \): Scattering angle of the \( \alpha \)-particle.
  • \( K \): Initial kinetic energy of the \( \alpha \)-particle.
  • \( Z \): Atomic number of the target nucleus.

Key Takeaways:

  • If \(b=0\), then \(\cot(\theta/2)=0\), giving \(\theta=180^\circ\). This corresponds to a head-on collision in which the \( \alpha \)-particle is scattered directly backwards.
  • For large \(b\), the \( \alpha \)-particle passes far from the nucleus and \( \theta\) becomes very small.
  • The Rutherford scattering law predicts that the scattering probability varies as \(\displaystyle \frac{1}{\sin^4(\theta/2)}\).

Rutherford obtained the scattering law \[ N(\theta)\propto\frac{1}{\sin^4(\theta/2)} \] where the number of scattered particles decreases extremely rapidly as the scattering angle increases.

The basic idea behind the derivation is to relate the number of particles having a particular range of impact parameters to the corresponding range of scattering angles.

Consider the incident beam as a collection of particles passing the nucleus with different impact parameters. The particles whose impact parameters lie between \(b\) and \(b+db\) occupy an annular area \(dA=2\pi b\,db\) . The impact-parameter relation gives the corresponding scattering angle \( \theta \), allowing the distribution of particles in \(b\)-space to be converted into a distribution in scattering angle.

The Rutherford scattering formula is derived under the assumption that the gold foil is sufficiently thin and the incident \( \alpha \)-particles have sufficiently high kinetic energy. Under these conditions, the dominant contribution to the observed deflection can be treated as scattering from a single nucleus, rather than multiple successive nuclear scatterings.

The incident beam is also taken to be well collimated and approximately monoenergetic, so that the \( \alpha \)-particles have essentially the same initial kinetic energy \(K\). The impact parameter \(b\), however, is different for different particles across the beam.
