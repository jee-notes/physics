Early Atomic Models

The journey from a uniform sphere of positive charge to the discovery of the dense nucleus.

Modern Physics Experiments

The experiments studied in modern physics are generally not related to ordinary day-to-day human-scale observations.

Unlike mechanics or thermodynamics, modern physics deals with the deeper structural nature of matter and radiation.

At the macroscopic level, we do not directly interact with individual atoms, electrons, molecules, or photons. Matter appears continuous and smooth to our senses.

Yet, the microscopic constituents hidden beneath this macroscopic appearance ultimately determine the behavior and properties of all materials around us.

Modern Chemistry:
Modern chemistry is one of the greatest applications of modern physics.
Modern chemistry explains why different materials possess different physical and chemical properties.
For example, tungsten has the highest melting point among metals because of its extremely strong metallic bonding and electronic structure.
Certain metals like platinum act as excellent catalysts because of their surface adsorption properties and electronic interactions with reacting molecules.
In organic chemistry and biochemistry, even the three-dimensional shape of molecules determines how they participate in enzymatic and biological reactions.
All these material properties ultimately arise from microscopic atomic and electronic structure.
The principles responsible for these explanations are rooted in the concepts developed through modern physics experiments.

Nature of Experiments:

  • The primary objective of these experiments was to uncover the deep structural nature of matter and radiation.
  • Condensed matter such as solids and liquids often do not easily reveal microscopic structural effects because of their complexity and dense interactions. Even dense gases provide limited clarity.
  • As a result, scientists increasingly turned toward experiments involving rarefied gases where particles could move more freely and individual effects became easier to observe.
  • Gas discharge tube experiments became extremely important during this period. Different gases and metallic electrodes were studied under high voltage conditions, leading to the observation of various emissions and radiations.
  • Matter was bombarded using different forms of energy such as light energy, thermal energy, electrical energy, and electromagnetic radiation to study how microscopic constituents responded.
  • Matter was also bombarded by other particles possessing mechanical kinetic energy. Scattering experiments later became one of the most powerful tools for probing internal structure.
  • The natural radioactivity of matter was also intensely investigated. In fact, Rutherford discovered the atomic nucleus while studying the scattering behavior of radioactive alpha particles.

Thomson's Plum Pudding Model

After discovering the electron in 1897, J. J. Thomson proposed the first major physical model of the atom.

Before Thomson's work, atoms were generally treated as indivisible entities in chemistry. The discovery of the electron established that atoms themselves possess internal structure.

According to Thomson's model, the atom was imagined as a sphere of uniformly distributed positive electric charge having a radius of roughly \(10^{-10}\text{ m}\).

Negatively charged electrons were embedded inside this positively charged sphere like "plums in a pudding" or seeds embedded inside a watermelon.

Thomson himself determined the value of the charge-to-mass ratio \(\left(\frac{e}{m}\right)\) of the electron using cathode ray experiments.

Later, Millikan determined the elementary charge \(e\) through the oil drop experiment.

Combining both results gave complete information about the electron:

Charge: \( e = -1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C} \)

Mass: \( m = 9.1 \times 10^{-31}\text{ kg} \)

Success and Failure:
Thomson's model successfully explained why atoms are electrically neutral overall.
It also qualitatively supported phenomena such as thermionic emission and photoelectric emission because electrons were treated as mobile constituents inside matter.
However, the model completely failed when Rutherford's group later observed large-angle scattering of alpha particles in the gold foil experiment.
If positive charge were truly spread uniformly throughout the atom, alpha particles should experience only weak distributed deflections rather than sudden large-angle scattering.
The model also failed to explain the discrete spectral lines observed in hydrogen and other atomic spectra.

Rutherford's Alpha-Particle Scattering Experiment

In 1911, H. Geiger and E. Marsden, under the direction of Ernest Rutherford, performed an experiment where they bombarded a very thin gold foil (thickness \( \approx 2.1 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m} \)) with a beam of high-energy \( \alpha \)-particles (\( \approx 5.5\text{ MeV} \)) emitted from a radioactive source (like Bismuth-214).

Scattering means when a particle travelling in a straight line gets deflected by an angle with the original trajectory.

Key Observations:

  • Most \( \alpha \)-particles passed through the gold foil undeflected, indicating that most of the space inside the atom is empty.
  • Only about 0.14% of the incident \( \alpha \)-particles scattered by more than \( 1^\circ \).
  • A very small fraction (about 1 in 8000) suffered a deflection of more than \( 90^\circ \), and some even bounced back (\( 180^\circ \)).
Rutherford's astonishment:
Rutherford described the \( 180^\circ \) scattering as being "almost as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you".
This massive deflection is impossible if the positive charge is spread out as in Thomson's model.

Rutherford's Nuclear Model

To explain these massive deflections, Rutherford proposed a new atomic model:

  1. The entire positive charge and almost all the mass of the atom are concentrated in a tiny central region called the nucleus. Its size is on the order of \( 10^{-15}\text{ m} \text{ to } 10^{-14}\text{ m} \), whereas the size of the whole atom is about \( 10^{-10}\text{ m} \).
  2. The negatively charged electrons revolve around the nucleus in circular orbits, much like planets revolving around the sun. The electrostatic force of attraction between the nucleus and the electron provides the necessary centripetal force.
  3. The atom as a whole is electrically neutral, leading to zero net charge.

Drawbacks of Rutherford's Model:

According to classical electromagnetic theory, an accelerating charged particle continuously radiates electromagnetic energy.

An electron revolving in a circular orbit revolves with continuously changing velocity (thus accelerating).

Therefore, it should emit radiation, lose energy, and spiral into the nucleus within \( \approx 10^{-8} \text{ s} \).

This model completely failed to explain the stability of the atom and the discrete line spectra shown by gases.

Distance of Closest Approach

When an \( \alpha \)-particle of mass \( m \) and initial velocity \( v \) is projected directly toward the nucleus (head-on collision, Impact Parameter \( b=0 \)), it slows down as it approaches the nucleus due to electrostatic repulsion. At a certain distance \( r_0 \), it stops momentarily, its kinetic energy completely converting into electrostatic potential energy. This distance \( r_0 \) is the distance of closest approach and provides an upper limit for the size of the nucleus.

Distance of Closest Approach (\(r_0\)):

\[ K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f \] \[ \frac{1}{2}mv^2 + 0 = 0 + \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{(2e)(Ze)}{r_0} \] \[ r_0 = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{2Ze^2}{K} \]

Where:

  • \( Z \): Atomic number of the target nucleus (For gold, \( Z=79 \)).
  • \( e \): Elementary charge (\( 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C} \)).
  • \( K \): Initial kinetic energy of the \( \alpha \)-particle (\( \frac{1}{2}mv^2 \)).

Impact Parameter

The impact parameter \( b \) is defined as the perpendicular distance of the initial velocity vector of the \( \alpha \)-particle from the central axis of the target nucleus when the particle is far away.

The scattering angle \( \theta \) of an \( \alpha \)-particle depends heavily on its impact parameter. Because the repulsive force is a central \( 1/r^2 \) force (Coulomb force), it can be shown that the trajectory is a hyperbola.

Impact Parameter (\(b\)) Formula:

\[ b = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Ze^2 \cot(\theta/2)}{K} \]

Where:

  • \( \theta \): Angle of scattering.
  • \( K \): Initial kinetic energy of the \( \alpha \)-particle.

Key Takeaways:

  • If \( b = 0 \), then \( \cot(\theta/2) = 0 \implies \theta = 180^\circ \) (head-on collision, bounces back).
  • For large \( b \), \( \theta \) is nearly \( 0^\circ \) (particle passes with negligible deflection).
  • The number of alpha particles scattered at an angle \( \theta \) is inversely proportional to \( \sin^4(\theta/2) \), specifically \( N(\theta) \propto \frac{1}{\sin^4(\theta/2)} \).
