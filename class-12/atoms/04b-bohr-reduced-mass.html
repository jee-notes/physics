Bohr Energy

Energy Levels in Bohr Model.

Third Postulate

The third postulate is the main operational postulate of Bohr's model. The first two postulates establish the allowed structure of the atom; the third describes how this quantized atom actually interacts with its surroundings through energy exchange.

We can think of a single atom as a system with which we can interact by supplying or extracting energy. In the context of atomic physics, this interaction is primarily observed through photons.

If an atom absorbs an incident photon, the atom gains energy. If an atom emits a photon, the atom loses energy.

The crucial point is that, because the atom has only discrete allowed energy states, its energy cannot change by an arbitrary amount. Energy exchange occurs through transitions between these allowed states.

When a photon is absorbed, the electron moves from a lower-energy state \(E_i\) to a higher-energy state \(E_f\). The energy of the absorbed photon is

\[ h\nu=\frac{hc}{\lambda}=E_f-E_i \]

When a photon is emitted, the electron moves from a higher-energy state \(E_i\) to a lower-energy state \(E_f\). The energy of the emitted photon is

\[ h\nu=\frac{hc}{\lambda}=E_i-E_f \]

Thus, the mechanism of atomic energy interaction is a transition between allowed energy states.

In the Bohr picture, these transitions correspond to jumps between the corresponding stationary orbits.

The quantized nature of the atom's interaction with its surroundings is therefore a direct consequence of its quantized internal structure: the electron is restricted to certain stationary orbits, and the atom can exchange only the energy differences between those allowed states.

Our direct experimental interaction with an atom is essentially through the energy it absorbs or emits.
We cannot directly observe an electron sitting in a particular stationary orbit.
Instead, we probe the atom through its interaction with energy, especially through absorption and emission of photons.
The third postulate therefore connects the internal quantized structure of the atom to its observable experimental behaviour.

The transition itself takes place over a very short time, typically on the order of \(10^{-9}\,\text{s}\) for many allowed atomic transitions.

Energy Levels in Hydrogen Atom

The total energy of the electron in the \( n \)-th orbit is the sum of its kinetic energy (due to its motion) and its electrostatic potential energy (due to the nucleus).

  • Kinetic Energy (\(K_n\)): \( K_n = \frac{1}{2}mv_n^2 = \frac{1}{8\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{Ze^2}{r_n} \)
  • Potential Energy (\(U_n\)): \( U_n = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{(Ze)(-e)}{r_n} = -\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{Ze^2}{r_n} \)

Total Energy \( E_n = K_n + U_n = -\frac{1}{8\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{Ze^2}{r_n} \). Notice that \( E_n = -K_n \) and \( U_n = 2E_n \).

Substituting the expression for \( r_n \), we get the energy quantization formula:

Total Energy in \(n\)-th orbit (\(E_n\)):

\[ E_n = -\frac{m e^4}{8\epsilon_0^2 h^2} \cdot \frac{Z^2}{n^2} \] \[ E_n = -13.6 \frac{Z^2}{n^2} \text{ eV} \]

The negative sign in the total energy indicates that the electron is bound to the nucleus. If energy is supplied such that the total energy becomes positive or zero, the electron will escape the atom (ionization).

Correction for Nuclear Motion (Reduced Mass)

In deriving the above formulas, we assumed the nucleus is infinitely heavy and remains stationary while the electron revolves around it. In reality, both the nucleus (mass \( M \)) and the electron (mass \( m \)) revolve around their common center of mass.

To account for this, we replace the mass of the electron \( m \) with the reduced mass \( \mu \) of the system:

\[ \mu = \frac{m \cdot M}{m + M} \]

Since the nucleus is much heavier than the electron (\( M \gg m \)), \( \mu \approx m \), which is why the previous formulas hold well. However, for precision spectroscopy or for positronium (an electron and a positron revolving around each other, where \( M = m \)), the reduced mass must be used.

Corrected Rydberg Energy: Using reduced mass \( \mu \), the modified energy levels are:

\[ E_n' = \left(\frac{\mu}{m}\right) E_n \]
