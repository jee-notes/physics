Nuclear Density

Super Dense Nucleus.

Experimental Determination

To resolve a length scale of the order of \(10^{-15}\text{ m}\), the de Broglie wavelength of the probing particle must satisfy the diffraction resolution criterion:

\[ \lambda = \frac{h}{p} \lesssim R \]

If we attempt to use alpha particles (as in Rutherford's scattering experiments), the distance of closest approach for a head-on collision is affected by Coulomb repulsion.

Modern precision measurements therefore use ultra-relativistic electron scattering (e.g., Hofstadter's experiments). Electrons interact purely through the electromagnetic force (governed strictly by quantum electrodynamics), acting as clean, non-destructive point-like probes of the nuclear charge distribution.

\[ E \approx pc = \frac{hc}{\lambda} \gtrsim \frac{1240\text{ MeV}\cdot\text{fm}}{1\text{ fm}} \sim 100\text{--}500\text{ MeV} \]

These electron-scattering diffraction patterns reveal that the nuclear volume \(V\) is directly proportional to the total number of nucleons \(A\).

\[ V \propto A \]

Radius Scaling Law: \(R = R_0 A^{1/3}\)

Treating the nucleus as an approximately spherical distribution of mass and charge with radius \(R\):

\[ V = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \]

Because the total volume increases linearly with the mass number \(A\):

\[ V \propto A \implies \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \propto A \implies R^3 \propto A \]

Taking the cube root yields the empirical nuclear radius relation:

\[ \boxed{R = R_0 A^{1/3}} \]

where \(R_0\) is an experimentally determined constant:

\[ \boxed{R_0 \approx 1.2\text{ fm} = 1.2 \times 10^{-15}\text{ m}} \]

Order of Magnitude Scaling:

  • For Carbon-12 (\(A = 12\)): \(R \approx 1.2 \times (12)^{1/3} \approx 2.7\text{ fm}\)
  • For Iron-56 (\(A = 56\)): \(R \approx 1.2 \times (56)^{1/3} \approx 4.6\text{ fm}\)
  • For Uranium-238 (\(A = 238\)): \(R \approx 1.2 \times (238)^{1/3} \approx 7.4\text{ fm}\)

A twentyfold increase in nucleon count (\(A = 12 \rightarrow 238\)) increases the physical radius by a factor of only \((238/12)^{1/3} \approx 2.7\).

Nuclear Density

Nuclear matter density \(\rho_{\text{nuc}}\) is defined as the total mass of the nucleus divided by its spatial volume:

\[ \rho_{\text{nuc}} = \frac{M_{\text{nucleus}}}{V_{\text{nucleus}}} \]

Let \(m_u\) be the average rest mass of a bound nucleon (\(m_u \approx 1\text{ u} \approx 1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}\)). The total nuclear mass is:

\[ M_{\text{nucleus}} \approx A \cdot m_u \]

Assuming spherical geometry:

\[ V_{\text{nucleus}} = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 = \frac{4}{3}\pi \left(R_0 A^{1/3}\right)^3 = \frac{4}{3}\pi R_0^3 A \]

Substituting \(M_{\text{nucleus}}\) and \(V_{\text{nucleus}}\) into the density definition:

\[ \rho_{\text{nuc}} = \frac{A \cdot m_u}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R_0^3 A} = \frac{m_u}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R_0^3} \]

Notice that the mass number \(A\) cancels completely:

\[ \boxed{\rho_{\text{nuc}} = \frac{3 m_u}{4\pi R_0^3} = \text{constant}} \]

Substituting numerical values:

\[ \rho_{\text{nuc}} = \frac{3 \times (1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg})}{4\pi \times (1.2 \times 10^{-15}\text{ m})^3} \approx 2.29 \times 10^{17}\text{ kg/m}^3 \] \[ \boxed{\rho_{\text{nuc}} \approx 2.3 \times 10^{17}\text{ kg/m}^3 = 2.3 \times 10^{14}\text{ g/cm}^3} \]
Physical Meaning of \(A\)-Independence:
Unlike macroscopic planetary bodies or atomic electron clouds, where density varies widely across species, all nuclei have approximately the same internal mass density.
Whether it is a light nucleus like \({}^{4}_{2}\text{He}\) or a heavy nucleus like \({}^{238}_{92}\text{U}\), the concentration of nucleons per unit volume remains invariant: \[ n = \frac{A}{V} = \frac{3}{4\pi R_0^3} \approx 0.14\text{ nucleons/fm}^3 \]

What Does \(V/A=\text{constant}\) Tell Us?

The experimentally observed relation

\[ \boxed{V\propto A} \]

can be rewritten as

\[ \boxed{\frac VA=\text{constant}} \]

This means that each nucleon occupies approximately the same amount of nuclear volume. As we add more nucleons, the nucleus therefore grows in size rather than continuously becoming denser.

In other words, nuclear matter has an approximately fixed equilibrium density. The nucleus does not become progressively more "squeezed" as \(A\) increases.

Think of putting more and more balloons into a chamber.
If the balloons could be squeezed more and more as their number increased, the volume per balloon would keep decreasing: \[ \frac VA\downarrow \] But for nuclei, \[ \boxed{\frac VA\approx\text{constant}} \] So adding nucleons mainly means adding more nuclear volume, rather than continuously compressing the existing nuclear matter.

This tells us something important about the physical interaction between nucleons. The nucleus behaves as a saturating system: there is a characteristic nuclear density beyond which adding more nucleons does not simply keep compressing the existing matter.

This observation will become even more meaningful when we look at the binding energy per nucleon.
