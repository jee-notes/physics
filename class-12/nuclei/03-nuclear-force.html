Nuclear Force

The strongest force in nature

Introduction to Nuclear Force

Inside a tiny nucleus, multiple protons are packed very closely together. According to Coulomb's law, there should be tremendous electrostatic repulsion between these positively charged protons, tearing the nucleus apart. However, most nuclei are extremely stable. This implies the existence of another force, much stronger than the electrostatic force, which holds the nucleons (protons and neutrons) together. This force is called the strong nuclear force.

Properties of Nuclear Force

The strong nuclear force has several unique characteristics that distinguish it from gravitational and electromagnetic forces:

  • Strongest force in nature: At short distances, it is about 100 times stronger than the electromagnetic force and roughly \(10^{38}\) times stronger than gravity.
  • Short-range force: It operates only over extremely short distances, on the order of a few femtometers (\(1 \text{ fm} = 10^{-15} \text{ m}\)). Its strength rapidly decreases to zero at distances beyond \(2-3 \text{ fm}\).
  • Charge independent: The strong nuclear force between a pair of protons (\(p-p\)), a pair of neutrons (\(n-n\)), or a proton and a neutron (\(p-n\)) is approximately the same, provided their separation state is the same. It does not depend on the electric charge of the particles.
  • Saturation property: The nuclear force exhibits saturation, meaning a nucleon only interacts with its nearest neighbors, not with all the nucleons in the nucleus. This is why the binding energy per nucleon is roughly constant for a large range of mass numbers.
  • Spin dependent: The force is stronger if the spins of the interacting nucleons are parallel compared to when they are anti-parallel.
  • Non-central and exchange force: Unlike gravity or electrostatic force, the nuclear force does not merely act along the line joining the centers of the nucleons. It is also believed to be mediated by the exchange of particles called mesons (like pions), as proposed by Hideki Yukawa.

Potential Energy between Nucleons

The relationship between the strong nuclear force and the distance between nucleons can be best understood by plotting the potential energy (\(U\)) of a pair of nucleons as a function of their separation distance (\(r\)).

The force \(F\) between the nucleons is related to the potential energy by the equation:

\[ F = -\frac{dU}{dr} \]

Key features of the Potential Energy curve:

  • Minimum Potential Energy (\(r = r_0\)): At a separation of \(r_0 \approx 0.8 \text{ fm}\), the potential energy is at its minimum (most negative). Here, the strong nuclear force is exactly zero. This represents the equilibrium position.
  • Attractive Region (\(r > r_0\)): For distances greater than \(0.8 \text{ fm}\) but less than about \(3 \text{ fm}\), the potential energy is negative and increases towards zero. Since the slope \(dU/dr\) is positive, the force \(F = -dU/dr\) is negative, indicating an attractive force. This force holds the nucleus together.
  • Repulsive Core (\(r < r_0\)): For distances less than \(0.8 \text{ fm}\), the potential energy curve rises steeply and becomes positive. The slope \(dU/dr\) is negative, making the force \(F\) positive, indicating a very strong repulsive force. This hard repulsive core prevents the nucleus from collapsing in on itself and explains the constant density of nuclei.

The balance between the short-range attractive nuclear force and the even shorter-range repulsive core is what maintains the stable volume and density of atomic nuclei.
