Radioactive Decay Law

The mathematics of nuclear disintegration

Rutherford-Soddy Law of Radioactive Decay

Radioactive decay is a statistical process. It is impossible to predict when a specific individual nucleus will decay. However, for a large sample containing many identical nuclei, the overall rate of decay follows a precise macroscopic law established by Rutherford and Soddy.

The Law of Radioactive Decay states: The rate of disintegration (decay) of a radioactive sample at any instant is directly proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei present in the sample at that instant.

Mathematically, if \(N\) is the number of active nuclei at time \(t\), the decay rate is \(-dN/dt\):

\[ -\frac{dN}{dt} \propto N \]

\[ \frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N \]

Integrating this gives the exponential decay law:

\[ N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t} \]

Where:

  • \(N(t)\) = Number of undecayed nuclei at time \(t\)
  • \(N_0\) = Initial number of nuclei at \(t = 0\)
  • \(\lambda\) = Decay constant (or disintegration constant), specific to the isotope. Unit is \(\text{s}^{-1}\).

The decay constant \(\lambda\) represents the probability of decay per unit time for a single nucleus. A larger \(\lambda\) means the substance decays more rapidly.

Half-life and Mean Life

To characterize how fast a radioactive sample decays, we use two practical time parameters: half-life and mean life.

Half-life (\(T_{1/2}\))

The half-life is the time required for exactly half of the active nuclei in a sample to decay. At \(t = T_{1/2}\), \(N = N_0 / 2\).

Using the decay equation: \(\frac{N_0}{2} = N_0 e^{-\lambda T_{1/2}} \implies \ln(2) = \lambda T_{1/2}\)

\[ T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(2)}{\lambda} \approx \frac{0.693}{\lambda} \]

After \(n\) half-lives (\(t = n \cdot T_{1/2}\)), the number of remaining nuclei is \(N = N_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n\).

Mean Life (\(\tau\))

Since nuclei decay randomly, some decay almost instantly while others may last thousands of years. The mean life is the average lifespan of all the nuclei in a given sample.

\[ \tau = \frac{1}{\lambda} \]

Relation between Half-life and Mean Life:

\[ T_{1/2} = \tau \ln(2) \approx 0.693 \tau \]

Activity of a Radioactive Substance

The activity (\(A\) or \(R\)) of a radioactive sample is its rate of decay. It tells us how many disintegrations occur per second.

\[ A = \left| \frac{dN}{dt} \right| = \lambda N \]

Substituting the exponential decay law, we find that activity also decays exponentially:

\[ A(t) = A_0 e^{-\lambda t} \]

Where \(A_0 = \lambda N_0\) is the initial activity at \(t=0\).

Units of Activity:

  • Becquerel (Bq): The SI unit. \(1 \text{ Bq} = 1 \text{ disintegration per second (dps)}\).
  • Curie (Ci): A historical unit. \(1 \text{ Ci} = 3.7 \times 10^{10} \text{ dps}\) (activity of 1 gram of Radium-226).
  • Rutherford (Rd): \(1 \text{ Rd} = 10^6 \text{ dps}\).

Successive Disintegration and Radioactive Equilibrium

Often, a radioactive nucleus (Parent, \(A\)) decays into a daughter nucleus (\(B\)), which is itself radioactive and decays into another nucleus (\(C\)). This is successive disintegration.

\[ A \xrightarrow{\lambda_1} B \xrightarrow{\lambda_2} C \]

If the half-life of the parent \(A\) is much greater than the half-life of the daughter \(B\) (\(\lambda_1 \ll \lambda_2\)), a state of Secular Equilibrium can be reached. In this state, the rate of formation of \(B\) (from \(A\)) equals its rate of decay into \(C\).

At equilibrium, the number of nuclei \(N_B\) remains constant (\(dN_B/dt = 0\)):

\[ \lambda_1 N_A = \lambda_2 N_B \]

Which means their activities are equal: \(A_A = A_B\).
