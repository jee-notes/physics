Beta Decay

Interconversion of nucleons

Beta Minus and Beta Plus Decay

Beta decay occurs in nuclei that have an improper ratio of neutrons to protons, lying outside the band of stability. It involves the conversion of a neutron to a proton or vice versa, with the emission of a beta particle (an electron or a positron).

Beta Minus (\(\beta^-\)) Decay

Occurs in neutron-rich nuclei. A neutron is transformed into a proton, an electron (\(\beta^-\)), and an antineutrino (\(\bar{\nu}\)).

\[ _{0}^{1}\text{n} \longrightarrow _{1}^{1}\text{p} + _{-1}^{0}\text{e} + \bar{\nu} \]

The general nuclear reaction is:

\[ _{Z}^{A}\text{X} \longrightarrow _{Z+1}^{A}\text{Y} + _{-1}^{0}\text{e} + \bar{\nu} \]

In \(\beta^-\) decay, the atomic number \(Z\) increases by 1, while the mass number \(A\) remains unchanged.

Beta Plus (\(\beta^+\)) Decay

Occurs in proton-rich nuclei. A proton is transformed into a neutron, a positron (\(\beta^+\), the antimatter counterpart of an electron), and a neutrino (\(\nu\)).

\[ _{1}^{1}\text{p} \longrightarrow _{0}^{1}\text{n} + _{+1}^{0}\text{e} + \nu \]

The general nuclear reaction is:

\[ _{Z}^{A}\text{X} \longrightarrow _{Z-1}^{A}\text{Y} + _{+1}^{0}\text{e} + \nu \]

In \(\beta^+\) decay, the atomic number \(Z\) decreases by 1, while the mass number \(A\) remains unchanged.

Electron Capture

Electron capture is a process that competes with \(\beta^+\) decay in proton-rich nuclei. Instead of emitting a positron, the nucleus captures an orbital electron (usually from the innermost K-shell). This electron combines with a proton in the nucleus to form a neutron and a neutrino.

\[ _{1}^{1}\text{p} + _{-1}^{0}\text{e} \longrightarrow _{0}^{1}\text{n} + \nu \]

The general nuclear reaction is:

\[ _{Z}^{A}\text{X} + _{-1}^{0}\text{e} \longrightarrow _{Z-1}^{A}\text{Y} + \nu \]

Like \(\beta^+\) decay, electron capture reduces the atomic number by 1 while keeping the mass number constant. It is often accompanied by the emission of characteristic X-rays as outer shell electrons cascade down to fill the vacancy left in the K-shell.

Neutrino Hypothesis

Early studies of beta decay showed a puzzling problem: the emitted beta particles had a continuous spectrum of kinetic energies, rather than a single discrete value. This seemed to violate the principles of conservation of energy and momentum, since a two-body decay (\(\text{Parent} \rightarrow \text{Daughter} + \text{Electron}\)) should yield fixed energies for the products.

To rescue the conservation laws, Wolfgang Pauli proposed the Neutrino Hypothesis in 1930. He suggested that a third particle is emitted during beta decay, which shares the available kinetic energy (\(Q\)-value) with the beta particle.

The neutrino (\(\nu\)) and antineutrino (\(\bar{\nu}\)) possess specific properties:

  • They are electrically neutral, hence unaffected by electric and magnetic fields.
  • Their rest mass is almost zero.
  • They interact extremely weakly with matter, making them extraordinarily difficult to detect.
  • They carry away the "missing" energy and momentum in beta decay. \(K_{\text{max}} = K_\beta + K_\nu \approx Q\).
