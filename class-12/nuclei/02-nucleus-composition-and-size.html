Size and Composition

What makes up the nucleus?

Nuclear Dimensions

How Small?

The size of an atom is of the order of \(\sim10^{-10}\,\text{m}\), whereas the size of a nucleus is of the order of \(\sim10^{-15}\,\text{m}\).

The nuclear scale has its own unit:

\[ \boxed{1\,\text{fm}=10^{-15}\,\text{m}} \]

This unit is called a femtometre or fermi. Nuclear dimensions are therefore usually expressed in femtometres.

Imagine enlarging an atom until the atom itself is the size of a classroom.
The nucleus at its centre would be roughly the size of a pinhead.
Yet this tiny region contains essentially all the mass of the atom and all its positive charge.

So the atom is overwhelmingly empty space, while almost all of its mass is packed into an extremely small nuclear volume. This makes the nucleus an extraordinarily dense physical system.

How Do We Measure?

Of course, we cannot put a ruler next to a nucleus. The nuclear radius has to be inferred from the way the nucleus interacts with a suitable probe.

The basic principle is simple: to resolve a structure of size \(d\), the probe must have a wavelength comparable to or smaller than \(d\). For a particle, its wavelength is given by the de Broglie relation

\[ \boxed{\lambda=\frac{h}{p}} \]

At the nuclear scale, even ordinary X-ray wavelengths (that are used widely at the atomic scale) are not sufficient to resolve the nucleus effectively. We therefore use a high-energy electron beam, whose de Broglie wavelength can be made comparable to nuclear dimensions, and study how the electrons are scattered by the nucleus.

The idea is the same as in Rutherford's experiment. If the nucleus were an ideal point charge, the scattering would follow the pattern expected for a point charge. A real nucleus has a finite spatial extent, and sufficiently high-energy electrons begin to resolve this extent. The way the scattering pattern deviates from the point-charge prediction therefore tells us about the size of the nucleus.

The nuclear radius is therefore not a directly observed geometrical length.
It is an inferred length obtained from the reproducible scattering behaviour of high-energy probes.

Composition of the Nucleus

We have already seen how the proton and neutron emerged historically as the constituents of the nucleus. They are collectively called nucleons.

\[ \boxed{\text{nucleus}=\text{protons}+\text{neutrons}} \]

If a nucleus contains \(Z\) protons and \(N\) neutrons, then its total number of nucleons is

\[ \boxed{A=Z+N} \]

Here \(A\) is called the mass number. The nuclear charge is determined only by the number of protons:

\[ \boxed{Q=+Ze} \]

Thus the two numbers \(Z\) and \(A\) tell us the basic composition of a nucleus:

\[ \boxed{Z=\text{number of protons}} \] \[ \boxed{A=\text{number of nucleons}} \]

and therefore

\[ \boxed{N=A-Z} \]
The nucleus is therefore characterized at the most basic level by its size, mass, charge and composition.
Its size is of the order of femtometres, its mass is dominated by its nucleons, and its charge is determined by its proton number \(Z\).

Nuclear Mass

Atomic masses are extraordinarily small, so instead of using kilograms, a special unit is used: the atomic mass unit, denoted by \(u\). It is defined using the carbon-12 atom:

\[ \boxed{1\,u=\frac{1}{12}\times\text{mass of one }{}^{12}\mathrm C\text{ atom}} \]

Numerically,

\[ \boxed{1\,u\approx1.6605\times10^{-27}\,\text{kg}} \]

Once this unit is defined, the masses of atoms and sub-atomic particles can be expressed conveniently in atomic mass units.

To determine nuclear mass, we adopted 2 different methods: for charged sub-atomic particles and neutral sub-atomic particles.

Masses of the Charged Constituents

The electron and proton are charged particles. Therefore, their motion can be studied using electric and magnetic fields. By measuring quantities such as their charge-to-mass ratio and combining these with the known charge, their masses can be determined.

Their masses are approximately

\[ \boxed{m_e=0.0005486\,u} \] \[ \boxed{m_p=1.007276\,u} \]

Thus, the proton is about \(1836\) times more massive than the electron.

Masses of the Neutral Constituents

The neutron has no electric charge. Therefore, we cannot determine its mass by measuring \(e/m\) in electric or magnetic fields. We need a different method.

The neutron was discovered through nuclear collisions. For example, when beryllium is bombarded with \(\alpha\)-particles, it produces a penetrating neutral radiation that can be represented by

\[ {}^{9}_{4}\mathrm{Be}+{}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}\rightarrow{}^{12}_{6}\mathrm C+{}^{1}_{0}\mathrm n \]

The neutron itself cannot be bent by an electric or magnetic field, but it can collide with a charged nucleus. If a neutron hits a proton, for example, it transfers momentum and kinetic energy to the proton. The recoil of that proton can be measured.

Now the laws of conservation come to our rescue. The incoming neutron and the target nucleus form a collision system, so conservation of energy and momentum relates the measured recoil to the unknown neutron mass. By comparing the recoil produced in different nuclei, the mass of the neutron can be inferred.

The neutron mass is approximately

\[ \boxed{m_n\approx1.008665\,u} \]

Thus, proton and neutron have nearly the same mass, while the electron is much lighter.

Total Atomic Mass

A remarkable fact about the atom is that almost all the mass of an atom is concentrated in this tiny nuclear region (\(\gt 99\%\)). The mass of the electrons is negligible compared with the total atomic mass, so to a very good approximation,

\[ \boxed{M_{\text{atom}}\approx M_{\text{nucleus}}} \]

Atomic (Ionic) masses can be measured using mass spectrometric techniques. The basic idea is to turn atoms into ions and study how their motion changes in electric and magnetic fields. Since the deflection depends on the particle's mass-to-charge ratio, the mass can be determined when the charge is known.

We have arrangements to measure the mass of entire atom using mass spectrometry.
We have also measured the mass of each constituent particle.
We have arrangements to determine the constituent structure of an atom.
In general, we can check if mass measured from method 1 is consistent with that measured from method 2

An atom contains a nucleus surrounded by electrons. If the nucleus contains \(Z\) protons and \(N\) neutrons, then, as a first approximation, its mass is the sum of the masses of its nucleons:

\[ M_{\text{nucleus}}\approx Zm_p + N m_n \]

The atom also contains \(Z\) electrons, so its mass is approximately

\[ M_{\text{atom}}\approx Zm_p+Nm_n+Zm_e \]

More precisely, the actual mass is slightly smaller than this sum because the bound system has negative binding energy:

\[ \boxed{M_{\text{atom}}c^2=Zm_pc^2+Nm_nc^2+Zm_ec^2-E_B} \]

where \(E_B\) is the total binding energy. This difference will become extremely important later.

Einstein Mass-Energy Equivalence

Einstein's mass-energy equivalence principle, \[ E=mc^2, \] provides a different way to express the masses of atomic-scale particles. Instead of expressing mass only in kilograms or atomic mass units, we can express it in terms of its equivalent energy.

\[ 1\,u=1.660539\times10^{-27}\,\text{kg} \] \[ 1\,u\equiv931.5\,\text{MeV}/c^2 \]

We can therefore express the masses of the proton, neutron and electron in the same form:

\[ \boxed{m_p\approx938.3\,\text{MeV}/c^2} \] \[ \boxed{m_n\approx939.6\,\text{MeV}/c^2} \] \[ \boxed{m_e\approx0.511\,\text{MeV}/c^2} \]

These numbers should not be interpreted as saying that the mass itself is an energy. Rather, they represent the energy equivalent of the mass through

\[ \boxed{E=mc^2}. \]

For example,

\[ m_pc^2\approx938.3\,\text{MeV} \]

means that the rest mass of a proton corresponds to an energy of about \(938.3\,\text{MeV}\). Similarly,

\[ m_nc^2\approx939.6\,\text{MeV}. \]
At the nuclear scale, it is often much more convenient to talk about mass in energy units.

\[ \boxed{m\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad mc^2} \]

This becomes extremely useful because nuclear processes involve energy transfers of the order of MeV. A tiny change in nuclear mass can therefore be converted directly into a measurable amount of energy.

This also gives us a very useful way to compare the mass of an entire atom with the masses of its constituents. If the atom is made of \(Z\) protons, \(N\) neutrons and \(Z\) electrons, we can calculate the total mass expected from its constituents and compare it with the experimentally measured atomic mass.

\[ \boxed{ M_{\text{atom, constituents}} = Zm_p+Nm_n+Zm_e } \]

If these two masses were exactly equal, there would be nothing further to explain. But experimentally, they are not exactly equal.

This difference is not an experimental nuisance. It is a physical quantity.

Using \[ E=mc^2, \] a difference in mass corresponds to a difference in energy.

This will lead us to one of the central ideas of nuclear physics: binding energy.

Thus, our two independent experimental descriptions of the atom — its measured mass and its constituent particles — can be connected through Einstein's mass-energy equivalence.

Atomic Masses and Isotopes

The mass of an atom is extremely small. It is conveniently expressed in terms of the atomic mass unit (u), which is defined as \(1/12^{th}\) of the mass of a carbon-12 (\(^{12}\text{C}\)) atom. Mass of one \(^{12}\text{C}\) atom is: \(1.992647 \times 10^{-26} \text{ kg}\)

\[ 1 \text{ u} = \frac{\text{mass of one }^{12}\text{C atom}}{12} = 1.660539 \times 10^{-27} \text{ kg} \]

Using Einstein's mass-energy equivalence principle (\(E = mc^2\)), the energy equivalent of \(1\text{ u}\) is:

\[ 1\text{ u} \equiv 931.5 \text{ MeV} \]

Isotopes, Isobars, and Isotones

  • Isotopes: Nuclei with the same atomic number \(Z\) but different mass numbers \(A\). Since they have the same number of protons, they belong to the same chemical element and have identical chemical properties. Examples: \(^{1}_{1}\text{H}, ^{2}_{1}\text{H}, ^{3}_{1}\text{H}\) (Hydrogen, Deuterium, Tritium).
  • Isobars: Nuclei with the same mass number \(A\) but different atomic numbers \(Z\). Example: \(^{14}_{6}\text{C}\) and \(^{14}_{7}\text{N}\).
  • Isotones: Nuclei with the same number of neutrons \(N\). Example: \(^{37}_{17}\text{Cl}\) (\(N=20\)) and \(^{39}_{19}\text{K}\) (\(N=20\)).

Since most elements occur in nature as a mixture of isotopes, their atomic mass is the weighted average of the masses of their isotopes, which often results in fractional atomic masses for elements.

Size and Density of Nucleus

Rutherford's alpha-scattering experiment established that the nucleus has a very small size, on the order of \(10^{-15}\) m to \(10^{-14}\) m. It was found experimentally that the volume of a nucleus is directly proportional to its mass number \(A\).

Assuming the nucleus to be spherical with radius \(R\):

\[ \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \propto A \]

\[ R = R_0 A^{1/3} \]

Where:

  • \(R\) = Radius of the nucleus
  • \(A\) = Mass number
  • \(R_0\) = An empirical constant, \(R_0 \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-15}\) m or \(1.2 \text{ fm}\) (fermi)

Nuclear Density

Nuclear density (\(\rho\)) is the ratio of the mass of a nucleus to its volume.

Mass of nucleus \(\approx A \cdot m_u\) (where \(m_u\) is the average mass of a nucleon \(\approx 1.66 \times 10^{-27}\) kg)

Volume of nucleus = \(\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 = \frac{4}{3}\pi (R_0 A^{1/3})^3 = \frac{4}{3}\pi R_0^3 A\)

\[ \rho = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Volume}} = \frac{A \cdot m_u}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R_0^3 A} = \frac{m_u}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R_0^3} \]

Notice that the mass number \(A\) cancels out. This means that nuclear density is essentially constant for all nuclei, regardless of their size. It is roughly \(2.3 \times 10^{17}\text{ kg/m}^3\). This incredibly high density suggests that matter inside the nucleus is tightly packed without any empty space.
