History of Nucleus

Sub-nuclear journey.

Nucleus

Rutherford's scattering experiment changed the picture of the atom completely.

In 1911, Rutherford explained the large-angle scattering of \(\alpha\)-particles by proposing that almost all the positive charge and essentially all the mass of an atom are concentrated in a tiny central region — the nucleus.

The nucleus was therefore known to exist, but a much deeper question had now appeared: What is the nucleus made of?

This question could not be answered easily. The nucleus is of the order of \(10^{-15}\,\text{m}\) in size, whereas an atom is of the order of \(10^{-10}\,\text{m}\). Thus, the nucleus is roughly \(10^{-5}\) times the linear size of the atom.

It would take a genius to be able to interact with the nucleus. Imagine someone trying to talk to your heart without opening your body — the nucleus was something like that inside the atom.

The next few decades were essentially the process of opening up this tiny "Pandora's box".

Using X-rays to Probe Nuclear Charge

Just as Rutherford bombarded gold with \(\alpha\)-particles and studied what came out, other scientists were bombarding different metals with high-speed electron beams. When electrons were made to strike a metal target, the target emitted X-rays.

These X-rays were not random. Each element produced its own characteristic X-ray frequencies.

It was because the high energy electron beam was able to knock off the innermost electrons of the atoms of the target metal.And so when another outer electron replaced it, it emitted an X-ray photon whose energy is the difference between the two energy levels and that is huge (as compared to the emission spectrum of hydrogen).

Now, the energies of the innermost atomic levels depend on the charge of the nucleus. A larger nuclear charge attracts the electrons more strongly and therefore changes the energies of these levels. Hence the energy of the emitted X-ray photon depends on the nuclear charge:

\[ \boxed{\text{nuclear charge}\rightarrow\text{electronic energy levels}\rightarrow\text{X-ray energy}} \]

And as \(E=h\nu\) , the frequency of the characteristic X-rays also carries information about the nuclear charge.

Henry Moseley systematically bombarded different elements with electron beams and measured their characteristic X-ray frequencies. He found a remarkably precise relation:

\[ \boxed{\sqrt{\nu}\propto Z} \]

where \(Z\) is the atomic number. Thus, the X-ray spectrum provided an experimental way of determining \(Z\).

Now combine this with Rutherford's nuclear model. If the atom has atomic number \(Z\), and its positive charge is concentrated in the nucleus, then the nuclear charge must be

\[ \boxed{Q_{\text{nucleus}}=+Ze} \]

Once the nuclear charge was known, the next question appeared: if the nucleus carries \(+Ze\), what is actually carrying this positive charge? Is it made of discrete units, like the electron charge, or is the nuclear charge some continuous distribution?

The deeper experimental idea is broader than X-rays. At the microscopic level, we cannot simply open up an atom or nucleus and look inside it. We therefore make matter and radiation interact and study the observable outcome of that interaction — usually through changes in energy, momentum and radiation.

This is where the modern concept of energy becomes extraordinarily powerful. We are not merely looking for new phenomena; we are looking for the form in which the conserved quantity — energy — manifests itself in the particular physical setup.

For radiation, \[ \boxed{E=h\nu} \] and for mass, \[ \boxed{E=mc^2}. \] These relations allow us to translate measurable radiation and matter into the same conserved quantity and thereby decode what is happening in microscopic interactions.

Thus, a large part of the development of modern and nuclear physics can be viewed as: \[ \boxed{ \text{interact} \rightarrow \text{observe the energy transformation} \rightarrow \text{identify its reproducible pattern} \rightarrow \text{discover the underlying structure} } \]

Is the Nucleus Built from Hydrogen?

Now we knew that every nucleus carries a positive charge \(+Ze\). But what actually constitutes this positive charge?

There was already an old idea about this. In the early nineteenth century, William Prout had noticed that the atomic masses of many elements were approximately whole-number multiples of the mass of hydrogen. He proposed that hydrogen might therefore be the fundamental building block of all elements.

At that time this was only a hypothesis based on the observed atomic masses. There was no known picture of the atom, let alone the nucleus, that could explain how such a thing might actually work.

Rutherford tried to investigate the nucleus by the same strategy that had led to its discovery: bombard it with energetic particles and observe what comes out. He was studying transmutation of elements. Imagine if one can convert iron to gold.

When \(\alpha\)-particles were fired at nitrogen, Rutherford observed fast particles that were identified as hydrogen nuclei.

\[ \alpha+\mathrm{N} \rightarrow \text{products}+\mathrm{H} \]

A hydrogen nucleus appeared as a product of a nuclear collision. Rutherford's interpretation was that the hydrogen nucleus was created as it was already a constituent of the nitrogen nucleus that had been knocked out.

This was the crucial transition: Prout had suggested that hydrogen might be the fundamental building block of matter. Rutherford now had experimental evidence that a hydrogen nucleus was actually present inside another atomic nucleus.

The hydrogen nucleus therefore emerged as a fundamental positive constituent of nuclei. This particle was later called the proton (from Prout's protyle).

The deeper idea of the history flow is that:
Mass and Charge are 2 properties of matter that we study in mechanics and electromagnetism.
We first study the atomic and sub-atomic matter based on these properties.
E.g. before Mosley, \(Z\) was not having a physical meaning and was just a serial number of the element ordered as per their masses.
Similarly, Prout came up with his "protyle" theory using masses.
Then we study charge of these particles and ultimately we describe these particles using their mass and charge only.
Other parameters describing the particles like spin quantum number or magnetic moment came later.

The Mystery of the Neutron

The proton seemed to solve the question of nuclear charge. If a nucleus contains \(Z\) protons, then its charge is naturally \(Q_{\text{nucleus}}=+Ze\) .

But there was now a problem with the mass of the nucleus. If nuclei were made only of protons, their mass should be closely connected with their charge. But this was clearly not the case.

For example, an \(\alpha\)-particle is a helium nucleus having \(Z=2\) and \(A=4\) . Isotopes made the problem even more obvious. They have same \(Z\) and different \(A\) . So the proton behaves differently in different isotopes or what is it?

Rutherford already suspected that some neutral constituent exists inside the nucleus. But this was only a hypothesis. The actual particle had to be found experimentally.

The clue appeared in 1930, when Bothe and Becker found that, when beryllium was bombarded with \(\alpha\)-particles, it produced an extraordinarily penetrating radiation.

The radiation was electrically neutral and highly penetrating. Since it was not deflected by electric or magnetic fields, it was initially interpreted as a very energetic form of \(\gamma\)-radiation (photon).

In 1932, Joliot and Curie allowed this radiation to strike a hydrogen-containing material such as paraffin. They found that it could knock out protons from the material with surprisingly large kinetic energies.

This was the crucial clue - Kinetic Energy. A photon could certainly transfer energy to a proton, but the observed proton recoil was far too large to be easily explained by the proposed \(\gamma\)-ray interpretation. It looked much more like a massive neutral particle had collided with the proton.

James Chadwick followed up these experiments in 1932. By studying the recoil of protons and other nuclei, he showed that the penetrating radiation from beryllium was not \(\gamma\)-radiation at all. It consisted of a new particle with approximately the same mass as the proton but with no electric charge.

This particle was named the neutron. The nuclear reaction can be written as

\[ {}^{9}_{4}\mathrm{Be}+{}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}\rightarrow{}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}+{}^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n} \]

The neutron was therefore the missing constituent that could contribute substantial nuclear mass without changing the nuclear charge. The nuclear picture finally became

\[ \boxed{\text{nucleus}=\text{protons}+\text{neutrons}} \]

Why the Photon Hypothesis Failed: Chadwick's Kinematics

If the radiation emitted by beryllium were neutral mass-less \(\gamma\)-photons, the observed proton recoil kinetic energy (\(K_p \approx 5.7\,\text{MeV}\)) would require a photon energy of:

\[ h\nu \approx \sqrt{\frac{m_p c^2 \cdot K_p}{2}} \approx 55\,\text{MeV} \]

Such an energy was completely incompatible with the available energy of the incident \(\alpha\)-particles.

Chadwick assumed the radiation consisted of neutral particles of unknown mass \(m_n\) traveling with velocity \(v_0\). In a head-on elastic collision with a stationary nucleus of mass \(M\), conservation of linear momentum and kinetic energy gives the recoil velocity \(v\) of the target:

\[ v = \frac{2m_n}{m_n + M}v_0 \]

By measuring the maximum recoil speeds of both hydrogen nuclei (protons, \(M = m_p\)) and nitrogen nuclei (\(M = m_{\text{N}} \approx 14m_p\)):

\[ \frac{v_p}{v_{\text{N}}} = \frac{m_n + m_{\text{N}}}{m_n + m_p} \]

Substituting the experimental velocity ratio gave:

\[ \boxed{m_n \approx m_p} \]

This confirmed that the radiation was a neutral particle with a mass almost identical to that of a proton, ruling out the \(\gamma\)-ray hypothesis entirely.

Nucleons

The nucleus had now revealed its constituents: the proton and the neutron. They are collectively called nucleons.

\[ \boxed{\text{nucleus}=\text{protons}+\text{neutrons}} \]

This immediately opens up the next layer of questions. What are the properties of these nucleons inside a nucleus? How does the mass of a nucleus arise? Why do nuclei remain bound despite the repulsion between protons? What determines whether a nucleus is stable or unstable? What happens when nuclei interact with other particles or radiation?

These are precisely the questions of nuclear physics.

Having identified the nucleus as a system of protons and neutrons, we can now study the behaviour of this system — its mass, size, binding, stability, interactions and transformations.
