Nuclear Reactions

Fission, Fusion, and Energy generation

Q-value of Nuclear Reactions

A nuclear reaction generally involves bombarding a target nucleus with a projectile such as a neutron, proton or \(\alpha\)-particle, resulting in new products.

A general reaction can be written as

\[ \boxed{A+a\longrightarrow B+b+Q} \]

where \(A\) is the target nucleus, \(a\) is the projectile, \(B\) is the product nucleus and \(b\) is the emitted particle.

The endeavour here is to study the energy change involved in the reaction. So, how do we determine this energy change? At the nuclear scale, one important form of energy is associated with mass. Using Einstein's relation \(E=mc^2\), we can determine the energy change from the difference between the total masses of the reactants and products.

\[ \boxed{Q=\left[(m_A+m_a)-(m_B+m_b)\right]c^2} \]

Thus, the Q-value represents the energy associated with the mass defect of the reaction.

However, the next question is: how does this \(Q\)-energy manifest itself?

In ordinary chemical reactions, we generally study energy changes for bulk matter and use thermodynamic quantities such as enthalpy \(\Delta H\). We may describe a reaction as exothermic when energy is released to the surroundings, usually as heat, or endothermic when energy is absorbed from the surroundings.

At the nuclear scale, however, we can follow the energy more directly. The mass of a particle represents mass-energy \(mc^2\), its motion carries kinetic energy, and electromagnetic radiation carries energy \(h\nu\).

\[ \boxed{E_{\text{mass}}=mc^2,\qquad E_{\text{kinetic}}=K,\qquad E_{\text{radiation}}=h\nu} \]

Therefore, when \(Q>0\), the decrease in mass-energy can manifest itself as kinetic energy of the products and/or as energy carried by photons.

\[ \Delta E_{\text{mass}}\rightarrow \Delta K \quad \text{and/or} \quad E_\gamma \]

Thus, for a reaction in which no radiation is emitted and the final nuclei remain in their ground states,

\[ \boxed{Q=K_{\text{final}}-K_{\text{initial}}} \]

so a positive \(Q\)-value means that the products have gained kinetic energy.

If \(Q \lt 0\), the final particles have greater mass-energy than the initial particles. That additional mass-energy has to be supplied by the initial system, usually through the kinetic energy of the projectile. Hence an endothermic reaction requires a minimum threshold kinetic energy to occur.

At the nuclear scale, an individual reaction is not described in terms of heat.
We directly track how the energy is distributed among its different forms: mass-energy, kinetic energy and radiation energy.
A single nucleus does not possess a temperature merely because its constituent particles have kinetic energy.
Temperature is a macroscopic quantity associated with bulk matter.
However, heat is still relevant when we consider bulk nuclear matter.
The energy released by a huge number of nuclear reactions can be transferred through the material and eventually appear as thermal energy. At that macroscopic scale, temperature and heat become meaningful quantities.
At the nuclear scale, an individual nucleus can certainly have constituent particles with kinetic energy. But kinetic energy by itself does not imply temperature.

Temperature is a thermodynamic quantity whose physical meaning comes from thermal energy transfer: a temperature difference between systems in thermal contact drives energy flow from higher temperature to lower temperature.

Thus, \[ \boxed{\text{temperature}\rightarrow\text{thermal energy transfer}} \] but not \[ \boxed{\text{kinetic energy}\rightarrow\text{temperature}}. \] A single nucleus therefore is not assigned a temperature merely because its constituent nucleons possess kinetic energy.

For bulk nuclear matter or a sufficiently large collection of nuclei in thermal equilibrium, temperature becomes meaningful again.

Thus, the Q-value is essentially an energy-conservation statement written in the nuclear language of mass:

\[ \boxed{\text{initial mass-energy}+\text{initial kinetic energy}=\text{final mass-energy}+\text{final kinetic/radiation energy}} \]

Q-value from Binding Energy

The Q-value can also be understood through the change in the binding energy of the nuclei involved.

If a nucleus has binding energy per nucleon \(b\) and mass number \(A\), then its total binding energy is simply

\[ BE=Ab \]

Therefore, for a nuclear reaction,

\[ \boxed{ Q=\sum BE_{\text{products}}-\sum BE_{\text{reactants}} } \]

So, if the products are more tightly bound than the reactants, their total binding energy is greater and

\[ Q>0. \]

The increase in binding energy appears as released energy. Conversely, if the products are less tightly bound,

\[ Q<0. \]

Thus, when the \(BE/A\) values of the nuclei involved are known, we can calculate the Q-value directly without separately calculating the masses.

For example, consider the complete breakup of an alpha particle:

\[ {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}\rightarrow2p+2n \]

The alpha particle has binding energy per nucleon \(b\), so its total binding energy is

\[ BE_{\alpha}=4b. \]

The final protons and neutrons are free nucleons, so their binding energy is zero. Therefore,

\[ Q=0-4b \] \[ \boxed{Q=-4b}. \]

So complete breakup of a bound nucleus into free nucleons is always endothermic: we have to supply the binding energy that was holding the nucleus together.

This gives a very useful way of looking at the \(BE/A\) curve.

For a reaction involving bound nuclei, compare the total binding energy of the products and reactants: \[ BE=A\left(\frac{BE}{A}\right). \]

If the total binding energy increases, \[ \boxed{Q>0} \] and the reaction releases energy. If the total binding energy decreases, \[ \boxed{Q<0} \] and energy must be supplied.

For a free proton or neutron, \[ \boxed{BE=0}. \] So whenever a single nucleon is produced or consumed, its binding-energy contribution is simply zero.

Nuclear Fission Mechanism

Nuclear fission is the splitting of a heavy nucleus (like Uranium-235) into two lighter, more stable intermediate-mass nuclei, accompanied by the release of a massive amount of energy and typically a few fast neutrons.

When a slow (thermal) neutron strikes a \(^{235}\text{U}\) nucleus, it is absorbed to form a highly unstable intermediate \(^{236}\text{U}^*\), which rapidly undergoes fission.

\[ _{92}^{235}\text{U} + _{0}^{1}\text{n} \longrightarrow _{92}^{236}\text{U}^* \longrightarrow _{56}^{141}\text{Ba} + _{36}^{92}\text{Kr} + 3 _{0}^{1}\text{n} + Q \]

The \(Q\)-value for uranium fission is extraordinarily high, approximately \(200 \text{ MeV}\) per fission event. This energy comes from the fact that the \(BE/A\) for intermediate nuclei is higher (\(\approx 8.5 \text{ MeV}\)) than that of Uranium (\(\approx 7.6 \text{ MeV}\)).

Because fission produces 2-3 new neutrons for every one neutron consumed, it can trigger a chain reaction. If unchecked, this leads to a massive, explosive energy release (atomic bomb). If controlled, it can generate steady power (nuclear reactor).

Nuclear Reactor Fundamentals

A nuclear reactor is a device used to initiate and control a self-sustained nuclear chain reaction. The critical parameter is the multiplication factor (\(k\)), which is the ratio of the number of neutrons in a given generation to the number in the previous generation.

  • \(k < 1\): Subcritical (reaction dies out)
  • \(k = 1\): Critical (steady power operation, goal of a reactor)
  • \(k > 1\): Supercritical (power increases exponentially)

Key Components of a Reactor:

  • Fuel: Usually enriched Uranium (contains a higher percentage of \(^{235}\text{U}\)).
  • Moderator: Slows down fast neutrons produced in fission to thermal speeds, making them more likely to cause further \(^{235}\text{U}\) fission. Common moderators: Water, Heavy Water (\(D_2O\)), Graphite.
  • Control Rods: Made of materials that readily absorb neutrons (e.g., Cadmium, Boron). They are inserted or withdrawn to keep \(k = 1\).
  • Coolant: Liquid or gas circulated through the reactor core to absorb heat and transfer it to a turbine to generate electricity.

Nuclear Fusion and Stellar Energy

Nuclear fusion is the process in which two very light nuclei combine to form a single, heavier, and more stable nucleus. This process also releases a tremendous amount of energy, as the \(BE/A\) of the product is significantly higher than that of the light reactants.

For fusion to occur, the nuclei must overcome a massive electrostatic repulsion (Coulomb barrier) to get close enough for the strong nuclear force to take over. This requires extraordinarily high temperatures (on the order of \(10^7 \text{ K}\)) and high pressure, which is why it is called a thermonuclear reaction.

Example: Fusion of Deuterium and Tritium (D-T reaction)

\[ _{1}^{2}\text{H} + _{1}^{3}\text{H} \longrightarrow _{2}^{4}\text{He} + _{0}^{1}\text{n} + 17.6 \text{ MeV} \]

Stellar Energy (Energy of the Sun)

Fusion is the source of energy in stars, including our Sun. In the core of the Sun, the dominant process is the Proton-Proton (\(p-p\)) cycle, where four hydrogen nuclei (protons) eventually fuse to form a helium nucleus.

The net reaction for the \(p-p\) cycle is:

\[ 4 _{1}^{1}\text{H} \longrightarrow _{2}^{4}\text{He} + 2 _{+1}^{0}\text{e} + 2 \nu + 26.7 \text{ MeV} \]

While fission releases about \(200 \text{ MeV}\) per nucleus and fusion only \(\sim 26 \text{ MeV}\), fusion actually produces far more energy per unit mass. One kilogram of hydrogen undergoing fusion yields significantly more energy than one kilogram of uranium undergoing fission.
