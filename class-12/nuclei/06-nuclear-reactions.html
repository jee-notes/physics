Nuclear Reactions

Fission, Fusion, and Energy generation

Q-value of Nuclear Reactions

A nuclear reaction generally involves bombarding a target nucleus with a projectile (like a neutron, proton, or alpha particle), resulting in a new product nucleus and an emitted particle.

Reaction equation: \(A + a \longrightarrow B + b\)

Where \(A\) is the target, \(a\) is the projectile, \(B\) is the product, and \(b\) is the emitted particle.

The Q-value represents the energy released or absorbed in the reaction, calculated from the mass difference between reactants and products.

\[ Q = [ (m_A + m_a) - (m_B + m_b) ] c^2 \]
  • If \(Q > 0\), the reaction is Exothermic (releases energy). The total kinetic energy of the products is greater than that of the reactants.
  • If \(Q < 0\), the reaction is Endothermic (absorbs energy). The reaction requires a minimum threshold kinetic energy from the projectile to occur.

Nuclear Fission Mechanism

Nuclear fission is the splitting of a heavy nucleus (like Uranium-235) into two lighter, more stable intermediate-mass nuclei, accompanied by the release of a massive amount of energy and typically a few fast neutrons.

When a slow (thermal) neutron strikes a \(^{235}\text{U}\) nucleus, it is absorbed to form a highly unstable intermediate \(^{236}\text{U}^*\), which rapidly undergoes fission.

\[ _{92}^{235}\text{U} + _{0}^{1}\text{n} \longrightarrow _{92}^{236}\text{U}^* \longrightarrow _{56}^{141}\text{Ba} + _{36}^{92}\text{Kr} + 3 _{0}^{1}\text{n} + Q \]

The \(Q\)-value for uranium fission is extraordinarily high, approximately \(200 \text{ MeV}\) per fission event. This energy comes from the fact that the \(BE/A\) for intermediate nuclei is higher (\(\approx 8.5 \text{ MeV}\)) than that of Uranium (\(\approx 7.6 \text{ MeV}\)).

Because fission produces 2-3 new neutrons for every one neutron consumed, it can trigger a chain reaction. If unchecked, this leads to a massive, explosive energy release (atomic bomb). If controlled, it can generate steady power (nuclear reactor).

Nuclear Reactor Fundamentals

A nuclear reactor is a device used to initiate and control a self-sustained nuclear chain reaction. The critical parameter is the multiplication factor (\(k\)), which is the ratio of the number of neutrons in a given generation to the number in the previous generation.

  • \(k < 1\): Subcritical (reaction dies out)
  • \(k = 1\): Critical (steady power operation, goal of a reactor)
  • \(k > 1\): Supercritical (power increases exponentially)

Key Components of a Reactor:

  • Fuel: Usually enriched Uranium (contains a higher percentage of \(^{235}\text{U}\)).
  • Moderator: Slows down fast neutrons produced in fission to thermal speeds, making them more likely to cause further \(^{235}\text{U}\) fission. Common moderators: Water, Heavy Water (\(D_2O\)), Graphite.
  • Control Rods: Made of materials that readily absorb neutrons (e.g., Cadmium, Boron). They are inserted or withdrawn to keep \(k = 1\).
  • Coolant: Liquid or gas circulated through the reactor core to absorb heat and transfer it to a turbine to generate electricity.

Nuclear Fusion and Stellar Energy

Nuclear fusion is the process in which two very light nuclei combine to form a single, heavier, and more stable nucleus. This process also releases a tremendous amount of energy, as the \(BE/A\) of the product is significantly higher than that of the light reactants.

For fusion to occur, the nuclei must overcome a massive electrostatic repulsion (Coulomb barrier) to get close enough for the strong nuclear force to take over. This requires extraordinarily high temperatures (on the order of \(10^7 \text{ K}\)) and high pressure, which is why it is called a thermonuclear reaction.

Example: Fusion of Deuterium and Tritium (D-T reaction)

\[ _{1}^{2}\text{H} + _{1}^{3}\text{H} \longrightarrow _{2}^{4}\text{He} + _{0}^{1}\text{n} + 17.6 \text{ MeV} \]

Stellar Energy (Energy of the Sun)

Fusion is the source of energy in stars, including our Sun. In the core of the Sun, the dominant process is the Proton-Proton (\(p-p\)) cycle, where four hydrogen nuclei (protons) eventually fuse to form a helium nucleus.

The net reaction for the \(p-p\) cycle is:

\[ 4 _{1}^{1}\text{H} \longrightarrow _{2}^{4}\text{He} + 2 _{+1}^{0}\text{e} + 2 \nu + 26.7 \text{ MeV} \]

While fission releases about \(200 \text{ MeV}\) per nucleus and fusion only \(\sim 26 \text{ MeV}\), fusion actually produces far more energy per unit mass. One kilogram of hydrogen undergoing fusion yields significantly more energy than one kilogram of uranium undergoing fission.
