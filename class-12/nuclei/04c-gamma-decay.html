Gamma Decay

Relaxation of excited nuclei

Gamma Decay Mechanism

Following alpha or beta decay, the resulting daughter nucleus is often left in an excited, higher-energy state. Just as electrons in an atom transition from excited states to lower energy states by emitting photons (light/X-rays), nucleons transition to a lower energy state by emitting a highly energetic photon called a gamma (\(\gamma\)) ray.

Since gamma rays are massless photons, the emission of a gamma ray does not alter the mass number \(A\) or the atomic number \(Z\) of the nucleus. It merely lowers its energy state.

\[ _{Z}^{A}\text{X}^* \longrightarrow _{Z}^{A}\text{X} + \gamma \]

Where \(X^*\) denotes an excited state of the nucleus.

Energy Levels of Nucleus

Nuclei, like atoms, have discrete, quantized energy levels. The energy differences between nuclear states are typically on the order of MeV (Mega electron-volts), which is why the emitted gamma photons are of very high energy and short wavelength.

The energy of the emitted gamma ray is given by:

\[ E_\gamma = h\nu = E_i - E_f \]

Where:

  • \(E_\gamma\) = Energy of the gamma photon
  • \(h\) = Planck's constant
  • \(\nu\) = Frequency of the gamma ray
  • \(E_i\) = Initial excited energy state
  • \(E_f\) = Final, lower energy state (often the ground state)

Example: Decay of Cobalt-60

\(^{60}_{27}\text{Co}\) undergoes beta decay to form \(^{60}_{28}\text{Ni}^*\) in an excited state. The Nickel-60 nucleus then emits two successive gamma rays of energies \(1.17 \text{ MeV}\) and \(1.33 \text{ MeV}\) to reach its stable ground state.

Internal Conversion

Internal conversion is an alternative process to gamma emission. In some excited nuclei, instead of emitting a gamma photon, the excitation energy is directly transferred electromagnetically to one of the atom's orbital electrons (typically from the K shell).

This electron is then ejected from the atom with significant kinetic energy. The kinetic energy \(K\) of the ejected electron is:

\[ K = \Delta E - E_b \]

Where \(\Delta E\) is the transition energy of the nucleus and \(E_b\) is the binding energy of the electron in its orbit. Internal conversion is more probable in heavy nuclei and for transitions between states with a small energy difference.

Note that internal conversion electrons are distinct from beta particles. Beta particles originate from the transformation of nucleons inside the nucleus and have a continuous spectrum, while internal conversion electrons originate from atomic orbitals and have discrete, specific kinetic energies.
