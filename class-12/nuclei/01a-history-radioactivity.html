History of Radioactivity

When matter started talking on its own.

From X-rays to Radioactivity

Before radioactivity was discovered, scientists had already begun encountering mysterious beams and radiations while studying matter. But there were different experimental traditions for studying them.

Scientists working with light and optical radiation had developed one kind of experimental toolkit: mirrors, prisms, spectrometers, photographic plates and other methods for studying reflection, refraction, interference, polarization, fluorescence and related optical effects.

Scientists studying charged particles and electrical discharges had a rather different toolkit: discharge tubes, electric and magnetic fields, electrodes and methods of studying how beams were deflected or accelerated.

The same word — ray — did not tell you what the thing actually was. The important question was: Is this a beam of matter, or is this electromagnetic radiation? The answer had to come from how the unknown phenomenon behaved in experiments.

This distinction became particularly important in those days. J. J. Thomson studied the mysterious cathode rays produced in discharge tubes. By studying their deflection in electric and magnetic fields and their charge-to-mass ratio, he showed in 1897, that cathode rays were actually beams of charged particles — electrons.

In 1895, Rontgen, while working with a cathode-ray tube, noticed another completely unexpected effect. A nearby fluorescent screen was glowing even though the cathode rays themselves were confined inside the tube.

Some new radiation was evidently escaping from the tube. Rontgen called it X-rays. But what were X-rays? Were they another kind of particle beam, or were they some new form of electromagnetic radiation? The natural response was to study their physical behaviour.

Scientists tried to investigate them using the methods they already knew from the study of light and radiation — how they interacted with matter, whether they could be reflected or refracted, whether they showed polarization, and so on. The nature of X-rays was eventually established as electromagnetic radiation.

This discovery immediately attracted scientists who were already experts in the study of light and its interaction with matter. One of them was Henri Becquerel, who had been studying phenomena such as fluorescence and phosphorescence.

Becquerel naturally wondered whether there could be a connection between the phosphorescence of certain materials and the newly discovered X-rays. He therefore investigated uranium salts, which were known to phosphoresce.

His original idea was roughly:

\[ \text{sunlight} \rightarrow \text{phosphorescence} \rightarrow \text{X-rays} \]

He placed uranium salts near a photographic plate protected from ordinary light, expecting that sunlight would first excite the material and that the resulting radiation might then affect the plate.

But then something completely unexpected happened. The plate was affected even when the uranium salt had not been exposed to sunlight. The phenomenon therefore could not simply be ordinary phosphorescence followed by the emission of X-rays.

The uranium was producing penetrating radiation by itself.
No external optical excitation (like exposure to sunlight) was required.
This was a completely new physical phenomenon: \[ \boxed{\text{matter}\rightarrow\text{spontaneous radiation}} \]

Becquerel had therefore discovered a new kind of behaviour of matter. But the photographic plate was only a detector. It told him that something had happened; it was not a convenient way to continuously measure how much radiation was being produced.

This is where Marie Curie became crucial. Instead of relying on the photographic effect, she looked for a way to turn the radiation into a measurable physical quantity.

Curie's Solution

Becquerel had detected the mysterious radiation using a photographic plate. But the photographic method was slow: it told us that radiation had produced an effect, but it was not a convenient way to continuously measure and compare the strength of the radiation.

Marie Curie approached the problem differently. She made use of another effect of the radiation: it could ionize air.

When the radiation passes through air, it produces charged particles, making the air slightly conducting. Thus an electrically charged system can slowly discharge through the ionized air. By measuring this electrical discharge with a sensitive electrometer, Curie could obtain a quantitative measure of the radiation.

\[ \boxed{\text{radiation}\rightarrow\text{ionization of air}\rightarrow\text{electrical measurement}} \]

This was a major change in the way radioactivity could be studied. Instead of merely asking "Is there radiation?", Curie could now ask: "How much radiation is being produced?"

She compared different uranium compounds and found something remarkable. The amount of radioactivity depended on the amount of uranium present, not on the particular chemical compound in which the uranium occurred. So, changing the chemical arrangement around uranium did not fundamentally change its radioactivity.

\[ \boxed{\text{chemical composition}\;\not\rightarrow\;\text{radioactivity}} \]

This suggested that the source of the radiation was deeper than chemistry. Radioactivity was not a property of a particular molecule or compound. It was associated with the nucleus of the radioactive atom. Therefore the origin of radioactivity must lie below the level of chemistry. It must be a property of the atomic nucleus.

Curie then asked the natural next question: is uranium the only element whose nuclei are radioactive?

Her measurements showed that thorium was radioactive as well. She gave this newly recognized phenomenon the name radioactivity.

Now the same quantitative method led to an even more remarkable observation. Some uranium-containing minerals, such as pitchblende, were much more radioactive than the amount of uranium present could explain.

But Curie already knew something important: uranium's radioactivity was an intrinsic nuclear property and did not depend strongly on its chemical form. So the extra activity could not simply be explained by saying that the uranium in pitchblende was somehow behaving differently because of its chemical environment.

The measurement was showing more radioactivity than the known uranium could account for.
Since the chemical form of uranium could not explain the excess,something else radioactive had to be present. - a different nucleus.

This turned radioactivity itself into a tool for discovering new matter. Marie Curie and Pierre Curie began chemically separating the components of pitchblende and repeatedly measuring the radioactivity of the separated fractions. Whenever a fraction was more radioactive than expected, it indicated that the source of the radioactivity had been concentrated there.

Through this process they discovered two new radioactive elements: polonium and radium.

Thus, what began with Becquerel's unexpected photographic observation had therefore become a powerful method of investigating the hidden structure of matter. Radioactivity itself had become a probe of the nucleus.

Deconstructing the Radiation: \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), and \(\gamma\)

Once radioactive substances like radium were isolated, physicists asked the natural operational question: Was this radiation homogeneous, and was it matter or wave?

Ernest Rutherford (1899) and Paul Villard (1900) subjected the radiation emitted from a lead collimator to intense electric and magnetic fields, as well as absorbing sheets of varying thickness:

\[ \boxed{\text{Radioactive Beam} \xrightarrow{\mathbf{E}\text{ and }\mathbf{B}\text{ fields}} \text{Three distinct components}} \]

The radiation split cleanly into three behaviors:

  • \(\alpha\)-rays (Alpha): Deflected weakly toward the negative plate; positively charged, heavy particles with high ionizing power but low penetration (stopped by a sheet of paper). Rutherford later proved they are doubly ionized helium ions (\(\text{He}^{2+}\)).
  • \(\beta\)-rays (Beta): Deflected strongly toward the positive plate; negatively charged, highly energetic electrons (\(e^-\)) with moderate penetration and moderate ionizing power.
  • \(\gamma\)-rays (Gamma): Completely undeflected by electric or magnetic fields; neutral, extremely penetrating electromagnetic radiation of wavelength much shorter than X-rays.
The Transmutation of Elements:
In 1902–1903, Ernest Rutherford and Frederick Soddy arrived at a radical conclusion: radioactive decay is not an atom shedding stored energy like a cooling stone; it is an element spontaneously transforming into an entirely different element by ejecting charged constituents.
This established the fundamental conservation rule governing all nuclear disintegrations: \[ \boxed{\sum Z_{\text{initial}} = \sum Z_{\text{final}} \quad \text{and} \quad \sum A_{\text{initial}} = \sum A_{\text{final}}} \]
