Dispersion

Wavelength-dependent refraction and separation of light into its constituent colors.

Nature of Color

Color corresponds to the wavelength of visible light. Different wavelengths within the visible spectrum are perceived as different colors.

Within the visible range, red light has the largest wavelength and violet light has the shortest:

\[ \lambda_r > \lambda_v \]

This range of wavelengths (or frequencies) is called the visible spectrum.

White light is a mixture of all visible wavelengths, while black corresponds to the absence of visible light.

The Mechanism of Color

For non-luminous objects, perceived color arises due to selective absorption. An object appears a certain color because it reflects specific wavelengths of light while absorbing others.
Example: Chlorophyll appears green because it reflects green light and absorbs red and blue.

For self-luminous objects, color depends on the emission mechanism.
Example: A red-hot iron emits due to temperature (thermal radiation), while a sodium vapor lamp emits characteristic yellow light due to atomic transitions. A very easily accessible method in inorganic chemistry, for salt analysis, is to do flame test.

A prism is commonly used as a device to separate wavelengths already present in white light.
This principle is used in spectrometry and in the study of atomic spectra.

Dispersion using a Prism

White light consists of different wavelengths traveling in the same direction. When it passes through a prism, each wavelength experiences a different refractive index.

The refractive index of any transparent material depends on the wavelength (\(\lambda\)) of light according to Cauchy's Formula

\[ \mu (\lambda) = \mu_0 + \frac{A}{\lambda^2} \]

Where \(A\) is small positive constant called Cauchy's constant. Thus \(\mu \propto 1/\lambda\). As \(\lambda_r > \lambda_v\), \(\mu_r \lt \mu_v\).

Now, as angle of deviation depends on refractive index (assuming thin prism), different wavelengths will deviate by different amounts and emerge in different directions.

\[ \delta = A \cdot (\mu - 1) \]
Dispersion is the phenomenon in which different wavelengths of light are deviated by different amounts due to the wavelength dependence of refractive index.
\[ \delta_v = A \cdot (\mu_v - 1) \] \[ \delta_r = A \cdot (\mu_r - 1) \]

In general, shorter wavelengths (violet) have higher refractive index and hence deviate more than longer wavelengths (red).

\[ \mu_v > \mu_r \implies \delta_v > \delta_r \]

From rarer to denser, deviation is towards normal. So, violet will be closer to normal than red.

From denser to rarer, deviation is away from normal. So, red will be closer to normal than violet.

For critical angle, \(c_r\) and \(c_v\). As \(\mu_v > \mu_r\) and \(c \propto 1/\mu\)

\[ c_v \lt c_r \]

As a result, in case of refraction from denser to rarer medium, if incident angle is being increased, violet will undergo TIR before red.

No Dispersion by Glass Slab

A glass slab also produces wavelength-dependent refraction at each surface. However, since the two faces are parallel, the angular separation produced at the first surface is exactly cancelled at the second surface.

Thus, all wavelengths emerge parallel to each other, similar to the original incident ray, resulting only in a small lateral shift and no observable dispersion.

In a prism, the two refracting surfaces are inclined.
The angular separation produced at the first surface is not cancelled at the second surface—instead, the second refraction increases the separation.
Thus, dispersion is reinforced rather than cancelled.

Angular Dispersion

The difference in deviation between extreme colors (violet and red) is called angular dispersion.

\[ \theta = \delta_v - \delta_r \]

For a thin prism:

\[ \delta_v = A(\mu_v - 1), \quad \delta_r = A(\mu_r - 1) \] \[ \boxed{\theta = A(\mu_v - \mu_r)} \]
Dispersion arises because deviation depends on refractive index, and refractive index depends on wavelength.

Dispersive Power

Dispersive power is the ratio of angular dispersion to the mean deviation (usually taken for yellow light, which represents the average wavelength).

\[ \omega = \frac{\theta}{\delta_y} = \frac{\delta_v - \delta_r}{\delta_y} \] \[ \boxed{\omega = \frac{\mu_v - \mu_r}{\mu_y - 1}} \]

It is a dimensionless property of the material and depends only on how refractive index varies with wavelength, not on the prism angle.

If \(\mu_y\) is not given, it is often approximated as:

\[ \mu_y \approx \frac{\mu_v + \mu_r}{2} \]

This is an approximation valid when dispersion is small. In general, refractive index varies nonlinearly with wavelength.

Combination of Prisms

By combining two thin prisms of different materials and opposite orientations, we can control deviation and dispersion independently.

  1. Dispersion without Deviation (Direct Vision Spectroscope):

    Condition: net deviation of mean ray is zero

    \[ A(\mu_y - 1) + A'(\mu'_y - 1) = 0 \]

    Different wavelengths still emerge at different angles, producing dispersion without overall deviation.

  2. Deviation without Dispersion (Achromatic Prism):

    Condition: net angular dispersion is zero

    \[ A(\mu_v - \mu_r) + A'(\mu'_v - \mu'_r) = 0 \]

    All wavelengths deviate equally, eliminating color separation.
