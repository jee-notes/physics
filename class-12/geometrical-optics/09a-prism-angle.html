Prism Angle

Prism Angle and its impact on change.

What is Prism Angle?

To understand the prism angle, we first need to understand what makes a prism a distinct optical element.

A prism is an expanse of an optical medium bounded by two plane surfaces that are inclined to each other rather than parallel. The angle between these two surfaces is called the prism angle, denoted by \(A\).

If the two surfaces are parallel, then \(A=0^\circ\), as in a glass slab.
A prism is distinguished from a slab by the fact that its two refracting surfaces are non-parallel, so \(A\neq0^\circ\).

This non-parallel geometry is what makes a prism an important optical element. If the two surfaces were parallel, the ray would undergo refraction at the first surface and then an equal and opposite angular change at the second surface. The net deviation would therefore be zero, as in a glass slab.

Prisms are used for dispersion, which has important applications in spectroscopy. We will later see how spectroscopy of the hydrogen spectrum helped in the development of our understanding of atomic structure.
We can also exploit the prism angle to control the angle of incidence at the second surface and use the prism in TIR mode to redirect the ray. This principle is used in optical instruments such as telescopes.

Impact of Prism Angle

We know that for prism angle \(A\) and interior angles \(r_1\) and \(r_2\):

\[ \boxed{A = r_1 + r_2} \]

We now examine how changing the prism angle \(A\) affects the ray, while keeping the angle of incidence \(i\) at the first surface constant. Since \(i\) is constant, \(r_1\) also remains constant.

From\( r_2=A-r_1 \) an increase in \(A\) therefore causes \(r_2\) to increase. Since \(r_2\) is the angle of incidence at the second surface, the angle of emergence \(e\) also increases.

For a particular value of \(A\), the emergent ray reaches grazing emergence, for which \( e=90^\circ \)

TIR is already a known phenomenon.
The usefulness of a prism is that its angle \(A\) provides a geometric means of controlling the incidence angle at its second surface, allowing the prism to be deliberately designed to operate in the TIR regime.
