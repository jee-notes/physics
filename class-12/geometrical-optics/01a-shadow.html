Shadow Formation

Shadow formation explained by ray model.

Shadow Region

A shadow is a three-dimensional volume in space where rays from a source are obstructed. When this volume is intercepted by a 2D surface (a screen), it forms a Shadow Patch on the surface.

In the ray model, this corresponds to regions where no rays from the source are able to propagate as the line-of-sight is blocked.

In the ray model, shadow formation is the "inverse" of image formation. While images are formed by the presence and intersection of rays, shadows are defined by their absence.

Shadow Formation

Shadow formation, like image formation, is analyzed using ray tracing. However, the objective is different:

  • Image formation: Determine where rays meet or appear to meet.
  • Shadow formation: Determine where the rays are blocked.

To analyze shadows, we use the concept of boundary rays.

Boundary Rays: These are the extreme rays that just graze the edges of the obstructing object. They separate illuminated and shadow regions.

The use of boundary rays follows from a key property of a beam: rays belonging to the same beam propagate independently and form a continuous family. The geometry of the beam is completely determined by its boundary rays, making it sufficient to analyze only these extreme rays.

This is analogous to streamlines in steady flow, where the flow region is bounded by extreme streamlines, and no flow crosses these boundaries.

Boundary rays define the envelope of a beam. All other rays lie within this envelope and do not affect its boundary.

Types of Shadow

For extended sources, shadow formation leads to two distinct regions:

  • Umbra: Region where light from the source is completely blocked. No rays from the source reach this region.
  • Penumbra: Region where light from only a part of the source reaches. Some rays are blocked, while others are not.

For a point source, only a sharp shadow (umbra) is formed, since all rays originate from a single point.

The existence of penumbra is a direct consequence of the source being extended (a system of point sources).
We only have point source shadows for exam.

Geometric Interpretation

Shadow formation can be understood as a mapping of rays from the source through the edges of the object.

The edges of the object act as boundaries that block certain rays while allowing others to pass.

Thus, shadow formation is governed by:

  • Geometry of the source
  • Geometry of the obstructing object
  • Relative positions of source, object, and screen
Shadow formation is a consequence of rectilinear propagation of light combined with obstruction.

Illustrations

Ray diagrams for shadow formation are constructed by drawing boundary rays from the edges of the source to the edges of the object.

These rays are called as:

  • Boundary rays
  • Extreme rays
  • Grazing rays

Shadow region or light region are 3D regions and their 2D projections are also called as Patch on the 2D surface.

The Similar Triangle Logic

For a point source, the shadow is always a sharp-edged Umbra. The geometry is governed by the divergence of rays from a single vertex.

Consider a point source \(S\), an object of height \(h_o\) at distance \(d\), and a screen at distance \(D\) from the source. The height of the shadow \(h_s\) is determined by: \[ \frac{h_s}{D} = \frac{h_o}{d} \implies h_s = h_o \left( \frac{D}{d} \right) \]

Velocity of Shadow: If the object moves with velocity \(v_o\) perpendicular to the axis, the shadow patch moves with velocity \(v_s\): \[ v_s = v_o \left( \frac{D}{d} \right) \] The shadow always moves "faster" than the object if the screen is further than the object from the source.
