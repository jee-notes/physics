Geometrical Optics

Modeling light as rays to predict image formation through reflection, refraction and shadow formation.

Conceptual Overview

In classical physics, light is fundamentally an electromagnetic wave. A natural starting point is therefore to model it as a wave, for example: \[ y = A \sin(\omega t + kx) \]

At the scale of human observation (and experience), the wavelength of light is much smaller than the dimensions of typical objects (mirrors, lenses, apertures). Consequently, wave effects such as interference and diffraction are negligible.

In this limit, light can be modeled as energy propagating along straight lines called rays.

Ray optics is a geometric approximation in which light is treated as energy traveling in straight lines. It ignores interference, diffraction, polarization and focuses on geometric rules governing the path of light.
At this scale, the underlying nature of light (wave or particle) does not affect the predictions of the model.
Phenomena such as interference and polarization, however, necessarily require a wave description.

This approximation remains valid as long as variations in the medium or obstacles occur over length scales much larger than the wavelength of light.

History of nature of Light

In classical physics, the nature of light was debated between two models: the corpuscular (particle) model and the wave model. The corpuscular model, successfully explained phenomena such as reflection and refraction (by Newton).

Huygens had proposed wave nature of light, but it did not gain acceptance initially—not due to decisive physical refutation, but because Newton’s dominance led to the particle model being favored.

Even though, sound was already understood as a wave phenomenon, with well-established concepts such as interference and wave propagation. The discovery of interference and diffraction in light later provided strong evidence for its wave nature, and phenomena like polarization could only be explained using wave theory.

This led to the acceptance of light as a wave, culminating in Maxwell’s formulation of light as an electromagnetic wave.

Geometrical optics does not contradict this wave description. Instead, it emerges as a limiting model of wave optics when wavelength effects are negligible. At this scale, it is not necessary to consider whether light is fundamentally a particle or a wave.

The core assumption of geometrical optics is the rectilinear propagation of light.
A ray represents the direction of energy propagation (equivalently, the normal to the wavefront), and a collection of rays forms a beam of light.

Within this framework, optical elements act as geometric transformers of rays. Image formation reduces to determining how incident rays are transformed into emergent rays by these elements.

Despite its simplicity, this model is extremely powerful. Using only geometric laws of reflection and refraction, we can analyze optical systems such as mirrors, lenses, and instruments like telescopes and microscopes.

Ray Diagrams

This chapter is primarily studied using ray diagrams.

A ray diagram is similar in spirit to a Free Body Diagram or a Circuit Diagram—it is a visual model that translates a physical situation into a form that can be studied mathematically.

Just as circuit diagrams use idealized elements (resistors, capacitors, sources), ray diagrams use optical elements (also called as optical device) such as mirrors, lenses, and refracting surfaces.

In a ray diagram, light is represented by rays. Optical elements interact with rays incident on their surface, and this interaction is described as a geometric transformation (such as deviation or dispersion).

For example, parallel rays from sunlight falling on a convex lens converge to a point, while white light incident on a prism is dispersed into its constituent colors.

After interaction, the rays are called emergent rays. Thus, a ray diagram represents how an optical element transforms a system of incident rays into a system of emergent rays.

Solving a ray diagram is therefore a geometric tracing problem: follow the trail of a ray which is a straight line until it encounters an optical element, apply the appropriate transformation, and then continue tracing the resulting ray.

Unlike many topics in electrostatics and electromagnetism, geometrical optics involves minimal use of vector calculus.
The emphasis is on geometric reasoning—constructing ray diagrams and applying the laws of reflection and refraction.
Geometry and trigonometry are the primary tools in this chapter.
Ray tracing essentially means following the straight-line path of a ray and applying the appropriate transformation whenever it encounters an optical element.
