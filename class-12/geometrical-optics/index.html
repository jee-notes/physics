Geometrical Optics

Modeling light as rays to predict image formation through reflection, refraction and shadow formation.

Conceptual Overview

In the domain of classical physics, light is fundamentally an electromagnetic wave. If we want to study light, a natural starting point should be to model it as a wave, for example: \[ y = A \sin(\omega t + kx) \]

But, at the scale of human observation (and experience), the wavelength of light is much smaller than the dimensions of typical objects it interacts with (mirrors, lenses, apertures etc.). Consequently, wave effects such as interference and diffraction are negligible.

Within this limit, light can be modeled as energy propagating along straight lines called rays.

Ray optics is a geometric approximation in which light is treated as energy traveling in straight lines.
The core assumption of geometrical optics is the rectilinear propagation of light.
It ignores interference, diffraction, polarization and focuses on geometric rules governing the path of light.
At this scale, the underlying nature of light (wave or particle) does not affect the predictions of the model.

This approximation remains valid as long as variations in the medium or obstacles occur over length scales much larger than the wavelength of light.

History of Nature of Light

In classical physics, the nature of light was debated between two models: the corpuscular (particle) model and the wave model.

The corpuscular model, proposed by Newton, successfully explained phenomena such as reflection and refraction, and gained widespread acceptance.

Huygens proposed a wave theory of light, but it did not gain immediate acceptance due to the influence of Newton’s authority in the scientific community.

The situation changed with the discovery of interference and diffraction, which provided clear evidence of the wave nature of light. Polarization further confirmed that light must be a transverse wave.

Phenomena such as interference and superposition are characteristic of waves and these were already observed (and studied) in sound.
Classical particles do not exhibit superposition in this manner, which is why these phenomena strongly support the wave model of light. They occupy exclusive volumes and need to be displaced by the new occupant.
Common student doubt is that gases can superposition because a mixture of gases can occupy same volume e.g. air. But gas molecules cannot superposition at microscopic level.
It is similar to liquid solution in which there is no superposition at molecular level.

This led to the acceptance of light as a wave, culminating in Maxwell’s formulation of light as an electromagnetic wave.

Geometrical optics does not contradict this wave description. Instead, it arises as a limiting approximation of wave optics when wavelength effects are negligible.

Within this framework, optical elements (lenses, mirrors, refracting surface boundaries) act as geometric transformers of rays. Geometric transformation means bending (changing direction). No geometric transformation implies rectilinear propagation and line-of-sight travel.

Despite its simplicity, this model is extremely powerful. Using only geometric laws of reflection and refraction, we can analyze optical systems such as mirrors, lenses and instruments like telescopes and microscopes.

Optical equipment like telescopes and microscopes can be modeled as a system of optical elements like lenses and mirrors.
This equipment has been part of path-breaking scientific history.
Galileo invented the telescope and could observe the moons of Jupiter.
The microscope was invented and micro-organisms and cells were observed.
Humans strongly believe that "seeing is believing" and such equipment shattered many existing belief systems.

Ray Diagrams

This chapter is primarily studied using Ray Diagrams.

A Ray Diagram is conceptually similar to a Free Body Diagram or a Circuit Diagram — it is a visual model that translates a physical situation into a form that can be studied mathematically.

Just as circuit diagrams have idealized elements (resistors, capacitors, sources), ray diagrams have optical elements (also called optical devices) such as mirrors, lenses, glass slabs, prisms and refracting surface boundaries.

In a ray diagram, light is represented by rays. Optical elements interact with rays incident on their surface geometrically. This interaction is basically bending the initial straight path with some angle called "deviation".

Just like we study V-I characteristics of a circuit element, we study angles of deviation for optical elements.

The incident ray on the optical element is its input ray. The element transforms the input ray geometrically, and the transformed output ray is called the reflected ray, refracted ray, or emerging ray, depending on the characteristics of the optical element.

Solving a ray diagram is therefore a geometric tracing problem: follow the path of the incident ray (input), which is a straight line until it encounters an optical element, apply the appropriate transformation, and then continue tracing the resulting ray (output).

Unlike many topics in electrostatics and electromagnetism, geometrical optics involves minimal use of vector calculus.
The emphasis is on geometric reasoning — constructing ray diagrams and applying the laws of reflection and refraction.
Geometry and trigonometry are the primary tools in this chapter.
Ray tracing essentially means following the straight-line path of a ray and applying the appropriate transformation whenever it encounters an optical element.

Two Regimes in Ray Optics

Ray optics can be broadly divided into two regimes:

  • Single-Ray Optics
  • Object–Image Optics

In single-ray optics, we track the path of an individual ray. At each point of incidence, the ray is processed using the laws of reflection and refraction with respect to the local normal.

In object–image optics, we do not track a single ray in isolation. Instead, we consider at least two rays originating from a point of the object and incident on the optical element. This can be called as beam optics.

The optical element processes the geometry of these rays — effectively transforming the angular spread (or separation) of the rays associated with the object into a new set of rays corresponding to the image. This is represented through object distance \(u\) and image distance \(v\).

In this approach, we no longer work with local normals at every point. Instead, we work with a global geometric structure defined by the principal axis and restrict ourselves to paraxial rays, where small-angle approximations hold.

This approximation allows us to replace detailed local refraction with simple object–image relations such as:

\[ \frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f} \]

The plane mirror is a special case where no approximation is required, and the object–image relation holds exactly without invoking the paraxial approximation.

Single-ray optics is local and normal-based.
Object–image optics is global and axis-based, built on the transformation of ray geometry under the paraxial approximation.
Even dispersion is beam based optics.
