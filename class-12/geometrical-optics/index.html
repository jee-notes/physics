Geometrical Optics

Modeling light as rays to predict image formation through reflection, refraction and shadow formation.

Conceptual Overview of Ray Optics

Light is fundamentally an electromagnetic wave. However, when the wavelength of light is much smaller than the dimensions of the objects it interacts with (such as mirrors, lenses, and everyday objects), its wave nature becomes less prominent.

In this limit, light can be modeled as traveling in straight lines called rays.

Geometrical Optics (or Ray Optics) is the approximation in which light is treated as traveling in straight lines. It ignores interference, diffraction, and polarization, and focuses purely on geometric rules to determine the path of light.

This approximation is valid when variations in the medium or obstacles occur over length scales much larger than the wavelength of light.

Light in Classical Physics

In classical physics, there was a long-standing debate regarding whether light behaves as a particle or a wave. Initially, light was assumed to consist of particles (called corpuscles), and Newton explained phenomena such as reflection and refraction using this corpuscular model.

On the other hand, sound was understood as a wave phenomenon, with well-established concepts such as interference and wave propagation. The discovery of interference and diffraction in light later provided strong evidence for its wave nature, and phenomena like polarization could only be explained using wave theory.

This led to the acceptance of light as a wave, culminating in Maxwell’s formulation of light as an electromagnetic wave.

Geometrical optics does not contradict this wave description. Instead, it emerges as an approximation of wave optics in the limit of small wavelengths. At this macroscopic level, it is not necessary to consider whether light is fundamentally a particle or a wave.

The core assumption of geometrical optics is the rectilinear propagation of light.
A light ray represents the direction of energy propagation (equivalently, the normal to the wavefront), and a collection of such rays forms a beam of light.

Using this framework, all phenomena in this chapter—reflection from mirrors, refraction through lenses, and dispersion by prisms—can be understood through simple geometric laws governing these rays.

Despite its simplicity, this approach is extremely powerful. With just the laws of reflection and refraction and basic geometry, we can analyze and design optical instruments such as telescopes, microscopes, and cameras, and also understand the working of the human eye.

Ray Diagrams

This chapter is primarily solved using ray diagrams.

A ray diagram is similar in spirit to a Free Body Diagram or a Circuit Diagram—it is a visual model that translates a physical situation into a form that can be analyzed mathematically.

Just as circuit diagrams use idealized elements (resistors, capacitors, sources), ray diagrams use optical elements such as mirrors, lenses, and refracting surfaces.

In a ray diagram, light is represented by rays, and the behavior of these rays is governed by the laws of reflection and refraction.

Thus, a ray diagram reduces the problem of image formation to a problem in geometry—once the correct rays are constructed, the image follows directly from their intersections.

Unlike many topics in electrostatics and electromagnetism, geometrical optics involves minimal use of vector calculus.
The emphasis here is on geometric reasoning—constructing ray diagrams and applying the laws of reflection and refraction — rather than analyzing vector fields.
Geometry, Trigonometry are more important tools in this chapter.
