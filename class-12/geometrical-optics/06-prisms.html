Prisms

Refraction, deviation, and dispersion of light by angular transparent media.

Refraction and Deviation through a Prism

A prism is a wedged-shaped portion of a transparent refracting medium bounded by two plane faces inclined at an angle. The angle between the two refracting faces is called the Angle of Prism (\(A\)).

When a monochromatic ray of light passes through a prism, it suffers refraction at the two inclined faces. Unlike a rectangular glass slab where the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray, a prism bends the emergent ray at a considerable angle relative to the incident direction. This total angle of bending is called the Angle of Deviation (\(\delta\)).

Prism Formula

If a ray is incident at an angle \(i_1\), undergoes refraction at the first face at an angle \(r_1\), then strikes the second face at an angle \(r_2\), and emerges at an angle \(i_2\) (sometimes denoted as \(e\)):

  1. The sum of internal angles of refraction equals the angle of the prism: \[ A = r_1 + r_2 \]
  2. The net angle of deviation (\(\delta\)) is given by: \[ \delta = (i_1 + i_2) - A \]
A prism always bends the ray of light towards its thicker base.

Condition for Minimum Deviation

As the angle of incidence (\(i_1\)) increases, the angle of deviation (\(\delta\)) first decreases, reaches a minimum value called the Angle of Minimum Deviation (\(\delta_m\)), and then begins to increase.

Conditions at Minimum Deviation

The prism is in the position of minimum deviation if and only if the ray of light passes symmetrically through it. This implies:

  • The angle of incidence equals the angle of emergence: \(i_1 = i_2 = i\).
  • The internal angles of refraction are equal: \(r_1 = r_2 = r\).
  • The refracted ray inside the prism travels parallel to the base of the prism (assuming the prism is an isosceles triangle).

Refractive Index from Minimum Deviation

Using the symmetry conditions \(2r = A \Rightarrow r = \frac{A}{2}\) and \(2i = A + \delta_m \Rightarrow i = \frac{A+\delta_m}{2}\), substituting into Snell's Law (\(n = \frac{\sin i}{\sin r}\)) yields the crucial prism formula:

\[ n = \frac{\sin\left(\frac{A + \delta_m}{2}\right)}{\sin\left(\frac{A}{2}\right)} \]

Where \(n\) is the refractive index of the prism material relative to the surrounding medium.

Thin Prism Approximation

For a thin prism (where angle \(A\) is very small, typically \(< 10^\circ\)), the angles \(i, r\) and \(\delta\) are also small. The sine of small angles can be approximated by the angle itself (\(\sin \theta \approx \theta\)). The deviation formula simplifies to a linear relationship independent of the angle of incidence:

\[ \delta = A \cdot (n - 1) \]

Dispersion of Light and Dispersive Power

While a monochromatic ray only deviates, white light consists of multiple wavelengths. The refractive index of any transparent material depends on the wavelength (\(\lambda\)) of light according to Cauchy's Formula (\(n = A + \frac{B}{\lambda^2} + \dots\)). Therefore, violet light (shortest visible \(\lambda\)) experiences a higher refractive index than red light (longest visible \(\lambda\)).

Because deviation \(\delta\) depends on \(n\) (\(\delta = A(n-1)\)), different colors deviate by different angles. The splitting of white light into its constituent colors is called Dispersion.

Angular Dispersion (\(\theta\))

The difference in the angles of deviation of the extreme colors (violet and red) is the angular dispersion.

\[ \theta = \delta_v - \delta_r \]

For a thin prism: \( \delta_v = A(n_v - 1) \) and \( \delta_r = A(n_r - 1) \). Therefore:

\[ \theta = A(n_v - n_r) \]

Dispersive Power (\(\omega\))

The dispersive power of a prism material is the ratio of the angular dispersion to the mean deviation (usually taken for yellow light, \(\delta_y\)). It is a dimensionless, intrinsic property of the material alone, independent of the prism angle \(A\).

\[ \omega = \frac{\theta}{\delta_y} = \frac{\delta_v - \delta_r}{\delta_y} \] \[ \omega = \frac{n_v - n_r}{n_y - 1} \]

Combination of Prisms

By combining two thin prisms of different materials (e.g., Crown glass and Flint glass) and oppositely oriented angles (\(A\) and \(A'\)), we can achieve specific optical effects: canceling deviation while keeping dispersion, or canceling dispersion while keeping deviation.

  1. Dispersion without Deviation (Direct Vision Spectroscope): We want the net deviation of the mean (yellow) ray to be zero: \(\delta_{y} + \delta'_{y} = 0\). \[ A(n_y - 1) + A'(n'_y - 1) = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad A' = -A \left[ \frac{n_y - 1}{n'_y - 1} \right] \] Under this condition, net angular dispersion remains non-zero: \[ \theta_{\text{net}} = (\delta_v - \delta_r) + (\delta'_v - \delta'_r) = \delta_y \cdot \omega + \delta'_y \cdot \omega' = \delta_y (\omega - \omega') \]
  2. Deviation without Dispersion (Achromatic Prism): We want the net angular dispersion to be zero: \(\theta + \theta' = 0\). \[ A(n_v - n_r) + A'(n'_v - n'_r) = 0 \] Under this condition, the net mean deviation remains non-zero: \[ \delta_{\text{net}} = \delta_y \left[ 1 - \frac{\omega}{\omega'} \right] \] This combination is used in achromatic lenses in telescopes and cameras to eliminate chromatic aberration.
