Refraction at Spherical Surfaces

How curved boundaries between media form images.

Refraction Formula for a Spherical Surface

Consider a spherical interface separating two optical media of absolute refractive indices \(n_1\) and \(n_2\). Let the radius of curvature of the spherical surface be \(R\), and its pole be \(P\). If a point object \(O\) is placed on the principal axis in medium \(n_1\) at a distance \(u\) from the pole, refraction at the spherical surface will form an image \(I\) in medium \(n_2\) at a distance \(v\).

The General Refraction Formula

For paraxial rays, the relationship connecting object distance \(u\), image distance \(v\), the refractive indices \(n_1, n_2\), and the radius of curvature \(R\) is:

\[ \frac{n_2}{v} - \frac{n_1}{u} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{R} \]

Where:

  • \(n_1\) = Refractive index of the medium from which light is incident.
  • \(n_2\) = Refractive index of the medium into which light is refracted.
  • \(u\) = Object distance from pole (\(P\)).
  • \(v\) = Image distance from pole (\(P\)).
  • \(R\) = Radius of curvature of the spherical surface.

Crucial: Always apply proper Cartesian sign convention for \(u\), \(v\), and \(R\) when using this formula.

This formula holds true for both convex and concave spherical refracting surfaces, irrespective of whether the object is in the rarer or denser medium, and regardless of whether the image formed is real or virtual.

Lateral and Longitudinal Magnification

Just like spherical mirrors, refraction at a spherical surface changes the size of the object, depending on its position and the radii.

Lateral (Transverse) Magnification (\(m\))

Lateral magnification is the ratio of the height of the image (\(h_i\)) perpendicular to the principal axis to the corresponding height of the object (\(h_o\)).

\[ m = \frac{h_i}{h_o} \]

Using similar triangles from the ray diagram of refraction at a curved surface, the expression for lateral magnification is derived as:

\[ m = \left(\frac{n_1}{n_2}\right) \left(\frac{v}{u}\right) \]

Interpreting \(m\):

  • A positive magnification (\(m > 0\)) implies an erect image (usually virtual for single surfaces).
  • A negative magnification (\(m < 0\)) implies an inverted image (usually real).

Longitudinal Magnification (\(m_L\))

If an object lies along the principal axis with a small finite length \(du\), its longitudinal image size will be \(dv\). The longitudinal magnification is defined as:

\[ m_L = \frac{dv}{du} \]

To find \(m_L\), we differentiate the general refraction formula with respect to \(u\):

\[ \frac{d}{du}\left(\frac{n_2}{v} - \frac{n_1}{u}\right) = \frac{d}{du}\left(\frac{n_2 - n_1}{R}\right) \] \[ n_2 \left(-\frac{1}{v^2}\frac{dv}{du}\right) - n_1\left(-\frac{1}{u^2}\right) = 0 \] \[ \frac{n_2}{v^2} \frac{dv}{du} = \frac{n_1}{u^2} \]
\[ m_L = \frac{dv}{du} = \left(\frac{n_1}{n_2}\right) \left(\frac{v}{u}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{n_2}{n_1}\right) m^2 \]

Because \( \left(\frac{v}{u}\right)^2 \) and \( \frac{n_1}{n_2} \) are always positive, the longitudinal magnification is always positive. This signifies that the front and rear portions of the object keep their relative order in the image (unlike in plane mirrors where lateral inversion causes 'front' to become 'back').
