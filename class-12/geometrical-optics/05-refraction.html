Refraction of Light

The bending of light when it crosses the boundary between two transparent media.

Refraction Phenomenon

When a beam of light travelling in a medium encounters the boundary of another transparent medium, a part of it is reflected back into the first medium while the rest enters the second medium.

The portion of the incident light that enters the second medium is said to be transmitted. This process is called transmission.

For an obliquely incident ray (\(0^\circ \lt i \lt 90^\circ\)), the direction of propagation changes at the interface. This change in direction is called refraction of light.

Thus, refraction is specifically the change in direction associated with transmission across a boundary.

In addition to reflection and transmission, some energy may be absorbed by the medium. However, in most optical situations, absorption is negligible and is therefore ignored. (It is covered extensively in Thermal Radiation).

Physically, it is energy that is reflected, transmitted or absorbed, but in the ray model we represent this transfer using rays.

Natural phenomena such as the apparent bending of objects in water, rainbow formation and atmospheric effects are all consequences of refraction.

Laws of Refraction

Refraction was experimentally studied by Snell, who established the following laws governing the change in direction of light at the boundary between two media.

When a ray enters a different medium, its direction changes. The ray bends either towards the normal or away from the normal depending on the optical properties of the two media.

Optical Density and Refractive Index

The optical density of a medium is a measure of how much it reduces the speed of light. It is characterized by the absolute refractive index \(\mu\) of the medium:

\[ \mu = \frac{c}{v} \]

Since the speed of light in a medium is always less than or equal to that in vacuum (\(v \le c\)), we have \(\mu \ge 1\). For vacuum (and approximately air), \(\mu = 1\).

A medium with higher refractive index is optically denser, while one with lower refractive index is optically rarer.

When light is transmitted in a different medium, its frequency \(\nu\) does not change as it depends on source.

Since \(v = \lambda \nu\) and frequency \(\nu\) remains unchanged during transmission, the change in speed is reflected in the change in wavelength:

\[ \mu = \frac{\lambda_0}{\lambda_m} \]

where \(\lambda_0\) is the wavelength in vacuum and \(\lambda_m\) is the wavelength in the medium.

The Laws of Refraction

  1. First Law: The incident ray, the refracted ray, and the normal at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.
  2. Second Law (Snell's Law): For a given pair of media and a given wavelength of light, \[ \frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \text{constant} = \mu_{21} \]

Here, \(\mu_1\) is the refractive index of the medium containing the incident ray, and \(\mu_2\) is that of the medium containing the refracted ray.

The constant \(\mu_{21}\) is called the refractive index of medium 2 with respect to medium 1. Experimentally, it is found to be equal to the ratio of the speeds of light in the two media:

\[ \mu_{21} = \frac{v_1}{v_2} = \frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2} \]

Using the definition \(\mu = \frac{c}{v}\), we obtain:

\[ \mu_{21} = \frac{\mu_2}{\mu_1} \]

Thus, Snell’s law can be written in a symmetric form:

\[ \mu_1 \sin i = \mu_2 \sin r \]

Snell’s law determines the magnitude of how the direction of a ray changes when it enters a different medium. The bending occurs because the speed of light changes across the interface.

  • When light travels from a rarer to a denser medium (\(\mu_1 \lt \mu_2\)), its speed decreases, and the ray bends towards the normal.
  • When light travels from a denser to a rarer medium (\(\mu_1 > \mu_2\)), its speed increases, and the ray bends away from the normal.

Thus, refraction can be understood as a change in direction of propagation caused by a change in the speed of light. This change in direction will be better explained in wave optics using Huygens' Wavefront.

Total Internal Reflection (TIR)

When light travels from an optically denser medium to an optically rarer medium, it bends away from the normal. As the angle of incidence (\(i\)) in the denser medium increases, the angle of refraction (\(r\)) in the rarer medium also increases rapidly.

For a certain angle of incidence, called the Critical Angle (\(c\)), the angle of refraction becomes exactly \(90^\circ\). The refracted ray grazes the interface.

Critical Angle

Applying Snell's Law at the interface between denser medium (\(\mu_1\)) and rarer medium (\(\mu_2\)), where \(\mu_1 > \mu_2\): \[ \mu_1 \sin c = \mu_2 \sin 90^\circ \] \[ \sin c = \frac{\mu_2}{\mu_1} \]

If the rarer medium is air (\(\mu_2 = 1\)) and the denser medium has refractive index \(\mu\): \[ \sin c = \frac{1}{\mu} \quad \Rightarrow \quad c = \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\mu}\right) \]

If the angle of incidence is increased further such that \(i > c\), refraction is no longer possible. All the incident light is reflected back into the denser medium. This phenomenon is called Total Internal Reflection (TIR).

Students get confused and ask if \(\sin c = \frac{\mu_1}{\mu_2}\) or \(\sin c = \frac{\mu_2}{\mu_1}\). just use small / big.

Conditions for Total Internal Reflection

TIR occurs only when both of these conditions are met:

  1. The light ray must travel from an optically denser medium to an optically rarer medium.
  2. The angle of incidence (\(i\)) must be greater than the critical angle (\(c\)) for that pair of media (\(i > c\)).

Applications and Natural Phenomena involving TIR

  • Mirage: An optical illusion observed in deserts or hot asphalt during summer. Hot air layers near the ground become rarer (lower \(n\)) than cooler layers above. Light from the sky travels down through layers of decreasing refractive index, bending continuously until it undergoes TIR, creating the illusion of a reflecting pool of water on the ground.
  • Looming: An optical illusion observed in cold areas during winter. Looming is opposite of mirage in terms of bending.
  • Sparkling of Diamond: The refractive index of diamond is very high (\(n \approx 2.42\)), giving it a very small critical angle of about \(24.4^\circ\). Diamonds are cut with multiple faces such that light entering from any face strikes inside faces at angles greater than \(24.4^\circ\), undergoing multiple TIR before exiting, causing the brilliant sparkle. (These numbers for diamond are there in NCERT to be by-hearted)
  • Optical Fibers: Extensively used in modern telecommunications and medicine (endoscopy). They consist of a core with a high refractive index and cladding with a lower refractive index. Light introduced into the core undergoes repeated TIR without significant energy loss, allowing it to travel over long, curved distances.
  • Totally Reflecting Prisms: Used in periscopes and binoculars. Right-angled isosceles prisms made of glass (critical angle \(\approx 42^\circ\)) can turn rays exactly by \(90^\circ\) or \(180^\circ\) effectively using TIR, which is perfectly efficient compared to silvered mirrors that absorb some light.

Why "Total" Internal Reflection?

In general, when light strikes a boundary, part of it is reflected and part is transmitted.

However, for \(i > c\), transmission becomes impossible (since Snell’s law would require \(\sin r > 1\)). As a result, all the incident energy is reflected back into the original medium.

This complete reflection of energy is why the phenomenon is called Total Internal Reflection.

The ratio of transmitted power to reflected power is maximum at normal incidence (\(i = 0\)). As the angle of incidence increases, the ray becomes more oblique, and a larger fraction of the incident energy is reflected.

As \(i\) approaches the critical angle, the transmitted component decreases and tends to zero. For \(i > c\), transmission becomes impossible and the transmitted power is zero.
