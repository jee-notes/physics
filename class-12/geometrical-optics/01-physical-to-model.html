Model of Light

Modeling light as rays to analyze reflection, refraction and image, shadow formation.

Sources of Light

In geometrical optics, light is treated as originating from a source. Sources are classified based on how they emit or reflect light:

  • Self-Luminous: Objects that generate their own light (e.g. Sun, stars, electric bulbs, red-hot iron). Their color depends on the physical mechanism of emission, such as temperature or atomic transitions.
  • Non-Luminous: Objects that do not emit light but become visible by reflecting light from a self-luminous source (e.g. Moon, planets, this page).

The Mechanism of Color

For non-luminous objects, perceived color arises due to selective absorption. An object appears a certain color because it reflects specific wavelengths of light while absorbing others.
Example: Chlorophyll appears green because it reflects green light and absorbs red and blue.

For self-luminous objects, color depends on the emission mechanism.
Example: A red-hot iron emits due to temperature (thermal radiation), while a sodium vapor lamp emits characteristic yellow light due to atomic transitions.

In modeling, sources are idealized as point sources (dimensionless) or extended sources (a system of point sources).
Ray diagrams typically begin by considering light originating from a point on the source.

Rays and Beams

A Ray is a straight line representing the direction of propagation of light energy.

A Beam is a system of rays.

As a more fuller physical model, light is an EM wave having amplitude, wavelength, velocity etc. But the ray model only deals with direction of energy propagation.

As a result, rays are modeled as straight-line directions and are represented using unit vectors (in vector form), as only the direction of propagation is relevant, and not the magnitude.

  • Parallel Beam: Rays are parallel to each other. Corresponds to light arriving from a very distant source.
  • Converging Beam: Rays meet at a specific point.
  • Diverging Beam: Rays spread out from a point.

This classification is geometric and depends on the angular spread of the rays in the beam. But, its operational use as a tool is similar to field lines or streamline in fluid flow. Diverging beam can be modelled as "originating" from a point source and converging beam can be modelled as "terminating" at a point.

However, the rays might continue beyond the "originating" or "terminating" point ( depending on given physical conditions ) but we can say that the beam itself originated or terminated.

The ray model can be summarized as rectilinear propagation of light.
This is why the subject is called Ray Optics or Geometrical Optics—light is analyzed by tracing straight-line paths of rays - by using geometry.

Ray diagram

A ray diagram is a system diagram comprising of beams ( rays ), optical elements and objects and images. It is a visual representation of an optics system similar to how a circuit diagram is a visual representation of an electric system. Just like a potentiometer is an electric system, a microscope is an optics system.

Objects and Images

Objects and images are elements of a ray diagram. They are points where diverging beam originate or converging beam terminate.

These originating or terminating points can be either an object or an image. It depends on the interacting optical element.

Relation between Object and Optical Element

An optical element like a lens, mirror, prism etc. interacts with incident rays that originate from an object and transforms them and the transformed emerging rays terminate at the image.

This mechanism is known as ray tracing. All transformations done by the optical element occur at point of incidence.

In geometrical optics, whether an object or image is real or virtual depends entirely on the behavior of the incident and emergent rays.

Type Incident Rays (Approaching Optical Element) Emergent Rays (Leaving Optical Element)
Real Diverging from a point Converging to a point
Virtual Converging toward a point Diverging from a point

Key Definitions:

  • Object: The point of actual or apparent intersection of incident rays.
  • Image: The point of actual or apparent intersection of emergent rays.
Rays will actually meet at a point if incident rays diverge and emergent rays converge.
If incident rays converge or emerging rays diverge, then we need to backtrace the rays to make them meet.
The backtracing needs to be done with reference to the optical element.

Point Object vs. Extended Object

An extended object can be viewed as a system of point objects, each emitting its own set of rays.

A point source, in isolation, emits light in all directions (a solid angle of \(4\pi\)). However, when a point is part of an extended object, the rays reaching an observer are restricted by the geometry of the object and possible obstructions.

This is why we cannot see the back of our head in a mirror placed in front of us—the rays from those points do not reach the mirror and hence do not contribute to image formation.

Image formation for an extended object is determined by analyzing the image formed by each point and preserving the geometric relationships between them.

Thus, the image of an extended object is a geometrically transformed system of image points corresponding to the system of object points.

Ray diagram Example

A ray diagram may comprise of a system of optical elements. Rays are incident or emergent with respect to an optical element. For a system of optical elements, emerging rays from one optical element can be incident rays for other. In such cases image of one optical element becomes object for next optical element.

Often students incorrectly memorize that "object is for diverging" or "image is for converging". Its the behavior of rays that determines everything. For any optical element (in ray diagram), the object is defined by the intersection of incident rays, and the image is defined by the intersection of emergent rays.

Convex Lens L followed by plane mirror M. Light rays from source O fall on convex lens, converge and fall on mirror M.

Solution

For Lens (L):

  • Rays from O are diverging and incident on the lens.
  • Hence, O is a real object for the lens.
  • After refraction, rays are converging .
  • Hence, I₁ is real image for the lens.
  • Even if extended forward, its a real image for Lens as rays are converging after refraction. It doesnt matter where the screen is.
A real image is defined by converging emergent rays, not by whether the rays are physically allowed to meet.

For Mirror (M):

  • Rays incident on the mirror are converging toward I₁.
  • Hence, I₁ is a virtual object for the mirror.
  • After reflection, rays converge to I₂.
  • Hence, I₂ is a real image formed by the mirror.
Our eyes are designed as an optical instrument to see real objects for the human eye.
Hence, even if a plane mirror makes virtual image of real objects, we can see the image as it is real object for human eye.
