Model of Light

Modeling light as rays to analyze reflection, refraction, and image and shadow formation.

Main Model

In a more complete physical description, light is an electromagnetic wave with amplitude, wavelength, phase, energy, etc.

In lower classes, we learned that light is a form of energy.
To describe it more physically, light is an electromagnetic wave — an oscillation of electric and magnetic fields.
Unlike mechanical waves, it does not require a material medium.
The energy of light is stored in and carried by the electromagnetic field itself.

However, in geometrical optics, only the direction of propagation of light is relevant. We consider the direction of propagation of light and disregard its wave nature, assuming that light propagates in straight lines called rays.

Thus, this branch of optics where light is simplified as rays is called ray optics or geometrical optics, as rays themselves are defined geometrically (straight-line direction of propagation of light).

This allows us to formulate the laws of optics in the language of geometry:

  1. Law of Rectilinear Propagation of Light: It states that light propagates in a homogeneous medium in straight lines.
  2. Law of Independence of Light Rays: It states that rays do not disturb each other upon intersection.
  3. The Law of Reversibility of Light: It states that if a ray of light, after suffering a series of reflections and refractions, has its path reversed at any instant, then the ray retraces its path back to the source.
  4. Law of Reflection: Will be studied further in detail in this chapter.
  5. Law of Refraction: Will be studied further in detail in this chapter.
Thus, rays as such have no physical existence of their own.
A ray is just a direction.

Sources of Light

Physically, light is treated as originating from a source. This is true for ray optics as well as wave optics. Sources are classified based on whether they emit or reflect light:

  • Self-Luminous: Objects that generate their own light (e.g. Sun, stars, electric bulbs, red-hot iron). Their color depends on the physical mechanism of emission, such as temperature or atomic transitions.
  • Non-Luminous: Objects that do not emit light but become visible by reflecting light from a self-luminous source (e.g. Moon, planets, a page of a book).

In geometrical optics, sources are idealized as point sources (dimensionless) or extended sources (a system of point sources).

Object-image formulae are derived for point sources and are extended to other cases by considering extended sources as a collection of point sources.

In a ray diagram, whenever a ray is drawn, it is assumed to originate from a point source, even if not shown explicitly.

Ray Diagram

A ray diagram is a system diagram comprising rays, optical elements, objects, and images. It is a visual representation of the entire geometrical optics branch.

Optical Elements

The main heroes of a ray diagram are optical elements. They are similar to circuit elements like resistors, capacitors and inductors in a circuit diagram.

They interact with input rays (called incident rays) and bend their path geometrically to produce output rays.

Optical elements in our syllabus are:

  • Mirror: Plane mirror and spherical mirror. These reflect the incident rays, and their output ray is called the reflected ray.
  • Lens: Convex lens and concave lens. These converge or diverge the incident rays and are used in image formation.
  • Glass Slab and Prism: These are different optical elements but are clubbed together here because both involve sequential refraction. Both transform the incident rays differently, but their output ray is called the emerging ray.
  • Refracting Boundary: Plane boundary and spherical boundary. These refract the incident rays, and the incident and refracted rays exist in different media.

Objects and Images

Objects and images are elements of a ray diagram. An object (or an image) is a point from which rays diverge or a point towards which rays converge.

An optical element interacts with incident rays (input) associated with an object and transforms them into emergent rays (output) associated with an image. This means that whether a point of convergence (or divergence) is an object or an image depends on the optical element with respect to which it is defined.

  • Object: The point of intersection of incident rays (actual or apparent).
  • Image: The point of intersection of emergent rays (actual or apparent).
This is related to how our eyes "see" things.
Our eyes are optical instruments and contain a converging convex lens which forms an image on the retina.
Thus, in reality, all that is visible is actually an image of the physical world formed by our eyes.
We are not actually looking at objects and everything that is "seen" is an image.

Real / Virtual Objects and Images

Whether an object or image is real or virtual depends entirely on the behavior of the incident and emergent rays.

Type Incident Rays - OBJECT Emergent Rays - IMAGE
Real Diverging from a point Converging to a point
Virtual Converging toward a point Diverging from a point
If rays actually meet, the point is real.
If rays meet only when extended, the point is virtual but still the object / image can be real.
All constructions must be interpreted with respect to the optical element.

Point Object vs. Extended Object

An extended object can be viewed as a system of point objects, each associated with a set of rays.

A point source is a physical emitter of light and radiates in all directions (a solid angle of \(4\pi\)).

A point object, however, is a geometrical concept: it is defined as a point from which incident rays originate (or appear to originate) with respect to an optical element.

Thus, a point object may be:

  • a real source (self-luminous),
  • a reflecting point on a non-luminous object, or
  • a virtual point formed by another optical element.

In practice, although a point source emits light in all directions, only a subset of rays contributes to image formation. This subset is determined by geometry and obstructions in the optical system.

For example, we cannot see the back of our head in a mirror placed in front of us because rays from those points do not reach the mirror. Another example is that even if we capture the image of the entire visible face of the Sun, we are not capturing all the light energy emitted by it — only the rays that reach the optical system contribute to image formation.

Image Formation of Extended Objects

Image formation for an extended object is determined by analyzing the image formed by each point object and preserving the geometric relationships between them.

The image of an extended object is a geometrically transformed system of image points corresponding to the system of object points.

Ray diagram Example

A ray diagram may comprise of a system of optical elements. Rays are incident or emergent with respect to an optical element. For a system of optical elements, emerging rays from one optical element can be incident rays for other. In such cases image of one optical element becomes object for next optical element.

Often students incorrectly memorize that "object is for diverging" or "image is for converging".
Its the role of intersecting rays that determines everything.
The object is defined as the intersection of incident rays (on the optical element), and the image is defined by the intersection of emergent rays (from the optical element).

Convex Lens L followed by plane mirror M. Light rays from source O fall on convex lens, converge and fall on mirror M.

Solution

Here there are 2 optical elements:

  1. Convex Lens L: This will converge the rays incident on it.
  2. Plane Mirror M: This will parallely reflect the rays incident on it.

O is point source. It will act as the first source of rays for the optical system.

Our objective is to trace the image of O. This means that O will also act as a point object here.

To trace a point object - to its image, we need minimum 2 rays from the same point.

In this problem, due to placement, an image of the the point source will first be formed by the lens, then the mirror. So, we send 2 rays from point O to be incident on lens L

For Lens (L):

At the location of lens (L), it got the 2 rays from O incident on it. Now (L) does not physically go at the location of O to check. It processes that the incoming incident rays are diverging.

Hence, O is a real object for the lens.

At the location of lens (L) itself, immediately after refraction, the emergent rays are converging.

Hence, I₁ is real image for the lens even if I₁ point is virtual. Its because it is determined by the optical element (Lens in this case). We can say that:

  • Object O is real as incident rays are diverging.
  • Image I₁ is real as emergent rays are converging.

It doesnt matter where the screen to capture the image is placed.

A real image is defined by converging emergent rays at the optical element, and not by whether the rays are physically allowed to meet.

For Mirror (M):

At the location of mirror (M), it got the 2 rays from virtual point I₁ incident on it. Now (M) does not physically go at the location of I₁ to check. It processes that the incoming incident rays are converging.

Hence, I₁ is a virtual object for the mirror.

At the location of mirror (M) itself, immediately after reflection, the emergent rays are converging.

Hence, I₂ is real image for the mirror. We can say that:

  • Object I₁ is virtual as incident rays are converging.
  • Image I₂ is real as emergent rays are converging.
A real image is not defined by whether it is actually captured on a screen, but by whether the rays actually converge. Such an image can be captured on a screen if placed appropriately.
What our eyes see is the real image formed on the retina by the convex lens of the eye.
Hence, even if a plane mirror forms a virtual image, we can see it because the rays from the mirror enter the eye and are focused to form a real image on the retina.
If an object is brought very close to the eye, the angular spread of rays becomes too large, and the lens can no longer form a sharp image on the retina, resulting in blur.
