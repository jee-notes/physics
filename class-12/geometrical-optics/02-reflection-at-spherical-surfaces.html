Reflection at Spherical Surfaces

Understanding image formation and magnification by curved mirrors.

Introduction to Spherical Mirrors

A spherical mirror is a reflecting surface that is part of a hollow glass shell.

Depending on which side of the spherical surface is polished, spherical mirrors are classified into two types:

  • Concave Mirror: The reflecting surface is the inner side of the spherical surface (facing the center of curvature). It converges parallel incident rays.
  • Convex Mirror: The reflecting surface is the outer side of the spherical surface (facing away from the center of curvature). It diverges parallel incident rays.

Key Terminologies:

  • Pole (\(P\)): The geometric center of the reflecting surface of a spherical mirror.
  • Center of Curvature (\(C\)): The center of the sphere of which the mirror forms a part.
  • Radius of Curvature (\(R\)): The radius of the sphere of which the mirror forms a part. The distance between \(P\) and \(C\) is \(R\).
  • Principal Axis: The straight line passing through the pole (\(P\)) and the center of curvature (\(C\)).
  • Aperture: The diameter of the reflecting portion of the mirror, measured perpendicular to the principal axis.

Converging and Diverging Nature

The effect of a spherical mirror can be understood in terms of how it transforms a beam of parallel rays.

  • Concave Mirror: Converts a parallel beam into a converging beam that meets at a point (focus).
  • Convex Mirror: Converts a parallel beam into a diverging beam that appears to originate from a point behind the mirror.
A plane mirror preserves the angular spread of a beam.
A spherical mirror modifies it through its converging or diverging nature.
This change in angular spread leads to changes in size, shape and orientation of the image.
Study of this geometric transformation enables the design of optical instruments such as microscopes and telescopes which revolutionized science.
