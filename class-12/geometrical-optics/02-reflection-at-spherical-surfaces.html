Reflection at Spherical Surfaces

Understanding image formation and magnification by curved mirrors.

Introduction to Concave and Convex Mirrors

A spherical mirror is a reflecting surface that forms a part of a hollow sphere. Depending on which side of the spherical surface is polished, spherical mirrors are classified into two types:

  • Concave Mirror: The inner (depressed) surface of the spherical part is the reflecting surface. It generally converges parallel incident rays.
  • Convex Mirror: The outer (bulging) surface of the spherical part is the reflecting surface. It generally diverges parallel incident rays.

Key Terminologies:

  • Pole (\(P\)): The geometric center of the reflecting surface of a spherical mirror.
  • Center of Curvature (\(C\)): The center of the sphere of which the mirror forms a part.
  • Radius of Curvature (\(R\)): The radius of the sphere of which the mirror forms a part. The distance between \(P\) and \(C\) is \(R\).
  • Principal Axis: The straight line passing through the pole (\(P\)) and the center of curvature (\(C\)).
  • Aperture: The effective diameter of the light-reflecting area of the mirror.
In geometric optics, we almost exclusively deal with paraxial rays. These are rays that are close to the principal axis and make very small angles with it. This approximation ensures a sharp image is formed at a single point (focal point). Rays far from the axis suffer from spherical aberration.

Sign Convention and Principal Focus

To mathematically determine the exact position and nature of an image, we use a Cartesian sign convention system.

Cartesian Sign Convention

  1. The pole (\(P\)) of the mirror is taken as the origin (\(0,0\)).
  2. The principal axis is taken as the \(x\)-axis.
  3. All distances are measured from the pole of the mirror.
  4. Distances measured in the direction of incident light are taken as positive (\(+\)).
  5. Distances measured in the direction opposite to incident light are taken as negative (\(-\)).
  6. Heights measured upwards and perpendicular to the principal axis are taken as positive (\(+\)).
  7. Heights measured downwards and perpendicular to the principal axis are taken as negative (\(-\)).

Principal Focus and Focal Length

When a narrow beam of rays, parallel to the principal axis, falls on a spherical mirror, the reflected rays either actually converge to an point on the principal axis (in a concave mirror) or appear to diverge from a point on the principal axis (in a convex mirror). This point is called the Principal Focus (\(F\)).

The distance from the pole (\(P\)) to the principal focus (\(F\)) is the focal length (\(f\)). For paraxial rays, the focal length is exactly half the radius of curvature:

\[ f = \frac{R}{2} \]

Following the sign convention, the focal length of a concave mirror is negative (\(f < 0\)), and the focal length of a convex mirror is positive (\(f > 0\)).

Mirror Formula and Magnification

The relationship between the object distance (\(u\)), image distance (\(v\)), and focal length (\(f\)) for a spherical mirror is encapsulated in the mirror formula. This equation is paramount for solving optical image formation problems.

The Mirror Formula

\[ \frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f} \]

Where:

  • \(u\) = object distance from pole.
  • \(v\) = image distance from pole.
  • \(f\) = focal length of the mirror.

Always substitute values of \(u, v, f\) with their proper signs based on the sign convention.

Lateral (Transverse) Magnification (\(m\))

Magnification describes how large or small the image is compared to the object. It is defined as the ratio of the height of the image (\(h_i\)) to the height of the object (\(h_o\)).

\[ m = \frac{h_i}{h_o} \]

Using similar triangles in the geometric proof of the mirror formula, we derive the relation:

\[ m = -\frac{v}{u} = \frac{f}{f - u} = \frac{f - v}{f} \]

Interpreting Magnification (\(m\)):

  • If \(m\) is negative (\(-\)), the image is inverted. Inverted images formed by single mirrors are always real.
  • If \(m\) is positive (\(+\)), the image is erect. Erect images formed by single mirrors are always virtual.
  • If \(|m| > 1\), the image is magnified (enlarged).
  • If \(|m| < 1\), the image is diminished.

Velocity of Image in Spherical Mirrors

If an object moves along the principal axis of a spherical mirror with velocity \(v_o = \frac{du}{dt}\), the image will also move with velocity \(v_i = \frac{dv}{dt}\). We can find the relationship by differentiating the mirror formula with respect to time.

Starting with the mirror formula: \[ \frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f} \]

Assuming the mirror is stationary (\(f\) is constant, and pole is fixed), differentiating with respect to time \(t\): \[ -\frac{1}{v^2}\frac{dv}{dt} - \frac{1}{u^2}\frac{du}{dt} = 0 \] \[ \frac{dv}{dt} = -\left(\frac{v}{u}\right)^2 \frac{du}{dt} \]

Velocity Relation (Along Principal Axis)

\[ \vec{v}_i = -m^2 \vec{v}_o \]

Where:

  • \(\vec{v}_i\) = Velocity of image relative to mirror.
  • \(\vec{v}_o\) = Velocity of object relative to mirror.
  • \(m = -\frac{v}{u}\) = Transverse magnification.
Because the square of any real number (\(m^2\)) is always positive, the negative sign strictly means that the velocity of the image is always opposite to the velocity of the object relative to the mirror when moving along the principal axis. They always move in opposing directions.

Velocity Perpendicular to the Principal Axis

If the object is moving perpendicular to the principal axis, its \(u\) coordinate remains constant, which means \(v\) and \(m\) remain constant. Since \( h_i = m \cdot h_o \), differentiating with respect to time gives: \[ \frac{dh_i}{dt} = m \frac{dh_o}{dt} \] \[ \Rightarrow v_{i,\perp} = m \cdot v_{o,\perp} \] Here, the magnification acts as a simple scale factor for vertical velocity components.
