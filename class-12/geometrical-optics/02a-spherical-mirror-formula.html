Mirror Formula for Paraxial Rays.

Paraxial Rays

In spherical mirrors, not all rays behave identically after reflection. To obtain a well-defined image and a unique focus, we restrict our analysis to paraxial rays.

Paraxial rays are those rays which:

  • travel very close to the principal axis, and
  • make very small angles with the principal axis.

Under this approximation, we can assume:

  • \( \sin \theta \approx \theta \)
  • \( \tan \theta \approx \theta \)

This simplifies the geometry of reflection and allows us to derive simple relationships such as the mirror formula.

Rays that are far from the principal axis are called marginal rays. These rays do not converge to the same point as paraxial rays and lead to deviations known as spherical aberration.

The concept of focus for spherical mirrors is strictly defined using paraxial rays. Without this approximation, different rays would intersect at different points.

Sign Convention and Principal Focus

To mathematically determine the exact position and nature of an image, we use a Cartesian sign convention system.

Cartesian Sign Convention

  1. The pole (\(P\)) of the mirror is taken as the origin (\(0,0\)).
  2. The principal axis is taken as the \(x\)-axis.
  3. All distances are measured from the pole of the mirror.
  4. Distances measured in the direction of incident light are taken as positive (\(+\)).
  5. Distances measured in the direction opposite to incident light are taken as negative (\(-\)).
  6. Heights measured upwards and perpendicular to the principal axis are taken as positive (\(+\)).
  7. Heights measured downwards and perpendicular to the principal axis are taken as negative (\(-\)).

Principal Focus and Focal Length

When a narrow beam of rays, parallel to the principal axis, falls on a spherical mirror, the reflected rays either actually converge to an point on the principal axis (in a concave mirror) or appear to diverge from a point on the principal axis (in a convex mirror). This point is called the Principal Focus (\(F\)).

The distance from the pole (\(P\)) to the principal focus (\(F\)) is the focal length (\(f\)). For paraxial rays, the focal length is exactly half the radius of curvature:

\[ f = \frac{R}{2} \]

Following the sign convention, the focal length of a concave mirror is negative (\(f < 0\)), and the focal length of a convex mirror is positive (\(f > 0\)).
