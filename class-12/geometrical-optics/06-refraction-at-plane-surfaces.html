Refraction at Plane Surfaces

The bending of light when it crosses the boundary between two transparent media.

Real and Apparent Depth

When an object placed in a denser medium (like water) is viewed from a rarer medium (like air) almost normally, it appears to be raised from its actual depth. This is a direct consequence of refraction bending rays away from the normal as they exit the denser medium into the observer's eye.

Apparent Depth Relation

Let an object \(O\) be at a real depth \(d\) in a medium of refractive index \(n_2\). An observer is looking from a rarer medium of refractive index \(n_1\) (\(n_1 < n_2\)). The apparent depth \(d'\) at which the virtual image \(I\) is formed is given by:

\[ d' = d \left(\frac{n_1}{n_2}\right) \]

If the observer is in air (\(n_1 \approx 1\)), the formula simplifies to: \[ d' = \frac{d}{n} \] Where \(n\) is the refractive index of the denser medium.

Apparent Shift (\(x\))

The apparent shift or apparent displacement is the distance by which the object appears to be raised.

\[ x = \text{Real Depth} - \text{Apparent Depth} = d - d' \] \[ x = d \left(1 - \frac{n_1}{n_2}\right) \]

Or for an observer in air: \( x = d \left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right) \) .

The converse is also true: if an object in a rarer medium (like a bird in air) is viewed by an observer in a denser medium (like a fish in water), the object appears farther away than its real height. The apparent height becomes \(h' = n \cdot h\), meaning the object appears shifted upward.

Refraction through a Rectangular Glass Slab

When a ray of light passes through a rectangular glass slab bounded by parallel faces, it undergoes refraction twice: once when entering the slab and again when exiting it.

Assuming the medium on both sides of the slab is the same (e.g., air), the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray. Because the two refractions occur at parallel interfaces with opposite relative refractive indices, the total deviation is zero (\(\delta = 0\)). However, the emergent ray is laterally shifted relative to the incident path.

Lateral Shift (\(x\))

The perpendicular distance between the original path of the incident ray and the path of the emergent ray is called the lateral shift. Let \(t\) be the thickness of the glass slab, \(i\) the angle of incidence, and \(r\) the angle of refraction at the first interface. The lateral shift is derived from geometry:

\[ x = \frac{t \cdot \sin(i - r)}{\cos r} \]

For small angles of incidence (where \(\sin \theta \approx \theta\) and \(\cos \theta \approx 1\)), and using \( i \approx n \cdot r \):

\[ x \approx t \cdot i \left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right) \]

Where \(n\) is the refractive index of the slab relative to the surrounding medium.
