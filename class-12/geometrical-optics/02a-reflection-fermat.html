Fermat Principle

Fundamental Principle behind Laws of reflection and refraction.

Fermat’s Principle

This entire chapter is based on two fundamental phenomena: reflection and refraction.

The Laws of both phenomena can be derived from a fundamentally deeper principle known as Fermat’s Principle of Least Time.

Light travels between two points along the path that requires stationary (usually minimum) time.

Although not required for exams directly, this principle provides a deeper understanding of why the laws of reflection and refraction hold.

Its interpretation as optimal path selection, leads to a powerful geometric method in reflection problems known as the Unfolding Technique.

Instead of treating reflection as a rule, Fermat’s principle allows us to view it as a consequence of optimal path selection.
This connects geometrical optics to a deeper variational principle.

Unfolding Technique

The unfolding technique is used when the source and destination points are known, and the path involves reflection from a surface.

The idea is to replace reflection by an equivalent straight-line motion by "unfolding" the path across the reflecting surface.

Reflection at a surface can be replaced by extending the path into a mirror image of the space across that surface.

Problem 1:

A bird is on a vertical perch at \((0, h_1)\). Its nest is at \((d, h_2)\). Seeds are scattered on the ground between \((0,0)\) and \((d,0)\).

The bird flies from the perch, touches the ground once to collect seeds, and then reaches the nest.

What is the minimum distance path it should follow?

Solution

This problem is equivalent to finding the path of a light ray traveling from \((0, h_1)\) to \((d, h_2)\) after reflecting from the ground \(y = 0\).

This is because, as per Fermat's principle, light will take minimum time path.

As light travels in the same medium, its speed is constant. Hence, the path of minimum time is also the path of minimum distance.

Instead of dealing with reflection, we unfold the problem by reflecting the destination point across the ground. Thus, the image of the nest is at \((d, -h_2)\).

Now the reflected path becomes a straight line from \((0, h_1)\) to \((d, -h_2)\).

By the triangle inequality, the shortest path between two points is a straight line. Hence, this straight-line path gives the minimum distance.

The actual path of the bird is obtained by folding this straight line back at the ground, giving the point of reflection.

Unfolding converts a reflection problem into a straight-line geometry problem.
This is a direct application of Fermat’s principle.
