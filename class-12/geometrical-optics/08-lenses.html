Lenses

Image formation by transparent media bounded by spherical surfaces.

Introduction to Thin Lenses

A lens is a transparent optical medium bounded by two surfaces, at least one of which is spherical. Lenses are fundamentally categorized into two main types based on their shape and how they affect a parallel beam of light:

  • Convex Lens (Converging): Thicker in the middle and thinner at the edges. Examples include bi-convex, plano-convex, and concavo-convex lenses.
  • Concave Lens (Diverging): Thinner in the middle and thicker at the edges. Examples include bi-concave, plano-concave, and convexo-concave lenses.

Key Terminologies:

  • Optical Center (\(O\)): A point within or outside the lens through which a ray of light passes without suffering any deviation.
  • Principal Axis: The line passing through the centers of curvature of the two bounding surfaces.
  • Principal Focus (\(F\)): For a convex lens, it is the point where rays parallel to the principal axis converge. For a concave lens, it is the point from which they appear to diverge. Because a lens has two surfaces, it has two principal foci (\(F_1\) and \(F_2\)).
We will exclusively study Thin Lenses. A lens is considered "thin" if its maximum thickness is negligible compared to its radii of curvature, object distance, and image distance. For a thin lens, distances can be measured from the optical center \(O\) without significant error.

Lens Maker's Formula

The Lens Maker's Formula relates the focal length of a lens to the refractive index of its material and the radii of curvature of its two surfaces. It is derived by considering refraction at the two spherical surfaces successively.

Consider a thin lens of refractive index \(\mu_L\) placed in a surrounding medium of refractive index \(\mu_S\). Let \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) be the radii of curvature of the first and second surfaces, respectively.

The Lens Maker's Formula

\[ \frac{1}{f} = \left( \frac{\mu_L}{\mu_S} - 1 \right) \left( \frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2} \right) \]

Where:

  • \(f\) = focal length of the lens.
  • \(\mu_L\) = refractive index of the lens material.
  • \(\mu_S\) = refractive index of the surrounding medium.
  • \(\left(\frac{\mu_L}{\mu_S}\right)\) is often written as \(\mu\) (relative refractive index).
  • \(R_1\) = Radius of curvature of the surface upon which light falls first.
  • \(R_2\) = Radius of curvature of the second surface.

Proper Cartesian sign convention must be strictly followed for \(R_1, R_2,\) and \(f\).

Dependence on Surrounding Medium

The focal length of a lens is not a fixed property; it depends heavily on the medium in which it is placed. If a glass convex lens (\(\mu_L \approx 1.5\)) is placed in water (\(\mu_S \approx 1.33\)), the relative refractive index \(\left(\frac{1.5}{1.33}\right)\) is less than its refractive index in air \(\left(\frac{1.5}{1}\right)\).

Consequently, when placed in water, the term \(\left( \frac{\mu_L}{\mu_S} - 1 \right)\) decreases, meaning '1/f' decreases, and therefore the focal length \(f\) increases (usually by a factor of 4).

If the lens is placed in a medium with a refractive index greater than its own material (\(n_m > n_L\)), the term \(\left( \frac{n_L}{n_m} - 1 \right)\) becomes negative. This means the nature of the lens reverses: a convex lens begins to act as a diverging concave lens, and a concave lens acts as a converging convex lens.

Lens Formula and Lateral Magnification

Once the focal length \(f\) is known via the Lens Maker's formula, the position of the image is found using the Lens Formula, which connects \(u\), \(v\), and \(f\).

The Thin Lens Formula

\[ \frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f} \]

Where:

  • \(u\) = object distance from the optical center.
  • \(v\) = image distance from the optical center.
  • \(f\) = focal length. Positive for convex, negative for concave.

Notice the difference in sign compared to the mirror formula (which has a plus sign: \(1/v + 1/u = 1/f\)).

Lateral Magnification (\(m\))

The lateral (or transverse) magnification is the ratio of image height \(h_i\) to object height \(h_o\). From the geometry of similar triangles passing through the optical center:

\[ m = \frac{h_i}{h_o} = \frac{v}{u} \]

Also expressed in terms of focal length:

\[ m = \frac{f}{f + u} = \frac{f - v}{f} \]

Interpreting \(m\) for Lenses:

  • \(m\) is positive: The image is erect and virtual.
  • \(m\) is negative: The image is inverted and real.
  • \(|m| > 1\): Image is magnified (larger than object).
  • \(|m| \lt 1\): Image is diminished (smaller than object).
