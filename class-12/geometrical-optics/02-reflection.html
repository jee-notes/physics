Reflection of Light

The geometry of light interaction with reflective boundaries and variational principles.

Laws of Reflection

When a ray of light strikes a boundary surface separating two different media, a part of the light is turned back into the same medium. This phenomenon is called reflection.

Laws of Reflection

Reflection of light is governed by two fundamental laws:

  1. First Law: The incident ray, the reflected ray and the normal to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.
  2. Second Law: The angle of incidence (\(i\)) is always equal to the angle of reflection (\(r\)). \[ \angle i = \angle r \]

Here:

  • Rays: The ray is incident or reflected - depending upon its direction with respect to reflecting surface.
  • Incident Ray (i): Incident ray is moving towards the reflecting surface before reflection.
  • Reflected Ray (r): Reflected ray is moving away from the reflecting surface after reflection.
  • Point of incidence: Point of incedence is a point on the reflecting surface where the incident ray strikes the surface.
  • Normal: A line drawn perpendicular to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence and pointing outwards. Note that direction of normal is localized at the point of incidence. For a flat reflecting surface (planar) it has uniform direction.
  • Angle of Incidence (\(i\)): The angle between the incident ray and the normal.
  • Angle of Reflection (\(r\)): The angle between the reflected ray and the normal.
The laws of reflection are completely independent of the wavelength of the light wave and apply to both plane and curved reflecting surfaces.
These laws are independent remain valid beyond approximations such as paraxial optics.

Vector Form

The laws of reflection can be expressed in vector form, which is especially useful for three-dimensional problems. In this formulation, rays are modeled as straight-line directions and represented using unit vectors, since only the direction of propagation is relevant in the ray model.

Let \(\hat{i}\) be the unit vector along the incident ray, \(\hat{n}\) be the unit normal to the surface (directed outward from the incident medium), and \(\hat{r}\) be the unit vector along the reflected ray.

The first law of reflection (coplanarity) is expressed as:

\[ [\hat{i}\ \hat{r}\ \hat{n}] = 0 \]

Here, \([\hat{a}\ \hat{b}\ \hat{c}]\) denotes the scalar triple product (standard math tool to express coplanarity), which vanishes when the three vectors are coplanar.

The second law of reflection is expressed as:

\[ -\hat{i}\cdot\hat{n} = \hat{r}\cdot\hat{n} \]

Collision Approach

A powerful problem solving technique to interpret reflection is by collision approach:

Reflection reverses the component of the incident direction along the normal, while leaving the component parallel to the surface unchanged.

This viewpoint is extremely useful for solving problems involving reflection in arbitrary orientations.

Relation between \(\hat{r}\) and \(\hat{i}\)

Since the component of the ray parallel to the surface remains unchanged, the change in direction between \(\hat{i}\) and \(\hat{r}\) must occur entirely along the normal.

\[ \hat{r} - \hat{i} = k\,\hat{n} \]

To determine \(k\), we take dot product of both sides \(\hat{r} - \hat{i}\) and \(k\,\hat{n}\) with \(\hat{n}\):

\[ (\hat{r} - \hat{i})\cdot\hat{n} = k \] \[ k = \hat{r}\cdot\hat{n} - \hat{i}\cdot\hat{n} = -\hat{i}\cdot\hat{n} - \hat{i}\cdot\hat{n} = -2(\hat{i}\cdot\hat{n}) \]

Using \(\hat{r}\cdot\hat{n} = -\,\hat{i}\cdot\hat{n}\), and \(\hat{n}\cdot\hat{n} = 1\). Substituting back, we get:

\[ \hat{r} = \hat{i} - 2(\hat{i}\cdot\hat{n})\hat{n} \]

Angle of Deviation

The angle of deviation (\(\delta\)) is defined as the angle between the direction of the incident ray (extended forward) and the reflected ray.

To determine this angle, we extend the incident ray beyond the point of incidence as if reflection had not occurred, and then measure the angle between this direction and the reflected ray.

For reflection, the magnitude of deviation is given by:

\[ \delta = 180^\circ - 2i \]

The direction of deviation (clockwise or anticlockwise) depends on the geometry of the situation and must be determined case by case.

Angle of deviation measures how much the direction of a ray changes due to reflection.
It depends only on the angle of incidence and is independent of the shape of the reflecting surface.

Normal and Grazing Incidence

Two special angles of incidence are important as they are used as limiting cases of reflection (and refraction):

  • Normal Incidence: \(i = 0^\circ\)
    The ray strikes the surface along the normal and retraces its path after reflection. The angle of deviation is: \[ \delta = 180^\circ \]
  • Grazing Incidence: \(i \to 90^{\circ -}\)
    The ray strikes the surface nearly parallel to it. The reflected ray also remains nearly parallel to the surface. The angle of deviation is: \[ \delta \to 0^\circ \]
Normal incidence corresponds to maximum deviation and is called as RETRACE.
Grazing incidence corresponds to minimum deviation.
These cases represent the limiting behavior of reflection.

Specular vs. Diffuse Reflection

If reflection occurs at every surface, why do we not see clear images everywhere? The answer lies in the microscopic structure of the surface.

Reflection behavior is determined by the surface roughness relative to the wavelength of light (\(\lambda \approx 400 - 700 \text{ nm}\)).

  • Specular Reflection: Occurs on highly polished surfaces. The surface is smooth at the scale of wavelength, so all local normals are nearly the same (parallel). As a result, parallel incident rays remain parallel after reflection, preserving the geometric structure of the beam and allowing image formation.
  • Diffuse Reflection: Occurs on rough surfaces (like paper or walls). Although the law \(\angle i = \angle r\) holds at every local point, the local normals vary randomly. This causes reflected rays to scatter in different directions, destroying the geometric structure of the beam.
Reflection occurs at every surface.
A mirror preserves the structure of a beam, while a rough surface destroys it.
Image formation requires preservation of geometric structure, not just reflection.
