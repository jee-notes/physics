Reflection of Light

The geometry of light interaction with reflective boundaries and variational principles.

Laws of Reflection

When a light ray strikes a smooth boundary separating two different media, a part of the light is turned back into the same medium. This phenomenon is called reflection.

Laws of Reflection

Reflection of light is governed by two fundamental laws:

  1. First Law: The incident ray, the reflected ray and the normal to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.
  2. Second Law: The angle of incidence (\(i\)) is always equal to the angle of reflection (\(r\)). \[ \angle i = \angle r \]

Here:

  • Rays: The ray is incident or reflected - depending upon its direction with respect to reflecting surface.
  • Incident Ray (i): Incident ray is moving towards the reflecting surface before reflection.
  • Reflected Ray (r): Reflected ray is moving away from the reflecting surface after reflection.
  • Point of incidence: Point of incedence is a point on the reflecting surface where the incident ray strikes the surface.
  • Normal: A line drawn perpendicular to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence and pointing outwards. Note that direction of normal is localized at the point of incidence. For a flat reflecting surface (planar) it has uniform direction.
  • Angle of Incidence (\(i\)): The angle between the incident ray and the normal.
  • Angle of Reflection (\(r\)): The angle between the reflected ray and the normal.
The laws of reflection are completely independent of the wavelength of the light wave and apply to both plane and curved reflecting surfaces.
These laws are independent remain valid beyond approximations such as paraxial optics.

Vector Form

The laws of reflection are described in vector form as well - useful for 3D mathematical operations. In vector form, rays are modeled as straight-line directions and are represented using unit vectors, as only the direction of propagation is relevant, not the magnitude. (In a more complete model, an EM wave has amplitude, energy, phase etc. but ray model only deals with direction of energy propogation.)

Let \(\hat{i}\) be the unit vector along the incident ray and \(\hat{n}\) be the unit vector normal to the surface (directed outwards from the incident medium) and \(\hat{r}\) be the unit vector along the reflected ray. The first law of reflection is given by:

\[ [\hat{i} \, \hat{r} \,\hat{n}] = 0 \]

\( [\hat{a} \, \hat{b} \,\hat{c}] \) is the scalar triple product and is used as a mathematical tool to express coplanarity relation.

The second law can be expressed as:

\[ - \hat{i} \cdot \hat{n} = \hat{r} \cdot \hat{n} \]

Combining these relations, the reflected direction can be written as:

\[ \hat{r} = \hat{i} - 2(\hat{i} \cdot \hat{n})\hat{n} \]

This shows that reflection reverses the component of the incident direction along the normal while leaving the parallel component unchanged.
This insight is used to solve problems by just using geometric tools.

Specular vs. Diffuse Reflection

Reflection behavior is determined by the surface "roughness" relative to the wavelength of light (\(\lambda \approx 400-700 \text{ nm}\)).

  • Specular Reflection: Occurs on highly polished surfaces. Parallel incident rays remain parallel after reflection, preserving the wavefront and allowing image formation.
  • Diffuse Reflection: Occurs on rough surfaces (like paper or walls). While the law \(\angle i = \angle r\) holds at every local point, the random orientation of local normals scatters rays in all directions.
Specular reflection creates images (like in a mirror), while diffuse reflection allows us to see non-luminous objects from various angles.

Point Object vs. Extended Object

An extended object can be viewed as a system of point objects, each emitting its own set of rays.

Image formation for an extended object is determined by analyzing the image formed by each point and preserving the geometric relationships between them.

Thus, the image of an extended object is a geometrically transformed system of image points corresponding to the system of object points.

Fermat’s Principle: The Variational Basis

The laws of reflection are a direct consequence of Fermat’s Principle of Least Time.

"Light travels between two points along the path that requires the stationary (usually minimum) time."

Mathematical Derivation

To travel from point \(A\) to point \(B\) via reflection at point \(P\) on a mirror, the total path length \(L\) is: \[ L = \sqrt{x^2 + h_1^2} + \sqrt{(d-x)^2 + h_2^2} \] Since the speed of light is constant in the medium, minimizing time is equivalent to minimizing the path length \(L\). Taking the derivative with respect to the point of incidence \(x\) and setting it to zero: \[ \frac{dL}{dx} = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + h_1^2}} - \frac{d-x}{\sqrt{(d-x)^2 + h_2^2}} = 0 \] This simplifies to: \[ \sin i = \sin r \implies i = r \]
