Prisms

Refraction, deviation and dispersion of light by angular transparent media.

Refraction and Deviation through a Prism

A prism is a wedged-shaped portion of a transparent refracting medium bounded by two plane faces inclined at an angle. The angle between the two refracting faces is called the Angle of Prism (\(A\)).

In general, prism is a classic example of sequential refraction about two different plane surfaces, such that the planes are at an angle called "prism angle" or "apex angle".

Prism theory assumes that the incident ray is oriented such that both refractions occur in the same plane (plane of incidence).
This coplanarity is essential for treating the prism as a 2D sequential refraction system.

When a monochromatic ray of light passes through a prism, it suffers refraction at the two inclined faces. Unlike a rectangular glass slab where the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray, a prism bends the emergent ray at a considerable angle relative to the incident direction. This total angle of bending is called the Angle of Deviation (\(\delta\)).

Prism Parameters

Prism parameters are basically 3 points and 6 angles in total. We need to accept this to understand prism. The 6 angles are:

  1. Angle of incidence : This is angle \(i\) made by the incident ray with the normal of the first surface. \(0^\circ \lt i \lt 90^\circ\).
  2. Angle of emergence : This is angle \(e\) made by the emerging ray with the normal of the second surface. \(0^\circ \lt e \lt 90^\circ\).
  3. Prism Angle : This is angle \(A\) between the two surfaces. \(0^\circ \lt A \lt 180^\circ\).
  4. Angle of deviation : This is the angle \(\delta\) between the undeviated incident ray and emerging ray.
  5. First inside angle : This is angle \(r_1\) made by the inside ray after refraction of incident ray with the normal of the first surface.
  6. Second inside angle : This is angle \(r_2\) made by the inside ray before refraction of emerging ray with the normal of the second surface.

The 3 points are:

  1. Apex : The apex is the line of intersection of the two refracting surfaces. In prism problems, the incident ray is chosen such that the incident ray, emergent ray, and the normals at both surfaces all lie in the same plane (plane of incidence). This ensures that both refractions occur in a single plane, allowing the problem to be treated as a 2D sequential refraction system. In this cross-sectional plane, the apex is treated as a point.
  2. Point of incidence : The point on the first surface at which the incident ray strikes it. We are going to draw normal to the surface at that point and we are only considering a single plane containing that normal and incident ray. All formulae and derivations are only valid in that plane.
  3. Point of emergence : The point on the second surface from which the emerging ray comes out. We are going to draw normal to the surface at that point and we are only considering a single plane containing that normal and emerging ray. This should necessarily be the same plane of incident ray. All formulae and derivations are only valid in that plane.

Relations between Prism Parameters

Refractive index of prism is \(\mu_{PS}\) and both surfaces are in same surrounding medium. If a ray is incident at an angle \(i\), undergoes refraction at the first face at an angle \(r_1\), then strikes the second face at an angle \(r_2\), and emerges at an angle \(e\):

  1. By geometry: The sum of internal angles of refraction equals the angle of the prism: \[ \boxed{A = r_1 + r_2} \]
  2. By Snell's Law at surface 1: \[ \boxed{\sin i = \mu_{PS} \sin r_1} \]
  3. By Snell's Law at surface 2: \[ \boxed{\mu_{PS} \sin r_2 = \sin e} \]
  4. The net angle of deviation (\(\delta\)) is given by: \[ \delta = \delta_1 + \delta_2 \] where \(\delta_1\) and \(\delta_2\) are individual deviation angles at both surfaces.
  5. Angle of deviation (\(\delta_1\)) at surface 1 is given by: \[ \delta_1 = i - r_1 \text{ clockwise}\]
  6. Angle of deviation (\(\delta_2\)) at surface 2 is given by: \[ \delta_2 = e - r_2 \text{ clockwise}\]
  7. The net angle of deviation (\(\delta\)) is given by: \[ \boxed{\delta = (i + e) - (r_1 + r_2) = (i + e) - A} \]

We have 7 independent variables: \(\mu_{PS}, i, e, \delta, A, r_1, r_2\)

and 4 independent relations. Hence, 3 additional independent conditions are required to completely solve a prism system.

A prism always bends the ray of light away from the apex.

Minimum Deviation

As the angle of incidence (\(i\)) increases from \(i_{min}\) to \(90^\circ\), the angle of deviation (\(\delta\)) first decreases, reaches a minimum value called the Angle of Minimum Deviation (\(\delta_m\)), and then begins to increase.

At \(i_{min}\), \(e = 90^\circ\) and \(r_2 = c\). For \(i\) from \(0^\circ\) to \(i_{min}\), TIR occurs at surface 2 and so it is considered out of domain.

If \(i\) is further increased, then \(r_1\) increases, causing decrease in \(r_2\) (as \(r_1 + r_2 = A\)). As a result, \(e\) always decreases.

In general, we can say that, when \(i\) increases, \(r_1\) increases, \(r_2\) decreases, \(e\) decreases.

Conditions at Minimum Deviation

The prism is in the position of minimum deviation if and only if the ray of light passes symmetrically through it. This implies:

  • The angle of incidence equals the angle of emergence: \(i = e\).
  • The internal angles of refraction are equal: \(r_1 = r_2 = A/2\).
  • The refracted ray inside the prism travels parallel to the base of the prism (assuming the prism is an isosceles triangle). Point of incidence and Point of emergence are equidistant from the apex.

Refractive Index from Minimum Deviation

Using the symmetry conditions \(2r = A \Rightarrow r = \frac{A}{2}\) and \(2i = A + \delta_m \Rightarrow i = \frac{A+\delta_m}{2}\), substituting into Snell's Law (\(\mu = \frac{\sin i}{\sin r}\)) yields the crucial prism formula:

\[ \mu = \frac{\sin\left(\frac{A + \delta_m}{2}\right)}{\sin\left(\frac{A}{2}\right)} \]

Where \(\mu\) is the refractive index of the prism material relative to the surrounding medium.

The condition for minimum angle deviation only adds 1 more independent relation. We still would need 2 more independent information to fully define the prism system.

Thin Prism Approximation

For a thin prism (where angle \(A\) is very small, typically \(\lt 10^\circ\)), all angles involved (\(i, r_1, r_2, e, \delta\)) are also small. This allows us to apply the small-angle approximation and simplify the prism relations.

The purpose of this approximation is to obtain a simple expression for deviation that is independent of the angle of incidence, depending only on the prism angle \(A\) and refractive index \(\mu\).

For small angles, we use: \[ \sin \theta \approx \theta \]

Applying this to Snell’s law:

\[ \sin i = \mu \sin r_1 \;\Rightarrow\; i = \mu r_1 \] \[ \sin e = \mu \sin r_2 \;\Rightarrow\; e = \mu r_2 \] \[ i + e = \mu (r_1 + r_2) = \mu A \]

Using the deviation relation:

\[ \delta = (i + e) - A = \mu A - A \] \[ \boxed{\delta = A(\mu - 1)} \]
For a thin prism, deviation is independent of the angle of incidence and depends only on the prism angle and refractive index.

Conditions for Thin Prism Approximation

  • The prism angle \(A\) is small (typically \(A \lt 10^\circ\)).
  • Another approximation is that \(\to\) The ray passes close to the condition of minimum deviation (i.e., near symmetric path).
  • Under these conditions, internal angles \(r_1, r_2\) are small, as \(r_1 = r_2 = A/2\) in case of minimum deviation.
  • Under these conditions, external ray angles \(i, e\) are also small, as \(r_1, r_2\) are small.
  • Thus, all angles \((i, r_1, r_2, e, \delta)\) are small, allowing the approximation: \[ \sin \theta \approx \theta \]
