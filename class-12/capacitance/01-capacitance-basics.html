Capacitance and Capacitor Geometries

How much charge a conductor system can hold per volt of potential difference.

Definition of Capacitance

The capacitance \( C \) of a system of conductors is defined as the charge \( Q \) stored on the positive conductor per unit potential difference \( V \) between the conductors:

\[ C = \frac{Q}{V} \]

The SI unit of capacitance is the Farad (F): \( 1 \text{ F} = 1 \text{ C/V} \). One Farad is an enormous capacitance — practical capacitors range from picofarads (\( 10^{-12} \) F) to millifarads (\( 10^{-3} \) F).

Capacitance is a purely geometric property — it depends only on the shape, size, and relative position of the conductors, not on the charge placed on them or the potential to which they are raised. Doubling \( Q \) doubles \( V \) in exact proportion, keeping \( C = Q/V \) constant.

How a Capacitor Works

A capacitor consists of two conductors (plates) separated by an insulator. When a charge \( +Q \) is placed on one plate, it induces \( -Q \) on the facing surface of the other plate (which is earthed or connected to a return path). The electric field exists only in the gap between the plates.

The potential difference between the plates is determined by the field in the gap. Since \( E \propto Q \) and \( V = Ed \), we get \( V \propto Q \) — confirming that \( C = Q/V \) is indeed a constant determined only by geometry.

Self-Capacitance of an Isolated Sphere

For an isolated conducting sphere of radius \( R \), the potential when charged to \( Q \) is:

\[ V = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \]

Therefore the self-capacitance is:

\[ \boxed{C_{\text{sphere}} = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \]

The self-capacitance only depends on the radius. Earth's self-capacitance (\( R \approx 6.4 \times 10^6 \) m) is about 711 µF — still far less than 1 F.
