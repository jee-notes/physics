Energy of Spherical Capacitor

Two types of Potential energy for System of Charges.

Self-Energy vs. Interaction Energy

For any system of charged bodies, the total electrostatic potential energy does not come from a single source. It can be understood as arising from two conceptually distinct processes:

  • Self-Energy: The work done in assembling a single continuous charge distribution from infinity. This is the energy required to build the charge configuration against its own electrostatic repulsion.
  • Interaction Energy: The work done in bringing two or more already-charged bodies from infinity to their final separation.

In Electrostatics chapter, when we studied two point charges, the potential energy \( U = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{q_1 q_2}{r} \) was purely interaction energy. Each charge was assumed to already exist. We only calculated the energy due to their mutual interaction.

But in a continuous conductor — like a spherical shell — the entire charge distribution must first be created. The energy required to build that distribution against electrostatic repulsion is called self-energy.

Self-energy is not the energy of one charge due to another charge. It is the energy required to assemble a charge distribution against its own repulsion.

In this section, we focus strictly on calculating the self-energy of a single isolated conductor.

Self-Energy of a Charged Conducting Spherical Shell

Consider a thin conducting spherical shell of radius \( R \), carrying total charge \( Q \). We want to determine the electrostatic self-energy of this configuration — that is, the work required to assemble this charge distribution from infinity.

Method 1: Using Potential During Assembly

When the sphere has charge \( q \), its potential is:

\[ V = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \]

To bring an additional small charge \( dq \) from infinity and deposit it on the sphere, the required work is:

\[ dU = V \, dq = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \, dq \]

Integrating from \( q = 0 \) to \( q = Q \):

\[ U = \int_0^Q \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \, dq = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \cdot \frac{Q^2}{2} \] \[ \boxed{ U_{\text{self}} = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 R} } \]

This is the electrostatic self-energy of a charged conducting spherical shell.

Relation to Capacitance Formula

The self-capacitance of an isolated sphere is:

\[ C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 R \]

Using the general capacitor energy expression:

\[ U = \frac{Q^2}{2C} \]

Substituting:

\[ U = \frac{Q^2}{2(4\pi\varepsilon_0 R)} = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \]

Thus, the self-energy derived from assembly matches exactly with the capacitor energy formula.

Method 2: Using Energy Density of the Electric Field

Energy in electrostatics can also be computed using field energy density:

\[ u = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E^2 \]

For a charged conducting spherical shell:

  • \( E = 0 \) for \( r \lt R \)
  • \( E(r) = \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2} \) for \( r \ge R \)

The energy is stored only in the region \( r \ge R \).

Consider a thin spherical shell of radius \( r \) and thickness \( dr \). Its volume element is:

\[ dV = 4\pi r^2 dr \]

Energy in this element:

\[ dU = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E^2 \, dV \] Substitute \( E(r) \): \[ dU = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 \left( \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2} \right)^2 4\pi r^2 dr \] Simplifying: \[ dU = \frac{Q^2}{32\pi^2\varepsilon_0 r^4} \cdot 4\pi r^2 dr = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{dr}{r^2} \] Now integrate from \( R \) to \( \infty \): \[ U = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0} \int_R^\infty \frac{dr}{r^2} \] \[ = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0} \left[ -\frac{1}{r} \right]_R^\infty = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \] \[ \boxed{ U = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 R} } \]
The self-energy of a charged spherical shell can be derived in two completely different ways:
  • By calculating the work required to assemble the charge distribution.
  • By integrating the energy density of the electric field in space.
Both methods give the same result. This confirms that electrostatic energy resides in the electric field configuration outside the conductor — not inside the metal and not in the charges themselves.

Self-Energy of a Uniformly Charged Non-Conducting Sphere

Consider a solid non-conducting sphere of radius \( R \) carrying total charge \( Q \), uniformly distributed throughout its volume.

Since the charge is distributed in the volume (not only on the surface), the electric field exists both inside and outside the sphere.

Method 1: Assembly of Charge Distribution

Let the sphere be built gradually by bringing thin spherical shells of charge from infinity and depositing them concentrically. It is critical that the charge dq is deposited concentrically such that the density is always uniform.

At an intermediate stage, suppose a sphere of radius \( r \) has already been assembled, containing charge:

\[ q(r) = Q \frac{r^3}{R^3} \]

The charge density is uniform:

\[ \rho = \frac{Q}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3} \]

A thin spherical layer of thickness \( dr \) contains charge:

\[ dq = \rho \cdot 4\pi r^2 dr = \frac{3Q}{R^3} r^2 dr \]

The potential at radius \( r \) due to the already assembled inner charge \( q(r) \) is:

\[ V(r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q(r)}{r} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{Q r^2}{R^3} \]

Work required to bring \( dq \) from infinity:

\[ dU = V(r)\, dq \] Substitute: \[ dU = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{Q r^2}{R^3} \cdot \frac{3Q}{R^3} r^2 dr \] \[ = \frac{3Q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R^6} r^4 dr \] Now integrate from \( r = 0 \) to \( R \): \[ U = \frac{3Q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R^6} \int_0^R r^4 dr \] \[ = \frac{3Q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R^6} \cdot \frac{R^5}{5} \] \[ \boxed{ U = \frac{3Q^2}{20\pi\varepsilon_0 R} } \]

Method 2: Using Energy Density of Electric Field

Energy density:

\[ u = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E^2 \]

Electric field:

  • Inside (\( r \lt R \)): \[ E_{\text{inside}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{Q r}{R^3} \]
  • Outside (\( r \ge R \)): \[ E_{\text{outside}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r^2} \]

Total energy:

\[ U = U_{\text{inside}} + U_{\text{outside}} \]

The outside contribution equals the self-energy of a conducting shell:

\[ U_{\text{outside}} = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \]

The inside contribution is:

\[ U_{\text{inside}} = \int_0^R \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E_{\text{inside}}^2 4\pi r^2 dr = \frac{Q^2}{40\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \] Adding: \[ U = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 R} + \frac{Q^2}{40\pi\varepsilon_0 R} = \frac{3Q^2}{20\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \] \[ \boxed{ U = \frac{3Q^2}{20\pi\varepsilon_0 R} } \]
The self-energy of a uniformly charged solid sphere is greater than that of a conducting shell of the same radius: \[ U_{\text{solid}} = \frac{3Q^2}{20\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \quad \text{vs} \quad U_{\text{shell}} = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \] This happens because charge is distributed throughout the volume, creating an additional electric field inside the sphere. More field volume → more stored energy.

Solved Example: Expansion of a Charged Spherical Shell

A conducting spherical shell of radius \( R_1 \) carries a uniform charge \( q \). It is slowly expanded (without loss of charge) to a new radius \( R_2 \). Find the work performed by the electric forces during this process.

Step 1: Initial and Final Electrostatic Energy

The self-energy of a charged conducting spherical shell of radius \( R \) is:

\[ U = \frac{q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \]

Therefore,

\[ U_1 = \frac{q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 R_1} \] \[ U_2 = \frac{q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 R_2} \]

Step 2: Work Done by Electric Forces

Work done by electric forces equals the decrease in electrostatic potential energy:

\[ W_{\text{electric}} = U_1 - U_2 \] \[ = \frac{q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0} \left( \frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2} \right) \]

Step 3: Physical Interpretation

  • If \( R_2 > R_1 \), then \( U_2 < U_1 \).
  • The electrostatic energy decreases as the shell expands.
  • Therefore, the electric forces perform positive work during expansion.

This is expected because like charges repel. The shell naturally tends to expand under electrostatic repulsion, and the system releases stored energy as it expands.

Electrostatic energy of a charged conductor decreases as its radius increases: \[ U \propto \frac{1}{R} \] Larger radius → weaker electric field near the surface → lower energy density → lower total stored energy. The work done by electric forces during expansion equals the reduction in self-energy.

Solved Example: Total Energy of Two Concentric Charged Spherical Shells

Two thin concentric conducting spherical shells have radii \( R_1 \) and \( R_2 \) (\( R_2 > R_1 \)) and carry charges \( q_1 \) and \( q_2 \), respectively. Find:

  • The self-energy of each shell
  • The interaction energy
  • The total electrostatic energy of the system

Method 1: Self-Energy + Interaction Energy

1. Self-Energy of Inner Shell

\[ W_1 = \frac{q_1^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 R_1} \]

2. Self-Energy of Outer Shell

\[ W_2 = \frac{q_2^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 R_2} \]

3. Interaction Energy

The inner shell produces a potential at radius \( R_2 \):

\[ V_{1}(R_2) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1}{R_2} \]

Interaction energy equals charge of outer shell times potential due to inner shell:

\[ W_{12} = q_2 V_{1}(R_2) = \frac{q_1 q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R_2} \]

4. Total Energy

\[ W_{\text{total}} = W_1 + W_2 + W_{12} \] \[ \boxed{ W = \frac{q_1^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 R_1} + \frac{q_2^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 R_2} + \frac{q_1 q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R_2} } \]

Method 2: Capacitor Decomposition

The system can be interpreted as two capacitors:

  • Capacitor 1: Between the two shells Capacitance: \[ C_1 = \frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R_1 R_2}{R_2 - R_1} \]
  • Capacitor 2: Outer shell with infinity Capacitance: \[ C_2 = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 R_2 \]

Now determine charges on each capacitor.

  • Charge on inner capacitor (between shells) = \( q_1 \) (on inner sphere outer surface and outer sphere inner surface)
  • Charge on outer capacitor = total charge seen outside = \( q_1 + q_2 \)

Energy stored:

\[ U_1 = \frac{q_1^2}{2C_1} \] \[ U_2 = \frac{(q_1 + q_2)^2}{2C_2} \] Substitute capacitances: \[ U_1 = \frac{q_1^2 (R_2 - R_1)} {8\pi\varepsilon_0 R_1 R_2} \] \[ U_2 = \frac{(q_1 + q_2)^2} {8\pi\varepsilon_0 R_2} \] Now add: \[ W = U_1 + U_2 \] After simplification, this reduces exactly to: \[ \boxed{ W = \frac{q_1^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 R_1} + \frac{q_2^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 R_2} + \frac{q_1 q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R_2} } \]
Both methods give the same result. Method 1 separates energy into:
  • Self-energy of each conductor
  • Mutual interaction energy
Method 2 treats the system as a combination of capacitors. This demonstrates that: Electrostatic energy can be interpreted either as field energy or as capacitor energy — both viewpoints are completely equivalent.

Conceptual Clarifications for Two Concentric Shells

This problem is subtle because several different physical ideas are interacting simultaneously: charge redistribution on conductors, self-energy, interaction energy, and equivalent capacitor interpretation. Let us carefully clarify the three main conceptual points.

1. Charge Redistribution on Conductors

When two conducting shells carry charges \( q_1 \) and \( q_2 \), the charges do not remain arbitrarily spread. They rearrange themselves to maintain:

  • Zero electric field inside the metal
  • Electrostatic equilibrium

For the outer shell:

  • Charge on its inner surface = \( -q_1 \)
  • Charge on its outer surface = \( q_1 + q_2 \)

Why? Because the inner shell produces field in the cavity region. To cancel field inside the metal of the outer shell, an induced charge \( -q_1 \) must appear on its inner surface.

Since total charge of outer shell is \( q_2 \), the outer surface must then carry:

\[ q_{\text{outer surface}} = q_2 - (-q_1) = q_1 + q_2 \]

This redistribution is automatic due to conductor physics.

2. Why Interaction Energy Uses Only Sphere 1 as a Point Charge

Interaction energy is:

\[ W_{12} = q_2 V_1(R_2) \]

Here is the key:

  • The inner sphere produces the same field as a point charge at the center (by spherical symmetry).
  • The outer shell does not produce field inside its own metal or cavity.

Therefore, at radius \( R_2 \), the potential due to sphere 1 is:

\[ V_1(R_2) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1}{R_2} \]

We multiply this by total charge of shell 2 (which is \( q_2 \)).

We do NOT treat shell 2 as a point charge for interaction energy, because interaction energy is computed as:

\[ \text{charge} \times \text{potential due to the other} \]

The symmetry argument applies only to the field created by sphere 1.

3. Why Self-Energy of Outer Shell is Based on Total Charge \( q_2 \)

Even though outer shell has surface charges:

  • Inner surface: \( -q_1 \)
  • Outer surface: \( q_1 + q_2 \)

Its total charge remains:

\[ q_2 \]

Self-energy depends only on the total charge residing on that conductor, not on how it splits between surfaces.

For a thin conducting shell of radius \( R_2 \), the self-energy formula is:

\[ W_2 = \frac{q_2^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 R_2} \]

This works because:

  • All charge resides effectively at radius \( R_2 \)
  • The field outside depends only on total charge \( q_2 \)
  • The field inside the metal is zero

Self-energy is computed as work required to assemble total charge \( q_2 \) on that shell. Induced charge redistribution does not change the total assembly energy of that conductor.

This problem is difficult because three different energy concepts are overlapping:
  • Self-energy of each conductor (depends on its total charge)
  • Interaction energy between conductors (depends on potential due to the other)
  • Charge redistribution required to maintain electrostatic equilibrium
Once these three roles are separated mentally, the problem becomes clean and structured.
