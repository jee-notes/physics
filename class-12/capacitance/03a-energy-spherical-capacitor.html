Energy of Spherical Capacitor

Two types of Potential energy for System of Charges.

Self-Energy vs. Interaction Energy

For any system of charged conductors, the total electrostatic potential energy consists of two distinct components:

  • Self-Energy: The work done against electrostatic repulsion to assemble a single continuous charge distribution from infinity (e.g., charging a single sphere).
  • Interaction Energy: The work done to bring two or more already-charged bodies into proximity from infinity (e.g., the potential energy between two point charges or two separate spheres).

In this section, we focus strictly on calculating the Self-Energy of a single conductor.

Self-Energy of a Charged Conducting Spherical Shell

Consider a thin conducting spherical shell of radius \( R \), carrying total charge \( Q \). We want to determine the electrostatic self-energy of this configuration — that is, the work required to assemble this charge distribution from infinity.

Method 1: Using Potential During Assembly

When the sphere has charge \( q \), its potential is:

\[ V = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \]

To bring an additional small charge \( dq \) from infinity and deposit it on the sphere, the required work is:

\[ dU = V \, dq = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \, dq \]

Integrating from \( q = 0 \) to \( q = Q \):

\[ U = \int_0^Q \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \, dq = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \cdot \frac{Q^2}{2} \] \[ \boxed{ U_{\text{self}} = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 R} } \]

This is the electrostatic self-energy of a charged conducting spherical shell.

Relation to Capacitance Formula

The self-capacitance of an isolated sphere is:

\[ C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 R \]

Using the general capacitor energy expression:

\[ U = \frac{Q^2}{2C} \]

Substituting:

\[ U = \frac{Q^2}{2(4\pi\varepsilon_0 R)} = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \]

Thus, the self-energy derived from assembly matches exactly with the capacitor energy formula.

Method 2: Using Energy Density of the Electric Field

Energy in electrostatics can also be computed using field energy density:

\[ u = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E^2 \]

For a charged conducting spherical shell:

  • \( E = 0 \) for \( r \lt R \)
  • \( E(r) = \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2} \) for \( r \ge R \)

The energy is stored only in the region \( r \ge R \).

Consider a thin spherical shell of radius \( r \) and thickness \( dr \). Its volume element is:

\[ dV = 4\pi r^2 dr \]

Energy in this element:

\[ dU = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E^2 \, dV \] Substitute \( E(r) \): \[ dU = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 \left( \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2} \right)^2 4\pi r^2 dr \] Simplifying: \[ dU = \frac{Q^2}{32\pi^2\varepsilon_0 r^4} \cdot 4\pi r^2 dr = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{dr}{r^2} \] Now integrate from \( R \) to \( \infty \): \[ U = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0} \int_R^\infty \frac{dr}{r^2} \] \[ = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0} \left[ -\frac{1}{r} \right]_R^\infty = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \] \[ \boxed{ U = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 R} } \]
The self-energy of a charged spherical shell can be derived in two completely different ways:
  • By calculating the work required to assemble the charge distribution.
  • By integrating the energy density of the electric field in space.
Both methods give the same result. This confirms that electrostatic energy resides in the electric field configuration outside the conductor — not inside the metal and not in the charges themselves.
